🧠 Learning How to Learn — Build the Skill Behind All Skills LIFESTYLE • Foundation ▶
THE FORGETTING CURVE
You Forget 90% If You Don't Review
Ebbinghaus (1885) showed that without review, humans forget ~40% of new material within 20 minutes, ~70% within a day, ~90% within a week. The cure isn't reading more — it's spaced repetition: review material at increasing intervals (1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month).
ACTIVE vs PASSIVE LEARNING
Watching ≠ Knowing
Passive: watching a video tutorial, reading, highlighting. Feels like learning, but retention is low (~5-10%).
Active: writing code from scratch, teaching, explaining concepts aloud, doing practice questions, building projects. Retention climbs to 70-90%.
For every hour of video, spend two hours doing something with the material. The discomfort of struggle IS the learning.
FEYNMAN TECHNIQUE
If You Can't Explain It Simply, You Don't Know It Yet
1. Pick a concept you're studying.
2. Explain it as if teaching a 12-year-old — no jargon allowed.
3. Where you stumble or grab for words → that's your gap.
4. Go back and study only that gap.
5. Repeat until the explanation is smooth.
This technique cuts through the illusion of familiarity ("I've seen this 10 times" ≠ "I understand it").
DELIBERATE PRACTICE
Push the Edge, Get Feedback, Adjust
Random repetition doesn't produce mastery. Deliberate practice does:
• Work at the edge of your ability — if it's easy, it's review, not growth
• Get immediate, accurate feedback (run the code; take the quiz)
• Focus on weaknesses, not strengths
• Rest and consolidate — sleep is when the brain cements learning
COMPOUNDING KNOWLEDGE
Small Daily Gains Add Up Exponentially
1% better every day for a year = 37× improvement (1.01^365 ≈ 37.8). The hard part is that the gains are invisible at first. The graph looks flat for months, then curves sharply upward. Most people quit in the flat zone. Don't compare your chapter 3 to someone else's chapter 20. Stack small wins daily, stay consistent, trust the process.
⏱️ Time Management — Making Your Hours Count LIFESTYLE • Productivity ▶
THE CORE PROBLEM
Tech Work Is Constantly Interrupted
Deep work — the focused, uninterrupted concentration that produces real output — takes approximately 23 minutes to recover from each interruption. A day of constant Slack pings and meetings produces the feeling of work with very little actual output. Protecting your focus is a technical skill, not a personality preference.
THE POMODORO TECHNIQUE
Work in Focused Sprints
Francesco Cirillo's technique: work in 25-minute focused blocks (a "pomodoro"), then take a 5-minute break. Every 4 pomodoros, take a 15-30 minute break. Simple and effective for fighting procrastination and tracking what you actually accomplished.

🏯 The Japanese Mastery Loop — Kata, Poka-Yoke, Hansei, Kaizen, Shokunin LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

Fast mastery is a correction system, not an absence of failure

Five practices from Japanese manufacturing and craft traditions compose into a learning loop that has nothing to do with talent and everything to do with how quickly a mistake becomes information. Soichiro Honda is the standing example: he left school young, learned mechanical work through apprenticeship, and went back to study metallurgy part-time only after his piston rings failed Toyota's quality tests. Honda has been the world's largest motorcycle manufacturer since 1959. The failure was not the detour — the targeted study that followed it was the method.

Say the obvious thing plainly: this is a transferable framework that happens to have Japanese names, not a claim that Japanese people learn faster for some innate reason. Every one of these practices was invented by someone solving a specific problem, which is exactly why they transfer.

Reference

The five practices and what each one does

PracticeIts job in the loopWhat it looks like when you study
Kata 型
the form		Gives you a model to copy before you improviseWork through solved examples before attempting blank problems. Type the tutorial's commands exactly once before changing anything
Poka-yoke ポカヨケ
mistake-proofing		Makes the error harder to commit, or impossible to missRedesign the setup so a mistake announces itself — a linter, a checklist, a lab you can reset
Hansei 反省
reflection		Converts what went wrong into information you can act onFifteen minutes at the end of a session, written down, on what broke and why
Kaizen 改善
continuous improvement		Changes one variable at a time so you can tell what workedAlter one thing about how you study per week — not five
Shokunin 職人
the craftsman's standard		Gates advancement on demonstrated mastery, over a horizon of yearsDon't move on because you finished the chapter. Move on because you can rebuild it from memory

Read as a cycle: copy a form → make errors visible → reflect on them → change one thing → refuse to advance until it holds. Most self-teaching skips steps two and three entirely, which is why it feels like effort without traction.

Concept

Poka-yoke came from a missing spring, and the rename matters

In 1961 at Yamada Electric, workers assembling a push-button switch sometimes forgot to place a small spring underneath. Shigeo Shingo did not issue a warning or retrain anyone. He changed the process so that a missing spring became immediately visible — the error could still happen, but it could no longer travel.

The technique was first called baka-yoke, "fool-proofing", and was renamed poka-yoke, "mistake-proofing", because the first name insulted the people doing the work. That rename is the whole philosophy in one word: the error is a property of the process, not a defect in the person. Applied to studying, it stops the useless move of resolving to "be more careful" and starts the useful one of asking what would have caught this.

Concept

Andon — stopping the line is the expensive part, and that's the point

Toyota's andon system gives a production worker the authority to signal a problem and halt the line so the underlying defect gets fixed rather than passed downstream. Notice what that costs: stopping a line is expensive and visible, and the system accepts that cost because a defect that keeps moving is more expensive still.

The study equivalent is the uncomfortable one. When you hit something you do not actually understand, the cheap move is to keep reading and hope it resolves. Andon says stop, find the real gap, fix it, then resume. Every hour spent on chapter nine while chapter four is still broken is a defect travelling downstream.

Concept

Kumon — what a mastery gate actually is

In 1954 a high-school maths teacher, Toru Kumon, started writing daily incremental worksheets for his son Takeshi after a poor test result. The design rule is the interesting part: the sheets advance in small steps, and only after correction and demonstrated mastery. Not after time spent, not after completion — after mastery. Kumon's own history reports that Takeshi reached calculus by sixth grade; the method later scaled to millions of students.

Two honest notes. That calculus claim comes from Kumon, an interested party, so treat it as the company's account rather than an independent finding. And the mechanism is not mysterious — a gate that only opens on mastery makes it impossible to accumulate the quiet debt that makes later material feel impossible.

The trap

Adopting the vocabulary instead of the loop

It is easy to put "kaizen" in a journal and change nothing. Three specific failure modes:

TrapWhy it fails
Kaizen as "improve everything constantly"Kaizen is one variable at a time. Change five things and you have learned nothing about any of them — you have run an experiment with no control
Kata as "never deviate"The form is a stage, not a destination. Traditional arts name three: shu (keep the form), ha (break it), ri (leave it). Copying is where you start, and staying there is its own failure
Hansei as self-criticismReflection that produces feeling bad is not reflection. The output of hansei is a specific change to make next session — if there is no change, it did not happen
In practice

Running the loop on this site

The tools here already implement three of the five, which makes the loop cheap to run rather than something extra to maintain:

PracticeWhat to use
Shokunin — mastery gatesGrade flashcards honestly. Pressing Again is a mastery gate refusing to open; pressing Good on a card you half-knew defeats the whole scheduler
Hansei — reflectionThe notepad. One entry per session: what broke, and the one change for next time
Poka-yoke — visible errorsThe quizzes, and the ✓ reviewed markers. A wrong quiz answer is an error made visible on the spot rather than discovered in an exam
Kata — copy the form firstRun the commands in a card exactly as written once, in a lab you can reset, before adapting them

🪞 Hansei — The 15 Minutes That Beat 15 More LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

The finding

In a field experiment at an Indian business-process-outsourcing company, employees in technical training were split: one group spent the final 15 minutes of each training day writing reflections on what they had learned, the other group spent those 15 minutes working. The reflection group scored roughly 23% better on the final assessment.

Same clock. Same material. The difference is entirely in what the last quarter-hour was spent on. The primary paper is "Learning by Thinking: How Reflection Can Spur Progress Along the Learning Curve", summarised in Harvard Business Review as "The Power of Reflection at Work".

Why it matters

It inverts the instinct

When time is short, nearly everyone spends the last fifteen minutes doing one more module, one more video, one more page. This result says that is the worse trade — and it is a *trade*, not an addition, which is why it is actionable for anyone who genuinely has no spare time. You are not being asked to find fifteen minutes. You are being asked to spend fifteen you already have differently.

The mechanism is well-supported by learning research more broadly: retrieving and articulating what you just learned consolidates it, while passive additional exposure mostly produces the feeling of learning. This site has a card on that too — see Learning How to Learn.

Hands-on

Four prompts, fifteen minutes, written down

# Written, not thought. Thinking about it feels identical to
# doing it and produces much less. The notepad on this site works.

# 1. What did I actually learn today? (not "what did I cover")
   Subnetting: the /26 case finally clicked - 4 subnets, 62 hosts.

# 2. Where did I get stuck, and what was the real cause?
   Kept miscounting host bits. Real cause: doing it in my head
   instead of writing the octet out.

# 3. What would have caught that error sooner?  (poka-yoke)
   Write the binary octet every time, even when it feels obvious.

# 4. What ONE thing changes next session?  (kaizen)
   Binary octet on paper for every question, no exceptions.

Prompt 1 is the retrieval. Prompts 2 and 3 are where the reflection earns its keep — a cause and a countermeasure, not a mood. Prompt 4 is the only line that has to survive until tomorrow.

The trap

Reflection that produces no change is a diary

Hansei in its original setting is not about feeling bad — it is structurally the opposite. It identifies a cause and a countermeasure, and in Toyota's practice the countermeasure belongs to the process rather than the person. Applied to study, the test is simple: if a session's reflection produced no specific change to how you work tomorrow, it did not happen. "Should focus more" is not a countermeasure. "Phone in another room, timer for 25 minutes" is.

The second trap is scale. One change per session, reviewed weekly. A reflection that generates six countermeasures produces none of them.

Honest limits

What the 23% figure does and does not support

It is one field experiment, in technical training, at one company, with a short assessment horizon. That is a genuinely good study design — a randomised field trial with a demanding control group who spent the time working rather than idling — but a single effect size does not transfer as a guarantee, and 23% is not a number to expect on your own study log.

What it supports well is the direction: at the margin, time spent consolidating beats time spent covering. That direction is corroborated by a large body of independent work on retrieval practice and the testing effect, which is the reason to act on it. Treat the specific figure as a headline and the direction as the finding.

🏛️ The Memory Palace — Method of Loci for Ordered Lists LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

Trade a weak memory for a strong one

Human memory for arbitrary ordered lists is poor. Human memory for places you have walked through is extraordinary — you can mentally tour your childhood home right now, room by room, in order, without effort. The method of loci exploits the gap: you attach each item you need to remember to a specific location along a route you already know, and then recall becomes a walk rather than a retrieval.

It is old. The technique is set out in the Rhetorica ad Herennium (roughly the first century BCE) and discussed by Cicero and Quintilian as standard equipment for orators speaking for an hour without notes. The origin story — the poet Simonides identifying crushed banquet guests by where they had been seated — is a traditional legend rather than documented history, but the technique it describes was taught as a professional skill for centuries.

Why it matters

Memory athletes are not built differently — they use a strategy

This is the finding worth knowing, because it changes what you conclude about your own memory. A study of World Memory Championship competitors published in Nature Neuroscience (Maguire and colleagues, 2003) found no structural brain differences between superior memorizers and controls, and no difference on general intelligence measures. What differed was which brain regions they engaged — spatial and navigational areas — consistent with nearly all of them reporting use of loci-based methods.

A later training study (Dresler and colleagues, Neuron, 2017) took people with ordinary memory, trained them in loci-based mnemonics for six weeks, and found large and durable improvements in list recall, alongside connectivity patterns shifting toward those of memory athletes. The capability is trainable, and the ceiling is much higher than most people assume theirs is.

Hands-on

Building one, using the OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) layers

# 1. Pick a route you know cold. Your home, your commute, your
#    old school. Fixed order, no ambiguity about what comes next.
#    Seven stops for seven layers.

# 2. Walk it once in your head and name the stops out loud.
#    Front door -> hallway -> kitchen -> stairs -> landing ->
#    bathroom -> bedroom.  Lock the ORDER before adding anything.

# 3. Place one item per stop, as a concrete absurd image.
#    Absurd is not decoration - distinctive images survive,
#    sensible ones blur together.

Front door   L1 Physical    - a fat CAT-6 cable used as a door chain
Hallway      L2 Data Link   - MAC addresses stencilled on every coat hook
Kitchen      L3 Network     - a satnav on the counter routing the kettle
Stairs       L4 Transport   - a courier hauling parcels up, demanding
                              a signature for each  (TCP)
Landing      L5 Session     - a bouncer with a clipboard, logging who's
                              still connected
Bathroom     L6 Presentation- a mirror translating your reflection into
                              another language  (encoding, encryption)
Bedroom      L7 Application - your laptop open on the bed, browser running

# 4. Walk it forwards. Then BACKWARDS - that is the test that
#    proves the locations are doing the work, not a sing-song
#    mnemonic sentence you memorised instead.

Backwards recall is the honest check. A first-letter mnemonic ("Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away") only runs one direction and collapses if you forget one word. A palace is random-access: ask "what's layer 4?" and you go straight to the stairs.

The trap

A palace stores; it does not explain

This is the failure mode that matters, and most enthusiastic accounts of the technique skip it. You can build a flawless palace for the seven OSI layers and still not be able to say which layer a problem lives at — because naming a list and understanding a model are different skills, and the technique only delivers the first. Worse, fluent recall feels exactly like understanding, so a palace can hide a gap rather than reveal one.

Use it for the index, not the content. Loci gets the seven layers into permanent, random-access storage in twenty minutes so that your actual study time goes on what happens at each one. Two further honest limits:

LimitWhat to do about it
Encoding costs real effort up frontOnly build one for material that is arbitrary, ordered, and that you have already failed to remember the normal way
Reusing a route overwrites the old contentsKeep a dedicated route per subject, or accept the palace is disposable — fine for an exam, not for a career
In practice

Where it sits alongside the tools here

Loci and spaced repetition solve different halves of the same problem and work well together. The palace gets a list in fast; the scheduler keeps it there. Build the palace once, then let the flashcards on this site surface the same list on the usual intervals — the review takes seconds because you are walking a route, not straining for an answer, and each successful walk strengthens both.

If the list is one you keep failing on, that is the signal to build a palace for it rather than grade it Again for the fifth time. Repeated failure on the same card is information about the method, not about you.

🌙 Sleep — Where the Studying Actually Sticks LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

Studying encodes. Sleep is what files it

Everything else on this site — flashcards, quizzes, spaced repetition, reflection — is encoding. It gets material in. The process that converts a fragile new trace into something durable happens largely while you are asleep, and it is not passive: during deep non-REM sleep the hippocampus replays the day's material and hands it off to the cortex for long-term storage. This is the consolidation the scheduler on this site is implicitly betting on.

Which produces an uncomfortable arithmetic. An extra hour of study taken out of sleep is not a straight trade of rest for revision. You are paying for the encoding with the consolidation, and the consolidation is the part that decides what you still know next month.

Reference

Sleep matters on both sides of a study session

WhenWhat it does
Sleep before learningPrepares the hippocampus to encode at all. Sleep-deprived subjects show measurably reduced hippocampal activity while trying to learn new material — the deficit is in taking it in, not just in keeping it
Sleep after learningConsolidation. Deep non-REM is associated with declarative material — facts, definitions, the things flashcards hold. REM is more associated with procedural skill and emotional processing
NapsA nap long enough to reach deep sleep can measurably help recall of material learned beforehand. Real but modest; a nap is a supplement to a night, not a substitute for one
ConsistencyA stable wake time matters alongside total duration — an erratic schedule produces the effects of short sleep even when the hours roughly add up

The practical shape: study, then sleep. Reviewing material in the evening and sleeping on it puts consolidation immediately after encoding, which is the ordering the mechanism wants. Cramming until 3am inverts it.

The trap

You lose the ability to notice you are impaired

This is the finding that matters most, and the reason "I do fine on five hours" is not evidence. In controlled chronic sleep-restriction studies, objective performance keeps degrading day after day while subjective sleepiness levels off. People adapt to how being impaired feels long before they stop being impaired. You are not calibrated to judge this about yourself, and neither is anyone else.

So treat the self-report as unreliable and look at outcomes instead: how many cards you are failing on the second pass, whether you reread paragraphs, whether you are irritable at things that would not normally irritate you.

The genuine short-sleeper phenotype — people who function normally on very little sleep, associated with rare gene variants — exists and is very rare. The base rate makes it a bad bet about yourself, and the people who claim it are overwhelmingly in the first category rather than the second.

Honest limits

What this card will not tell you

Sleep is where the self-improvement genre is at its worst, so it is worth being explicit about the edges of the claim:

ClaimStatus
"Sleep 4 hours and feel like 8"Not supported. You can reduce how bad it feels — caffeine does that — without recovering the function you lost
"7–9 hours for adults"A consensus recommendation across a population, not a personal prescription. Individual need genuinely varies; the variation is smaller than people want it to be
Specific sleep-stage percentages you should hitConsumer trackers estimate stages from movement and heart rate and are not reliable at that resolution. Useful for spotting duration and consistency trends; not for optimising a hypnogram
Catching up at the weekendPartially works for sleepiness. Does not retroactively consolidate what you failed to encode on Wednesday

And the obvious one: persistent trouble sleeping despite decent habits is a medical question, not a discipline problem. Insomnia and sleep apnoea are treatable and are not fixed by a better bedtime routine.

In practice

On-call makes this a professional problem, not a personal one

A rotation that wakes you at 03:00 is not just unpleasant — it removes the deep sleep in which the week's learning was going to consolidate, and it does so on a schedule you did not choose. That is worth naming out loud when a rota is being designed: follow-the-sun coverage, a compensating day, and a hard limit on consecutive nights are sleep-protection measures, not perks. See Surviving On-Call and Burnout on this site for the rest of that argument.

And if you are studying for a certification while on a rotation, expect the schedule to be the constraint rather than your discipline. Plan for fewer, better-timed sessions instead of assuming you will make up the difference by trying harder.

🎯 Attention — Task Switching, Residue & the Cost of Interruption LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

You do not multitask. You switch, and switching is not free

For anything requiring thought, the brain does not run two tasks at once — it alternates, and each alternation costs time and accuracy while it reconfigures. This is one of the more robust findings in cognitive psychology, and the practical consequence is blunt: two things done alternately take longer and come out worse than the same two done in sequence.

Which makes IT work structurally hostile to learning. The job is built out of interruptions — pages, tickets, chat, "quick question" — and a study session squeezed into that texture is mostly switch cost.

Concept

Attention residue — the part that explains the frustration

The switch cost is not just the moment of switching. Research by Sophie Leroy describes attention residue: after moving to a new task, part of your attention stays on the previous one, degrading performance on the new task for some time. You are physically at the new task while a fraction of you is still on the last one.

The useful nuance is when residue is worst: leaving a task unfinished and unplanned. Leroy's work indicates that having a concrete plan for how you will resume substantially reduces the residue — the mind appears to let go of an interrupted task more readily once there is a recorded intention to return to it. That turns an unpleasant finding into a two-line habit.

Observational studies of office work also report interruption recovery taking on the order of twenty minutes. That figure gets quoted with a precision the underlying research does not really support, so treat it as an order of magnitude — the direction is solid, the exact number is not a constant.

Hands-on

The parking note — the highest-value two lines you will write

# Before you answer the page, take the interrupt, or close the
# laptop mid-thought, write these two lines. Ten seconds.

WAS: tracing why the VLAN 40 hosts can't reach the gateway.
     Confirmed the trunk carries 40. Next: check the SVI is up.

# That is it. "WAS" kills the residue - the task is recorded,
# so you can stop holding it. "Next" removes the worst part of
# resuming, which is not the work but re-deciding where you were.

# The notepad on this site is a fine place for these. So is a
# sticky note. The medium does not matter; writing it does.

The trap

Believing you are the exception

People who multitask the most tend to rate themselves as best at it and to perform worst on objective switching measures. Some of the specific studies in this area have had a mixed replication record, so hold the effect size loosely — but the self-assessment problem is the same one as with sleep, and it is the actionable part: your confidence about your own attention is not measurement.

The second trap is treating focus as a character trait. It is mostly environment and structure. Someone who cannot concentrate with a phone buzzing and six tabs of chat open is not undisciplined; they are in a room designed to prevent concentration. Change the room before concluding anything about yourself.

Third: a long unbroken block is not automatically better. Sustained hard focus degrades, and deliberately spacing study — which the scheduler on this site does for you — beats one heroic session. The goal is uninterrupted blocks, not maximum duration.

In practice

Two hours, honestly measured

A useful and slightly deflating exercise: for one study session, tally a mark in the notepad every time your attention leaves the material — a check of a phone, a tab switch, a thought about work. Do not try to change anything, just count. Most people are surprised, and the count is a far better baseline than a memory of how the session felt.

Then change one thing next session and count again — which is the Kaizen rule from The Japanese Mastery Loop, applied to your own attention instead of to the material.

📝 Note-Taking for Learning — Notes You Never Reopen Are Theatre LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept

A note does two jobs, and most people only ever get one

Writing a note has an encoding function — the act of deciding what matters and putting it in your own words is itself the learning — and a storage function, where the note is a record you come back to. Both are real. The failure is that almost everyone optimises for storage, produces beautiful complete notes, and never reopens them. That gets you neither: the transcription was too shallow to teach you anything, and the archive is never read.

The single test worth applying to any note-taking system: does it force me to process the material, and does it produce something I will actually use later? If the answer to either is no, it is a hobby.

The trap

The collector's fallacy

Highlighting, saving, bookmarking and copying all feel like progress and produce almost none. The feeling comes from the effort and the visible output — 200 Kindle highlights, a tidy folder, a page of clean notes — and it is uncoupled from whether you know anything. Rereading has the same problem: it is the most popular study method and among the least effective, precisely because familiarity feels like knowledge.

The uncomfortable diagnostic: if you cannot remember roughly what is in a set of notes without opening them, they have not taught you anything yet. That is not an argument against notes — it is an argument that the note is the raw material and not the result.

Reference

Two kinds of note, and conflating them ruins both

This is the most useful distinction in the whole topic, and it matters especially in IT because so much of the material is commands.

Keep them in different places, or at minimum different sections. A runbook that has been "summarised in your own words" is dangerous. A conceptual note that is a verbatim copy of a paragraph is useless. Most people's notes are an unhelpful average of the two.

Honest limits

On handwriting versus typing

You will have heard that handwriting notes beats typing them. That comes from a well-known 2014 study (Mueller and Oppenheimer, "The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard"), which found longhand note-takers did better on conceptual questions. It has since had a mixed replication record — later attempts, including a larger 2019 study, did not reproduce the key effects. So do not treat "always use paper" as settled.

What survives is the proposed mechanism, and it is the part worth acting on: typing makes verbatim transcription easy, and transcription is shallow processing. Handwriting helps only because it is too slow to transcribe, forcing you to select and paraphrase. If you type but deliberately refuse to transcribe — paraphrase, compress, write questions — you get the benefit without the wrist ache. If you handwrite but copy the slide word for word, the paper does not save you.

The medium is not the variable. Paraphrasing is the variable.

🧩 Study Systems That Survive a Brain That Won't Cooperate PRODUCTIVITY • Executive Function ▶
Scope

What this card is, and what it deliberately is not

This is about structure: how to build a study system that keeps working when initiation is hard, attention is inconsistent, and time does not feel like it passes at a steady rate. Those difficulties are common in ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) and are also common without it — under stress, on shift work, during burnout, in a job made of interruptions.

It is not a diagnostic tool and contains no symptom checklist on purpose. Whether you have ADHD is a clinical question for a clinician, and a study system is not a substitute for assessment or treatment where those are indicated. What follows is useful regardless of the answer, which is why it is written this way.

Core Concept

Design for the bad day, not the good one

Most study plans are built by a motivated person on a good evening, for a person who will also be motivated on every future evening. That person does not reliably show up. A plan that requires consistency to work will fail the first week it does not get consistency — and then the failure gets read as a character flaw rather than as a design fault.

The inversion: assume you will miss days, assume some sessions will be four minutes, and build something where that is fine and the system still functions. A system that survives a bad week beats a better system that does not.

Reference

Five structural moves, and what each is actually solving

MoveThe problem it addressesWhat it looks like
Externalise everythingHolding intentions in your head is the expensive part, and the load is invisible until it drops somethingWritten parking notes, a visible next-action, alarms that carry meaning. Out of your head, into the environment
Cut initiation cost to near zeroStarting is very often the bottleneck — not effort, not capability, not wanting toLeave the laptop open on the card. Queue the deck the night before. Make the first action so small it is embarrassing to refuse
Make time visibleTime passing is not reliably felt, so "I'll just check one thing" becomes forty minutes with no internal alarmA visible countdown, not a clock. An alarm for the end of a session, not just the start

The trap

Streaks, and other systems that punish a missed day

A streak counter converts one missed day into a reason to stop entirely. It is a motivational device that works well for people who rarely miss and works actively against people who sometimes do — which is precisely backwards, because the second group is the one that needs to find restarting cheap.

The single most important property of a study system here is that restarting costs nothing. No streak to lose, no "start the plan over", no backlog that greets you with a number designed to make you feel behind. Anything done today counts, including four minutes.

Worth being straight about a limitation of the tools on this site: the spaced-repetition scheduler does accumulate due cards while you are away, so a week off produces a large number on the study button. That number is not a debt and it is not a verdict. Cap the session — twenty cards, then stop — and let the rest come back tomorrow. Clearing a backlog in one sitting is the least useful thing you can do with it.

Hands-on

Body doubling, and why it is worth trying despite thin evidence

Working alongside someone else — same room, or a video call with both of you silently working — is widely reported to make starting and sustaining much easier. The evidence base is mostly practice and self-report rather than trials, so it is offered here as worth testing, not as established. The reason to test it anyway: it is free, it takes one session to evaluate, and the downside is a wasted hour.

# A session designed to be easy to start, not impressive.

Notice that the plan has no failure state. There is no outcome where you "didn't really study", which is the point — a system with a failure state will eventually be avoided to prevent triggering it.

🎯 Retrieval Practice — Why Testing Yourself Beats Rereading Lifestyle • Learning ▶
Concept
Pulling It Out Is the Learning; Putting It In Is Not
The single most reliable finding in the study of studying is that trying to recall something strengthens the memory far more than reading it again does. Rereading feels productive because the material gets easier each pass — but that fluency is recognition, not recall, and recognition is not what an exam or a broken production system asks for. Closing the page and trying to reconstruct what was on it feels worse, goes slower, and works substantially better.
ActivityFeelsWorks
Rereading notesProductive and calmPoorly — the weakest common technique
HighlightingActivePoorly; it is mostly deciding, not learning
Watching a course againReassuringPoorly, for the same reason
Closing the book and writing what you rememberUncomfortableVery well
Answering questions without lookingEffortfulVery well
Explaining it to somebodyExposingVery well — the gaps announce themselves
The discomfort is not a side effect to be minimised. It is the signal that retrieval is happening, and study that feels easy is usually study that is not working — the point the next card is built around.
Reference
Turning Any Material Into Retrieval
None of this needs an app. The move is always the same: create a moment where the answer is not visible and you have to produce it.
MethodHow
Blank-page recallAfter reading, write everything you remember. Then check. The gaps are your study list
Question-first notesWrite notes as questions, not statements: "what does a SYN-ACK mean?" rather than a paragraph about it
Cover and reciteCover the right-hand column of any reference table and reconstruct it
Teach the empty roomExplain it out loud, at speaking pace, without notes
Do, do not readBuild the thing in a lab. Practical work is retrieval with consequences
Predict before revealingBefore running a command, say what the output will be
The last one is nearly free and unreasonably effective for technical study: it converts every command you were going to run anyway into a test. Being wrong out loud, before the machine tells you, is the moment the correction sticks.
Troubleshooting
Spacing — the Multiplier on All of It
Retrieval works best when it happens after you have started to forget. Reviewing while the material is still fresh costs the same time and returns much less, which is why cramming produces a pass and no durable knowledge.
PatternOutcome
Six hours the night beforeEnough to pass, gone in a fortnight
One hour a week for six weeksSame total time, far more retained
Review when it feels slightly hard to recallThe ideal moment — effort is the mechanism
Review when it is still easyMostly wasted; push the interval out
Review after total forgettingRelearning, which is slower than not forgetting
Scheduling this by hand is tedious, which is exactly why spaced-repetition software exists — an algorithm tracking when each item is about to slip. This site's own study tools implement one over the flashcards, so the cards return on a schedule rather than randomly. The teaching-side counterpart, for anyone who has to run training rather than sit it, is How Adults Actually Learn — Relevance, Practice, Feedback & Spacing.
🔀 Interleaving & Desirable Difficulties — Why Easy Study Works Worse Lifestyle • Learning ▶
Concept
Performance During Practice Is Not Learning
This is the idea that reorganises everything else. How well you do while practising and how much you retain afterwards are different things, and several conditions that make practice go worse make retention go better. Blocked practice — twenty subnetting questions in a row — produces a smooth session and a fragile skill, because after the first two you are applying a method rather than choosing one. Mixed practice feels clumsy and builds the thing you actually need: the ability to recognise which problem you are looking at.
DifficultyMakes practiceMakes learning
Mixing topics rather than blocking themSlower, more errorsMore durable, more transferable
Spacing sessions outHarder to recall each timeSubstantially better
Testing instead of reviewingUncomfortableBetter
Varying the conditionsInconsistentBetter — less tied to one context
Attempting before being taughtFrustrating, often wrongBetter, even when the attempt fails
These are called desirable difficulties for a reason: not everything hard is useful. Bad handwriting, a noisy room and an unclear explanation make practice worse and learning worse. The desirable ones are specifically those that force retrieval or discrimination.
Reference
What Interleaving Looks Like in Technical Study
The rule of thumb: if you can tell what kind of problem is coming next without reading it, you are blocking.
BlockedInterleaved
A chapter of subnetting, then a chapter of routingA mixed set where each question could be either
Fifty flashcards from one deckFifty from four decks, shuffled
All the Linux commands, then all the PowerShell"Do this task" — you choose the tool
Reading one domain end to endRotating three domains across the week
Practising the exploit you just read aboutA lab where the vulnerability is not announced
Notice what the right-hand column has in common: the identification step is preserved. Real work never announces which chapter it came from, and a person who has only ever practised in blocks has never rehearsed the first and hardest move.
Troubleshooting
When Blocking Is Right, and How to Tell
Interleaving is not a universal instruction, and applying it too early produces a discouraged beginner rather than a durable one.
SituationDo this
Meeting a concept for the first timeBlock. You need a method before you can choose between methods
The mechanics are shakyBlock until the procedure runs without thinking
You can do it, slowlyStart interleaving — this is the moment
You keep confusing two similar thingsInterleave those two specifically; discrimination is the whole skill
Practice feels smooth and scores are highIncrease the difficulty; you have stopped learning
Everything feels impossibleToo much at once — narrow it, then rebuild
The fourth row is the highest-value application on this page. Two things that are easy apart and confusing together — TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) versus UDP (User Datagram Protocol) behaviour, symmetric versus asymmetric keys, authentication versus authorisation — are learned by being forced to tell them apart, over and over, in a random order. Studying them separately is precisely what preserves the confusion.
🏋️ Deliberate Practice — Why "10,000 Hours" Is the Wrong Number Lifestyle • Learning ▶
Concept
Experience and Practice Are Not the Same Thing
Twenty years in IT can mean twenty years of improvement or one year repeated twenty times, and the difference is not talent or effort — it is whether the work contained any of the conditions under which skill actually changes. Most professional work does not. It sits comfortably inside what you can already do, gives feedback that is delayed or absent, and rewards finishing over improving. That is why "years of experience" predicts so much less than people expect, and why a deliberate hour can outperform a distracted week.
ConditionWhy it matters
Just beyond current abilityInside it, nothing changes; far beyond it, nothing sticks
A specific target, not "get better""Read a packet capture without the filter cheatsheet" is practisable; "improve at networking" is not
Immediate, honest feedbackWithout it you rehearse your mistakes to fluency
Full attentionPractice while distracted is repetition, not practice
Repetition with correctionThe correction is the point; the repetition only delivers it
Effortful, and therefore shortReal practice is tiring — an hour is a lot, and four is a fantasy
The "10,000 hours" figure entered popular use stripped of every one of these conditions, which is what makes it misleading. The original research described hours of *this*, under coaching, in domains with clear feedback — not hours of showing up.
Reference
Manufacturing the Conditions in an IT Career
The obstacle is usually feedback: most technical work tells you whether it worked, not whether you did it well. These are the practical substitutes.
Missing conditionSubstitute
No coachCode review, pull requests, a mentor, or a public write-up that invites correction
No feedbackPredict the outcome in writing before you act, then compare
Nothing at the edge of abilityVolunteer for the ticket you do not know how to solve
No repetitionA lab: rebuild the same thing three times, faster and cleaner each time
No specific targetPick one weakness a fortnight and name what "fixed" looks like
No timeTwenty focused minutes beats a scheduled two hours that never happens
Predicting in writing before acting is the highest-leverage habit on this table, and it is the same move as the retrieval card's "say what the output will be": it manufactures feedback out of work you were doing anyway, and it turns a vague sense of competence into a hit rate you can watch move.
Troubleshooting
Plateaus, and What They Usually Mean
A plateau is information. It almost always means the practice has drifted back inside the comfortable zone, and the fix is diagnostic rather than motivational.
SymptomLikely causeMove
Comfortable and not improvingDifficulty drifted downAdd a constraint — no notes, half the time, explain it aloud
Improving at practice, not at workPractice is too unlike the real thingMake the conditions messier and less announced
Same mistake repeatedlyFeedback is absent or arriving too lateShorten the loop until the error is visible immediately
Exhausted after twenty minutesNothing — this is correctStop. Two of those a day is a serious practice
Avoiding one area entirelyIt is the weakness, and avoidance is the tellSchedule it first, in small pieces
The last row is worth acting on the moment you notice it. The subject you keep rescheduling is almost never the boring one — it is the one where being bad at it is uncomfortable, which makes it precisely the highest-value hour available.
🕰️ Procrastination — What It Actually Is, and the Moves That Work Lifestyle • Getting Things Done ▶
Concept
A Mood Problem Wearing a Time-Management Costume
Procrastination is not poor planning and it is not laziness — people who procrastinate reliably do other demanding things instead. It is avoidance of a feeling attached to a task: boredom, confusion, fear of doing it badly, resentment at having been given it. Delay relieves the feeling immediately, which is why it is so persistent: it works, every time, for about ten minutes. This matters practically, because it means the effective moves target the feeling or the first step, and better calendars do almost nothing.
The feelingWhat it sounds likeWhat helps
Ambiguity"I don't know where to start"Define the next physical action, not the task
Fear of doing it badly"I need to do this properly"Give yourself permission to produce a bad first version
Boredom"This is tedious"Time-box it and pair it with something tolerable
Resentment"Why is this mine?"Name it honestly; sometimes the answer is to escalate, not to start
Overwhelm"It's enormous"Shrink the unit until starting is trivial
Perfectionism"Not yet, it isn't ready"Ship a draft on a fixed date and iterate
Reference
Moves That Work, Roughly in Order of Reliability
All of them share a shape: make starting smaller than the resistance.
MoveDetail
Name the next physical actionNot "do the migration plan" but "open a document and write three headings"
Five minutes, then permission to stopThe permission has to be genuine. Usually you continue; when you do not, five minutes still happened
Decide when and where, in advance"Tuesday 09:00, at my desk, the firewall doc" beats intention by a wide margin
Remove one step of frictionLeave the file open. Most starting cost is the setup, not the work
Make the avoidance harderPhone in another room. Willpower loses to proximity
Work where being seen is likelyAn office, a call with someone else working, a shared timer
Forgive the last delaySelf-criticism reliably increases the next one — this is a well-replicated finding, not encouragement
That last row surprises people. Being harsh with yourself about procrastinating raises the emotional cost of the task, and the emotional cost is the mechanism — so self-criticism makes the next attempt less likely, not more. Dropping it is one of the few genuinely free improvements available.
Troubleshooting
When It Is Not Procrastination
Several different problems present as "I can't make myself do this", and the productivity advice is actively wrong for most of them.
Looks like procrastinationIs actually
Cannot start anything, for weeksPossibly burnout or depression — a different problem with different help
Avoiding one specific task, indefinitelyOften a missing decision or a missing permission, not a missing hour
Endless research before startingFrequently a genuine competence gap; learn the piece, deliberately, then start
Everything urgent, nothing importantA workload problem. No personal technique fixes an overloaded queue
Cannot concentrate at all todaySleep, illness, or something happening in your life
Consistent, lifelong, across domainsWorth discussing with a professional rather than a productivity book
The distinction that matters most: a technique problem responds to a technique. If a month of honest attempts changes nothing, the diagnosis was wrong, and continuing to apply productivity advice to a health or workload problem mostly adds guilt to it — Burnout — Recognizing It Before It Breaks You is the better starting point when that is the shape of it.
📝 Exam Technique — The Marks You Lose Without Knowing Less Lifestyle • Certification ▶
Concept
The Exam Is a Second Skill, and It Is Cheaper to Learn
Two people with identical knowledge routinely score differently, and the gap is technique: how they read a question, when they guess, what they do when stuck, how they manage the clock. This is worth saying plainly because it is unglamorous and it is where the fastest improvement usually is. If you are failing by a small margin, more study is often the wrong prescription — a practice exam under real conditions will tell you within an hour whether you are losing marks to knowledge or to process.
FailureTell
Ran out of timeUnanswered questions at the end, or rushing the last third

Certification questions are written to a formula, and knowing the formula is worth marks.

A plan made in the exam room is made under stress. Make it beforehand.

A practice exam only tells you something if it is taken under real conditions — full length, timed, no notes, no pausing. Taken comfortably it measures recognition, which is the same trap as rereading: it feels like preparation and it is measuring the wrong thing. 🔋 Energy, Not Time — Scheduling Around the Brain You Actually Have Lifestyle • Getting Things Done ▶
Concept

Calendars treat every hour as interchangeable, and they are not: an hour at your sharpest might absorb a difficult new concept, while an hour at four in the afternoon after three meetings will not, no matter how firmly it is blocked out. 🔁 The Weekly Review — The Habit That Makes Every Other System Work LIFE • Getting Things Done ▶
Core Concept
Capture Without Review Is a Graveyard
Every capture system — inbox, notes app, task list, the notebook — works on the way in and degrades on the way out. Things go in, nothing comes out, and within a month the list is long enough that opening it feels bad, so it stops being opened. The review is the only mechanism that converts a list into a plan, and it is the first thing dropped when busy, which is exactly when the list is growing fastest.
Without a reviewWith one
The list grows monotonicallyIt is pruned weekly, so its length means something
Opening it produces guiltOpening it produces the next three things
Old items stay foreverOld items get deleted on purpose, which is a decision
You remember what is urgentYou are reminded of what is important, which urgency hides
The asymmetry is what makes this worth a habit rather than an intention. Capture is easy and immediately rewarding; review is dull and pays later, so without a fixed slot the first happens and the second does not, and the system degrades in a way that feels like a personal failing rather than a missing step.
The Session
Thirty to Sixty Minutes, Same Slot Every Week
The content matters less than the recurrence. A mediocre review that happens weekly beats a thorough one that happens when there is time, because the value is in the frequency, not the depth. Put it in the calendar in a slot you protect, and treat a missed week as a signal rather than a failure.
StepThe question it answers
Empty every inboxEmail, notes, photos of whiteboards, the scrap of paper. What arrived that I have not looked at?
Review last week's calendarWhat happened that created work I have not written down? This finds more than the inboxes do
Review the open listWhat is done, what is dead, what is next? Deleting is the point
Look at next week's calendarWhat needs preparing before it arrives?
Pick three for the weekIf only three things happen, which three? Not a plan — a filter
If only one step survives a bad week, keep “pick three”. It takes five minutes, it requires the list to have been looked at, and it produces the single artefact the rest of the week runs on. Everything above it is preparation for that decision.
Why It Fails
Three Predictable Failure Modes, and the Fix for Each
The review is a simple habit that fails in the same three ways for almost everyone, and each has a small fix rather than a discipline problem.
FailureFix

It turns into doing the workReviewing and doing are different modes. Write the next action down and move on; a review that becomes an afternoon of work will not survive

There is no obvious slotAttach it to something that already recurs — Friday afternoon, Sunday evening, the commute
It feels like adminIt is. That is why it needs a fixed slot rather than motivation — see Motivation — Why Waiting For It Fails, and What Low Motivation Is Telling You
The single highest-value step is the second in the previous card and it is the one most often omitted: read back through last week's calendar. Commitments made in meetings are the largest source of untracked work in most people's lives, and they never arrive in an inbox — they arrive as a sentence somebody said, which is why they only surface when something has already been missed.
📥 The Inbox — Why It Is Not a To-Do List, and What Replaces It LIFE • Getting Things Done ▶
Core Concept
An Inbox Sorts by Arrival, Which Is the One Ordering Guaranteed Not to Match Importance
Using an inbox as a to-do list means letting the order things happened to arrive in decide the order you do them. Arrival time correlates with nothing that matters: not urgency, not importance, not effort. It correlates only with how organised other people are, which is why an inbox-driven day feels busy and productive and ends with the important thing still undone.
Property of an inboxConsequence
Sorted by arrivalThe loudest and latest win. Yours never arrives, so it never surfaces
Mixed types in one listA two-second reply sits beside a two-week project, and the list reads as uniformly heavy
Read status as a proxy for doneItems are 'handled' by being read, then reappear as guilt
No place for “not now”Deferral has to be done by leaving things unread, which is not a system
Someone else fills itThe list of what you do is authored by other people
The last row is the one worth sitting with, because it is the whole argument rather than a grievance about email. An inbox is a list of other people's priorities, and working from it directly means your own work only happens in the gaps — which is a structural outcome, not a discipline problem.
Core Concept
Every Workable System Separates Capture, Decision and Action
The mechanism — app, notebook, index cards — matters far less than the separation. Three distinct activities get conflated in an inbox, and every method that works pulls them apart, whatever it calls them.
ActivityWhat it isWhen
CaptureGetting it out of your head, unsorted, instantlyContinuously, and it must be frictionless or it will not happen
DecisionWhat is this, and what is the next physical action?In a batch — this is what the weekly review is
ActionDoing the thingFrom a list of decided next actions, not from the inbox
Conflating capture and decisionDeciding while things arriveInterrupt-driven, and exhausting
Conflating decision and actionDoing whatever you just decided aboutThe two-minute rule aside, this is how a triage session becomes an afternoon
This is why arguments about which application to use miss the point. Any tool that lets you capture in one second and decide in a batch will work; no tool that skips the decision step will — which is also why the weekly review (The Weekly Review — The Habit That Makes Every Other System Work) is load-bearing rather than optional.
Practice
Emptying It, and What Each Outcome Means
“Inbox zero” is widely mocked as an aesthetic and is useful as a definition of done for a triage session: everything has been decided about, and nothing is left whose status is “unread”.
OutcomeWhere it goes
Not actionable, no valueDelete. The largest category if you are honest
Not actionable, might be usefulReference. And be strict — a reference pile nobody searches is a delete you did not commit to
Actionable, under two minutesDo it now. Filing it costs more than doing it
Actionable, longerWrite the next physical action on a list. “Email Sam about the Q3 numbers”, not “Q3”
Actionable, not by youDelegate, and record that you are waiting on someone. The waiting-on list is the one people skip
Actionable, not yetA dated reminder, not a mental note
The row that does the most work is the fourth: write the next physical action, not the topic. A list item that names a project rather than an action requires you to make a decision every time you read it, which is why such lists are re-read and not acted on — and it is why the same three items survive on a list for months.
🗓️ Time Blocking & Its Failure Modes LIFE • Getting Things Done ▶
Core Concept
Deciding When Something Happens Converts a List Into a Finite Day
A to-do list is unbounded and a day is not, and the gap between them is where the feeling of never catching up lives. Time blocking closes it by forcing a confrontation: everything on the list has to fit somewhere, or it is not happening this week. That is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the information.
What a list hidesWhat a calendar reveals
How long the list would takeWhether it fits at all
That two things are scheduled for the same hourThe conflict, before it happens
That the day is already full of meetingsThat there are two hours of work time, not eight
That a task needs a specific kind of attentionWhether that hour is a good one for it
The cost of saying yesWhat is being displaced. Nothing is added to a full day for free
The last row is the practical payoff and the reason to do this even badly. “Yes, and what comes off?” is a much easier conversation to have with a calendar open than with a list, because the displacement is visible to both people rather than being a claim one of them is making.
Practice
The Failure Mode Is Over-Scheduling, and It Is Almost Universal
A plan with no slack survives the first interruption and is then abandoned entirely — not adjusted, abandoned, because a broken plan feels worse than no plan. The abandonment is the failure, not the interruption, and it is caused by scheduling as though the day were empty.
MistakeFix
Every hour blockedBlock 50–60% of the working day. The rest absorbs what actually happens
Estimating optimisticallyTake your estimate and add half. Then check it against last week
No buffer between blocksFifteen minutes. Things overrun, and transitions are not free
Blocking the same work at any hourMatch the work to the energy — analytical work early for most people, and admin when tired
Treating the plan as a commitmentIt is a hypothesis about the day. Re-block at lunchtime rather than abandoning it
The recovery habit matters more than the planning habit. When a block is lost, move it rather than deleting it — the plan being wrong is expected and is not a reason to stop planning, and a person who re-blocks at lunchtime keeps the technique while one who treats the morning's plan as a promise abandons it by Wednesday.
Practice
What to Block, and What Not To
Not everything benefits. Blocking works for things that need continuous attention and fails for things that are inherently interruptible — and putting the second kind on a calendar is what makes people conclude the technique does not work for them.
TaskBlock it?
Deep work — writing, designing, debugging something hardYes. This is what the technique is for
A batch of small adminYes, as one block. Individually they are noise; batched they are a task
Reactive work — a support rota, on-callNo. Block the gaps around it instead, and expect to lose some
Recurring commitmentsYes, and defend them — a recurring block that is always moved is not a commitment
Everything elseLeave it unblocked. That is the buffer
The version that survives a real job is smaller than the technique usually described: block one or two protected sessions a day and leave the rest open. Two hours of genuinely uninterrupted work is more than most people currently get, and a plan that asks for eight will be abandoned by Wednesday — which loses the two as well.
🎯 Focus Blocks — Making Uninterrupted Time Possible in an Interrupt-Driven Job LIFE • Getting Things Done ▶
Core Concept
“I'll Focus When It's Quiet” Never Arrives
On an interrupt-driven job — a support rota, an on-call week, a shared inbox — quiet is not a thing that happens, it is a thing that is arranged. The existing card on attention covers what switching costs; this is the operational question that follows from it, which is how somebody whose job is to be available gets any continuous time at all.
BeliefReality
“Things will calm down next week”The interrupt rate is a property of the role, not of the week
“I can focus between tickets”Recovery from an interruption is measured in many minutes. The gaps are shorter than that
“I'll do it after hours”Works twice, then stops, and it is the mechanism behind most burnout in these roles
“My team knows not to interrupt me”Unstated norms do not survive somebody's urgent thing
“Nobody will cover for me”Usually untested. This is the assumption worth checking first
The last row is where the work is. Uninterrupted time on a rota is a coverage negotiation, not a discipline problem, and treating it as a personal failing produces guilt where a two-line agreement with a colleague would produce two hours.
Practice
Negotiating Coverage, and the Shape of a Realistic Block
The arrangement that works is explicit, reciprocal and small. Asking for a whole morning fails; asking for ninety minutes in exchange for covering somebody else's ninety succeeds, because it costs the team nothing in aggregate.
ElementWhat makes it work
ReciprocalYou cover theirs. The team's total availability is unchanged, which removes the objection
Named and visibleIn the calendar, with who is covering. An unannounced block is just being unresponsive
Bounded — 90 minutes, not a dayShort enough that a genuine emergency can wait, long enough to be worth having
With a real escape hatch“Page me if it is a P1”. Without it people interrupt anyway, because they are not sure
Same slot each dayPredictable to everyone else, which is what stops it being renegotiated daily
Ninety minutes is the number worth defending, and the reason is the previous card's second row: if recovery from an interruption takes 15–20 minutes, a 30-minute block is mostly recovery. Below about an hour the arithmetic stops working, which is why fragmenting a block into three short ones does not preserve its value.
Practice
Protecting the Block From Yourself
Once coverage is arranged, the remaining interruptions are self-inflicted, and they are the harder half. A block that is defended from colleagues and not from your own attention produces ninety minutes of tab-switching.
InterruptionCountermeasure
NotificationsOff, not silenced. A badge count is an interruption you have merely postponed
“I'll just check”Keep a scrap of paper for anything that surfaces, and look at it afterwards. Capture, do not chase
Starting coldLeave a note at the end of the last session saying what comes next. The first ten minutes are otherwise reorientation
A blocker mid-taskWrite it down and keep going on something adjacent. Resolving it usually means leaving the block
Finishing early and driftingHave a second, smaller thing ready. The block is time, and time is the thing you negotiated
The third row is the highest-value habit in this card and takes fifteen seconds: end every session by writing the next concrete step. It removes the reorientation cost from the start of the next one, and on a fragmented day that reorientation is frequently most of what the block would otherwise have been spent on.
🔎 Notes That Get Reopened — Structure, Linking & the Search Test LIFE • Learning Systems ▶
Core Concept
If You Cannot Find It in Ten Seconds, It Does Not Exist
The existing note-taking card is about learning — notes written to be re-encoded. This is about the other pile: the reference notes you wrote so you would not have to work something out twice. For those, retrieval is the only thing that matters, and a note that cannot be found is indistinguishable from a note that was never written.
Design questionThe answer that follows from retrieval
Folders or tags?Neither, mostly. Search is how you will actually look, so optimise for it
Where should this go?Wrong question. It goes anywhere searchable; spend the effort on the title instead
How do I keep it organised?You do not. Organisation is a cost paid continuously for a benefit search already provides
What if I forget it exists?The real failure mode, and the one linking solves rather than filing
How long should a note be?One idea per note. A long note is found once and then scrolled past
This reverses the usual advice deliberately, and the reason is that filing is a cost paid every time you write and a benefit collected almost never. The effort that would go into a folder hierarchy is better spent on two things search cannot do for you: a title you would actually type, and a link from somewhere you will be reading anyway.
Practice
Write the Title as the Search You Will Type
The single highest-leverage habit is in the title, because the title is what you are searching against and what you are scanning in the results. A title that describes the problem you had is findable; one that names the topic is not.
TitleFindable?
“Postgres”No. You will have forty of these
“Postgres connection pool exhausted — what we changed”Yes. It contains the words you would type in a panic
“Meeting notes 2026-04-11”Only if you remember the date, which you will not
“Q3 planning — decision to defer the migration”Yes. The decision is what you will look for
“Misc”This is a delete you have not committed to
The pattern in every findable title is the same: it contains the words you would use before you knew the answer, not the words you used once you did. That is the same constraint the `web` fundamentals cards were rewritten to satisfy — describe the symptom, because the symptom is what the person searching actually has.
Practice
Linking Beats Filing, and the Search Test Beats Both
Filing puts a note in one place and requires you to remember which. Linking puts it in as many contexts as you like and, more usefully, surfaces notes you had forgotten while you are reading something adjacent — which is the failure mode filing cannot address.
HabitWhat it buys
Link from a note to related notes as you writeRediscovery. You meet old notes while writing new ones
Keep a small number of index notesAn entry point per area, curated by hand. Five of these beat five hundred tags
The search testAfter writing, search for what you would have typed in six months. If it does not come up, fix the title
Prune on read, not on a scheduleA tidy-up session never happens. Deleting one thing while you are already there does
Record the why, not only the whatThe command is on the internet. The reason you chose it is not
The search test is the whole card in one habit and costs five seconds: before closing a note, search for the phrase you would use to find it later. It catches the title that made sense while the context was fresh, which is the single most common reason a reference note is never seen again.
