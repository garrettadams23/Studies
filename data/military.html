🔢 Functional Numbers 1 through 9 — What Each Number Means ALL NUMBERS ▶
Reference — Universal Functional Areas
Numbers are consistent across ALL prefix letters
Regardless of whether the prefix is J, G, S, N, or A — the number always means the same functional area. A J3 and an S3 both handle Operations — just at different command levels.
1
Manpower & Personnel
Human resources, strength reporting, assignments, promotions, awards, disciplinary actions, casualty reporting, morale and welfare. The "HR department" of a military headquarters. J1 · G1 · S1 · N1 · A1
2
Intelligence
All-source intelligence: HUMINT, SIGINT, GEOINT, OSINT (Open Source Intelligence), MASINT. Threat assessments, enemy order of battle, ISR (Intelligence, Surveillance, Reconnaissance), targeting. J2 · G2 · S2 · N2 · A2
3
Operations
Current and ongoing operations — the main effort of any HQ. Battle rhythm, OPORD execution, fires coordination, battle tracking, exercises, and training management. The largest and busiest staff section. J3 · G3 · S3 · N3 · A3
4
Logistics & Supply
Sustainment: supply (Class I–IX), maintenance, transportation, field services, mortuary affairs, contracting. Ensures the force has what it needs to fight and survive. J4 · G4 · S4 · N4 · A4
5
Plans & Strategy
Future operations planning — from 96 hours out through long-range strategy. Operational plans (OPLANs), concept development, war gaming, campaign design, interagency coordination. Distinct from J3 (current ops). J5 · G5 · S5 · N5 · A5
6
Communications, Signal & IT
All networks, radios, satellite comms, data links, IT infrastructure, cybersecurity, COMSEC (communications security), and information management. The military's IT department. J6 · G6 · S6 · N6 · A6
7
Training & Force Development
Exercise planning, readiness reporting, individual and collective training programs, force generation, leader development. Air Force A7 = installations & mission support. Usage varies by branch. J7 · G7 · A7
8
Financial Management & Resources
Budgeting, resource allocation, comptroller functions, program objective memoranda (POM), contracts oversight, cost analysis, financial reporting to higher commands. J8 · G8 · N8 · A8
9
Civil Affairs & Interagency
Civil-military operations, host-nation relations, NGO/IGO coordination, reconstruction, PSYOP (Psychological Operations) coordination, USAID partnerships, governance support in conflict zones. J9 · G9 · S9 · A9
🔭 Sub-Designators — The Third Digit (e.g., J63) BRANCH LEVEL ▶
Reference — Sub-Sections Within Directorates
Third Digit = Branch Within the Functional Directorate
Large headquarters (combatant commands, service HQs) further divide each directorate into branches. The third digit follows the same 1–9 logic: 1=Manpower, 2=Intel, 3=Ops, 4=Logistics within that function. Smaller units (battalions, brigades) rarely use sub-designators.

⚖️ Branch Comparison — Same Number, Different Context SIDE-BY-SIDE ▶
Cross-Branch Reference
How Each Branch Uses the Same Numbers Differently
The number means the same function, but the scope, size, and tools differ dramatically between a battalion S-section and a combatant command J-directorate.
▸ FUNCTION 1 — PERSONNEL
Code Level Focus & Scope
S1 Battalion (400–1000 personnel) Leave requests, promotions, awards, strength reports, casualty notification for the battalion. Usually 2–4 people.
G1 Division (10,000–20,000 personnel) Same functions but manages policy across multiple brigades. Interfaces with Army HRC (Human Resources Command).
J1 Combatant Command (multi-service, global) Manages manpower for all services assigned to the command; joint manning documents; handles inter-service personnel policy conflicts.
N1 Navy HQ Total force management for all Navy personnel: recruiting, retention, career development, diversity programs.
A1 Headquarters Air Force Manpower & personnel for the entire Air Force: force structure decisions, officer/enlisted development, Air Force Personnel Center (AFPC) oversight.
▸ FUNCTION 3 — OPERATIONS (THE MAIN EFFORT)
Code Level Focus & Scope
S3 Battalion Plans and supervises all battalion training and combat operations. Runs the battalion Tactical Operations Center (TOC). Usually a Major.
G3 Division Synchronizes maneuver, fires, aviation, and combat support across the division. Manages the Division Main CP battle rhythm.
J3 Combatant Command Executes the commander's operational concept across all services globally. Runs the 24/7 Joint Operations Center (JOC). Often a 2-star billet.
N3 Navy Fleet Fleet operational employment; ship deployment scheduling; current fleet operations and readiness.
A3 Air Force Largest Air Force staff directorate. Oversees air operations, flying hour programs, readiness, and airpower employment globally.
▸ FUNCTION 6 — COMMUNICATIONS & IT
Code Level Focus & Scope
S6 Battalion Maintains radios (SINCGARS, SOCOM radios), tactical comms, NIPR/SIPR terminal at battalion level. Often a Warrant Officer or Captain.
G6 Division Division network (Tactical LAN), frequency management, satellite terminals, Signal Battalion oversight.
J6 Combatant Command Enterprise-level network operations. Manages the Joint Information Environment (JIE); classified and unclassified networks; cyber integration with USCYBERCOM.
N6 Navy Navy enterprise networks (NMCI/ONE-Net), shipboard comms systems, spectrum management, Navy COMSEC program.
A6 Air Force AFNET, Air Force IT infrastructure, C2 (Command and Control) systems, spectrum, cyberspace capabilities integration into air operations.

🧒Reading Military Codes (Beginner)MIL • Start Here▶
BIG IDEA
Why the Letters and Numbers?
Militaries need to communicate fast and without confusion. So they standardize: a letter says which level/branch a staff section is, a number says its job, and a phonetic alphabet keeps spoken letters from being misheard over a noisy radio.
DECODE IT
Letter + Number
Read the letter, then the number. The letter is the echelon/service (S, G, J…), the number is the function (1 = people, 2 = intel, 3 = operations…). So J2 is Joint Intelligence and S4 is unit-level Logistics.
SPELL IT OUT
NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) Phonetic Alphabet
To avoid 'B' vs 'D' mix-ups, each letter gets a word: Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta… 'CAT' becomes 'Charlie-Alpha-Tango.' Pilots, police, and IT support all borrow this trick for spelling things aloud.
CLOCK
24-Hour (Military) Time
No AM/PM confusion: the day runs 0000 to 2359. 1:00 PM is 1300 ('thirteen hundred'). Subtract 12 from afternoon hours to get back to a 12-hour clock.

🎖️Ranks & Echelons (Very Basic)MIL • Start Here▶
THREE GROUPS
Enlisted, NCO, Officer
Most militaries have three broad tiers. Enlisted are the trained doers; NCOs (non-commissioned officers, like sergeants) are experienced leaders risen from the ranks; officers are commissioned leaders who plan and command. NCOs are often called ‘the backbone.’
SIZE LADDER
From Squad to Division
Units nest like Russian dolls, each led by a more senior person: a handful of troops form a squad, squads form a platoon, platoons a company, and so on up. Bigger unit = higher-ranking commander.

📻 Military Communication Standards — Clear, Concise, Correct MIL • Foundation ▶
WHY MILITARY COMMS
When Miscommunication Costs Lives
Military communication standards were designed for high-stress, noisy, partial-information environments where a misunderstood message can have catastrophic consequences. These principles apply directly to IT operations, incident response, and emergency management — anywhere clarity under pressure matters.
SALUTE REPORT
Size · Activity · Location · Unit · Time · Equipment
A SALUTE report is a standardized format for reporting enemy contact or suspicious activity. Gives responders exactly what they need, in the right order, nothing extra.
LetterFieldExample
SSize3 individuals
AActivityMoving northbound along the road
LLocationGrid 123456, 500m east of checkpoint
UUnit/UniformCivilian clothing, no visible insignia
TTime0830 local
EEquipmentArmed, backpacks, one vehicle
IT application: Use SALUTE-style structured reporting for security incidents — "3 systems affected, lateral movement detected, VLAN (Virtual Local Area Network) 10, identified at 14:32 UTC, using PSExec and Mimikatz."
SITREP
Situation Report — Periodic Status Update
A SITREP (Situation Report) is a standardized periodic update on current status. Key elements:
Current situation — what is happening right now
What has been accomplished — progress since last report
Next actions — what you're about to do
Issues/requests — what you need from higher command
Format matters: be concise, use the same structure every time, report at agreed intervals (even "no change" is information).

BREVITY IN ALL COMMS
Say It Once, Say It Right
Military communication demands brevity because radio time is shared, batteries die, and enemies can intercept. The principle: say everything necessary, nothing more. In IT: this same discipline makes incident bridge calls, escalation emails, and Slack channels dramatically more effective. State the problem, the impact, what you've tried, what you need — then stop talking.
🎯 OODA (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) Loop — Decision-Making Under Pressure MILITARY • Decision Frameworks ▶
WHAT IS THE OODA LOOP
The Framework Behind Fast, Good Decisions
Developed by Air Force Colonel John Boyd after studying why American pilots won more dogfights despite flying inferior aircraft in Korea. The answer: they could cycle through decisions faster. Boyd codified this as the OODA Loop — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. The fighter who gets inside the opponent's OODA loop wins. Directly applicable to incident response, red teaming, and any competitive situation.
THE FOUR PHASES
OODA — Breaking Down Each Stage
PhaseWhat HappensCybersecurity Application
ObserveGather information from the environment. Sensory data, signals, telemetry — raw uninterpreted facts.SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) alerts, network telemetry, threat intel feeds, user reports
OrientMake sense of what you observed. Filter through mental models, prior experience, cultural tradition, analysis. This is the most important phase — your worldview shapes everything.Apply threat knowledge (ATT&CK TTPs), understand adversary motivation, contextualize the alert
DecideChoose a course of action from available options based on your orientation.Contain vs. monitor, escalate vs. handle, which remediation path
ActExecute the decision. Then immediately begin observing again — it's a loop.Block IP (Internet Protocol), isolate host, patch, notify stakeholders
IT APPLICATION
Incident Response Through the OODA Lens
The fastest incident responders have fast OODA loops — not because they skip steps, but because their Orient phase is automatic from experience and training. This is why tabletop exercises, purple team exercises, and studying past incidents matter: they pre-load the Orient phase.
GoalHow to Achieve It
Speed up ObserveBetter tooling: more log sources, better SIEM, endpoint visibility (EDR)
Speed up OrientTraining, experience, runbooks, tabletop exercises, threat intel
Speed up DecidePre-approved playbooks, clear authority (who can approve isolation?), escalation paths
Speed up ActAutomation (SOAR), prepared scripts, pre-staged remediation tools
Disrupt attacker's loopDeception (honeypots), rapid changes (rotate credentials), unpredictable responses
📋 Commander's Intent — Empowering Action Without Micromanaging MILITARY • Leadership ▶
THE CONCEPT
Two Levels Up, Two Levels Down
In military doctrine, every order includes Commander's Intent — a clear statement of the end state (what does success look like?) and purpose (why are we doing this?). When the plan breaks down (and it always does — "no plan survives first contact with the enemy"), subordinates can make independent decisions that align with the intent rather than freezing to wait for orders.
The rule: know your commander's intent two levels up (why your boss's boss wants this), and ensure your subordinates know yours two levels down. This creates adaptive, decentralized execution.
IT APPLICATION
How Commander's Intent Applies to Tech Teams
Military ConceptIT/Tech Equivalent
Commander's IntentBusiness goal behind a project — "why are we building this?" Engineers who know the why make better tradeoff decisions when specs change
Plan breaks downRequirements change mid-sprint, production incident during deploy, API (Application Programming Interface) partner goes down
Decentralized executionOn-call engineers making incident decisions without waiting for management approval
Two levels upDevelopers understanding the business context; analysts understanding org risk tolerance

MISSION-TYPE ORDERS
Tell Them What and Why — Not Always How
The German military concept of Auftragstaktik (mission tactics): give subordinates the objective and constraints, then trust them to figure out the method. This produces faster, more adaptive results than prescriptive orders — because the person closest to the problem has the most relevant information.
Applied to IT: instead of "run these exact five commands in this order," a good manager says "we need the backup restored and the service healthy before 8am Monday — you have authority to make it happen." The engineer has context the manager doesn't.
📝 After-Action Review — Learning From Every Event MILITARY • Continuous Improvement ▶
WHAT IS AN AAR
The Army's Structured Debrief
The After-Action Review (AAR) is a U.S. Army practice for learning from any event — a mission, an exercise, a failure. It's a structured, honest discussion conducted immediately afterward while memories are fresh. The civilian tech world adopted it as the "post-mortem" or "retrospective." It's one of the highest-leverage habits a team can build: every incident becomes a lesson instead of just a scar.
THE FOUR QUESTIONS
The Core of Every AAR
QuestionPurpose
1. What was supposed to happen?Establish the plan/expectation
2. What actually happened?Establish the facts, honestly
3. Why was there a difference?Root cause analysis — the real learning
4. What do we do differently next time?Actionable improvements, assigned owners
THE BLAMELESS PRINCIPLE
Hunt for Causes, Not Culprits
The single most important rule: AARs (and post-mortems) must be blameless. The moment people fear punishment, they hide information — and you lose the lessons. The premise is that everyone acted reasonably given what they knew at the time. The goal is to fix the system (better tooling, clearer process, more training) that allowed the mistake, not to punish the person. This maps directly to IT incident post-mortems: when engineers can speak freely about what went wrong, the organization actually improves.
Blameless (Good)Blameful (Toxic)
"The deploy process let an untested change reach prod""Bob broke production"
"Our runbook was unclear about the rollback step""You should have known better"
People share mistakes openly → org learnsPeople hide mistakes → org repeats them
🧭 Troop Leading Procedures — A Framework for Planning Anything MILITARY • Planning ▶
THE 8 STEPS (BMNT)
How Small-Unit Leaders Plan and Prepare
Troop Leading Procedures (TLP) are the 8-step process small-unit leaders use to plan and execute a mission. The genius is in step 3 — issue a warning order early so the team can start preparing in parallel while you finish planning. This concept maps perfectly to running an IT project or incident response.
StepMilitaryIT/Project Equivalent
1. Receive the missionGet the order/taskReceive the ticket/project/incident
2. Issue a warning orderAlert the team early"Heads up team, big task coming — start prepping"
3. Make a tentative planInitial course of actionDraft approach, identify resources
4. Initiate movementStart positioningProvision environments, gather access
5. Conduct reconnaissanceRecon the terrainInvestigate the system/codebase/logs
6. Complete the planFinalize detailsFinalize the runbook/design
7. Issue the orderBrief the teamKickoff: assign roles, confirm understanding
8. Supervise & refineExecute, adjust, rehearseExecute, monitor, adapt as you learn
PARALLEL PLANNING
Don't Make Your Team Wait on You
The biggest lesson from TLP for civilian work: warning orders enable parallel work. A weak leader plans everything in silence, then dumps a finished plan on the team at the last minute. A strong leader gives early, partial information ("we'll be migrating the database this weekend — start reviewing the schema") so the team prepares simultaneously. This compresses timelines dramatically and respects your team's ability to contribute.
📡 PACE Planning — Redundancy by Design MILITARY • Resilience ▶
WHAT IS PACE
Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency
PACE is a military communications planning method: for any critical capability, you pre-plan four methods ranked by preference. If the Primary fails, you fall back to Alternate, then Contingency, then Emergency — no panic, no improvising, because the fallbacks were decided in advance. It's a discipline of building redundancy before you need it, and it maps perfectly to IT resilience planning.

PACE FOR IT
Apply It to Anything Critical
The brilliance of PACE is that it forces you to ask "what if this fails?" three times — before the crisis. During a major incident, your primary tools may be down (what if the outage takes out Slack and the monitoring dashboard?). A PACE plan means the team already knows where to go.

🎖️ Leadership — Styles, Mission Command & Cohesion MILITARY • Leadership ▶
LEADERSHIP STYLES
Match the Style to the Situation
There's no single "best" leadership style — effective leaders adapt to the situation, the people, and the stakes. The military teaches this explicitly, and it maps directly to leading technical teams and incidents.
StyleWhen It WorksIT Example
Directive (autocratic)Crisis, no time to debate, clear dangerActive incident — "isolate that host NOW"

The skill is the switch: a leader who is directive during an outage but participative in planning earns far more than one stuck in a single mode. Reading which the moment needs is the art.
MISSION COMMAND
Decentralized Execution Through Trust
Mission Command is the modern military's core leadership philosophy: leaders provide clear intent and the "why," then empower subordinates to use disciplined initiative to achieve it — without waiting for detailed orders. It rests on mutual trust, shared understanding, and accepting prudent risk. In fast-moving situations (combat or a production incident), the people closest to the problem must be able to act. Micromanagement is too slow and doesn't scale.

UNIT COHESION
What Makes Teams Perform Under Stress
The military invests heavily in cohesion because cohesive units perform dramatically better under pressure — and the research (e.g., Google's Project Aristotle) found the same for tech teams: the #1 predictor of high-performing teams is psychological safety — members feel safe to take risks, admit mistakes, and speak up. This connects directly to blameless post-mortems: people who fear punishment hide problems, and hidden problems compound. Leaders build cohesion by sharing hardship, being competent and fair, communicating honestly, and putting the team's welfare above their own ego. Take care of your people, and they'll take care of the mission.
Beginner OPSEC – Operational Security in Cyber & Real Life
Core Concept
What Is OPSEC?
OPSEC (Operational Security) originated in the military but now applies universally to anyone who needs to protect sensitive information or activities from adversaries.

The core question: "What information, if known by the wrong person, would allow them to harm me, my organisation, or my mission?"

The five-step OPSEC process:
1. Identify critical information — what do I need to protect? (credentials, plans, personnel, locations).
2. Analyse threats — who wants this information and why? (criminals, competitors, nation-states, insiders).
3. Analyse vulnerabilities — where could I accidentally expose this? (social media, email, public logs).
4. Assess risk — likelihood × impact for each vulnerability.
5. Apply countermeasures — reduce the vulnerability (encryption, access control, need-to-know, training).

"Not my circus, not my monkey" — if sensitive information belongs to your organisation, don't share it even with colleagues who don't need it. Need-to-know is a core OPSEC principle.
Digital OPSEC
Personal Digital Security Hygiene
Every IT professional is a target. Apply OPSEC to yourself:
PERSONAL OPSEC CHECKLIST ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── CREDENTIALS [ ] Use a password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password) — unique passwords everywhere [ ] Enable MFA on every account that supports it (TOTP app, not SMS) [ ] Never reuse passwords — one breach = one compromised account, not many COMMUNICATIONS [ ] Sensitive work: use encrypted email (ProtonMail) or end-to-end encrypted chat [ ] Assume corporate email is monitored and archived [ ] Don't discuss sensitive projects on public Slack workspaces / Discord SOCIAL MEDIA [ ] Don't post photos that reveal physical security details (badge readers, screens) [ ] Job posting recon: attackers read your LinkedIn to map tech stack [ ] Don't announce travel dates publicly — burglars and social engineers note it DEVICE SECURITY [ ] Full-disk encryption on laptops (BitLocker/FileVault/LUKS) [ ] Screen lock within 2 minutes of inactivity [ ] VPN on untrusted Wi-Fi (coffee shops, hotels, airports) [ ] Webcam cover when not in use WORK HABITS [ ] Clear desk policy — sensitive docs face down or locked away [ ] Shred documents before disposal; don't put them in recycling [ ] Be aware of shoulder surfing in public spaces [ ] Verify caller identity before providing information (voice phishing = vishing)
Org OPSEC
Protecting Sensitive Information at Scale
Organisational OPSEC controls applied in enterprise security:

• Information classification: Public / Internal / Confidential / Restricted — policies dictate how each is handled.
• Data Loss Prevention (DLP): software that blocks classified files from being emailed externally or uploaded to personal cloud storage.
• Privileged Access Workstations (PAW): a dedicated, hardened device used only for admin tasks — never for browsing or email.
• Air-gapped networks: classified systems with no internet connection; data moves via signed, approved media only.
• Red team deception: honeytokens — fake credentials or files planted in your system; if accessed, you know an attacker has them.

Mindset
"Assume Breach" – The Modern Security Posture
"Assume" makes an ass out of you and me — but in security, the one assumption you should make is that you have already been breached.

The Assume Breach philosophy:
• Design systems as if an attacker already has a foothold — use micro-segmentation, least privilege, and mutual TLS (Transport Layer Security) between services.
• Invest in detection and response as much as prevention — because prevention eventually fails.
• Run red-team exercises to find gaps before real attackers do.
• Know your mean time to detect (MTTD) and mean time to respond (MTTR) — measure and improve them continuously.

You can't make someone make the right choice, yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards — an attacker who got in through a phishing email is not your fault; the speed and quality of your response is entirely within your control.
Beginner Leadership – Military Principles Applied to IT Teams
Core Concept
Why Military Leadership Models Translate
Military leadership doctrine has been refined over centuries under the highest-stakes conditions imaginable — and a surprising amount of it maps directly onto leading an IT team through an outage, a project crunch, or simply day-to-day operations.

Both environments share key features: high-pressure decisions with incomplete information, the need to coordinate people who can't all see the whole picture, and consequences that compound when communication breaks down.

You don't need a title to lead — the engineer who keeps a calm head during an outage and gets the team organised is leading, regardless of what the org chart says.
Commander's Intent
"Commander's Intent" – Goals Over Scripts
In the military, a leader gives Commander's Intent: a clear statement of the end goal and the "why" — not a rigid step-by-step script. This way, when the situation changes (and it always does), subordinates can adapt without waiting for new orders.

In IT, this looks like:
Bad delegation: "Run these five exact commands in this order."
Good delegation: "We need this service back online with zero data loss — here's what I know about the failure. Use your judgment; call me if you hit something I haven't covered."

Good intent-based delegation does two things: it grows your team's capability, and it means the mission survives even if the leader is unreachable — exactly when it matters most (3 AM incidents).
After-Action Review
The After-Action Review (AAR) Process
The military's After-Action Review is the direct ancestor of the modern "blameless postmortem." Four simple questions, asked after every significant event:
THE AFTER-ACTION REVIEW — FOUR QUESTIONS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────── 1. WHAT WAS SUPPOSED TO HAPPEN? "The deploy was supposed to complete in 10 minutes with zero downtime." 2. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED? "The deploy took 47 minutes and caused a 12-minute outage." 3. WHY WAS THERE A DIFFERENCE? "The database migration locked a table longer than expected because the table had grown 3x since the migration was last tested." 4. WHAT WILL WE DO DIFFERENTLY NEXT TIME? "Test migrations against a copy of production-sized data. Add a pre-flight check that estimates migration duration. Add an automatic abort if a migration runs longer than 5 minutes." RULES THAT MAKE IT WORK: - No rank in the room — the newest hire's observation counts as much as the senior engineer's - Focus on the SYSTEM, not the PERSON ("the process allowed this" not "Dave messed up") - Write it down — verbal lessons evaporate; written ones compound
Mission Mindset
Putting It All Together — Leading Through Chaos
These three phrases — central to this entire guide — are themselves leadership principles refined into short, memorable forms (the way good military doctrine often is):

"Not my circus, not my monkey" — a leader protects their team's focus. Shield people from drama and scope creep that doesn't serve the mission; redirect it to whoever owns it.

"Assume makes an ass out of you and me" — a leader verifies before committing resources. "I think the backup ran" is not the same as "I confirmed the backup completed and is restorable."

"You can't make someone make the right choice, yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards" — a leader's real value often shows after something goes wrong: staying calm, organising the recovery, protecting the team from blame spirals, and turning the failure into the team's next strength.

Master these three, lead with clear intent, and run honest AARs — and you'll out-perform leaders with twice your technical knowledge but none of this foundation.
Beginner Translating Military Experience into IT Resume Language
Why this matters

The translation gap is real — and fixable

A hiring manager reading "Maintained communications equipment for a forward-deployed unit under field conditions" may not immediately connect that to "can troubleshoot hardware, manage inventory, and keep systems running with no backup support nearby." The skills transfer; the vocabulary doesn't, automatically. Translating your experience into civilian IT language isn't dishonesty or exaggeration — it's making sure the reader understands what you actually did.

Concept

Side-by-side: military experience to IT resume language

What you didHow it translates
Stood watch / monitored systems on shift rotations"Performed continuous system monitoring and incident triage across rotating shift schedules — directly applicable to SOC/NOC environments"
Maintained or repaired communications/radio/radar equipment"Diagnosed and resolved hardware faults under time pressure with limited resources — comparable to field/desktop support and infrastructure troubleshooting"

Concept

Lead with impact, not just duties

Civilian resumes reward quantified outcomes over lists of responsibilities. Compare:

# Weak — describes a duty, says nothing about the outcome
"Responsible for maintaining unit IT equipment."

# Strong — same underlying experience, framed around impact
"Maintained 40+ workstations and network devices supporting a 120-person
unit with zero unplanned downtime over a 12-month deployment, reducing
average ticket resolution time by replacing ad-hoc fixes with a
documented troubleshooting checklist."

If you don't have exact numbers, reasonable estimates are fine — "approximately," "more than," and "supported a team of roughly X" all read as honest and specific. What recruiters are scanning for is scale and outcome, not decimal-point precision.

Mindset

Not my circus, not my monkey — but also, it's now your circus

Military culture often instills "stay in your lane, don't volunteer for extra work" as survival wisdom — and that instinct can undersell you in interviews. The civilian IT world rewards people who *did* step outside their narrow job description: the radio tech who also fixed everyone's laptop issues, the supply clerk who built a spreadsheet that automated half the unit's paperwork. Those moments where you picked up something that "wasn't your job" are often the most resume-worthy stories you have. Don't bury them out of habit.

Beginner Using the GI Bill and Tuition Assistance for IT Certifications
Why this matters

Education benefits are some of the best-kept "secrets" in the military community

Many service members and veterans leave significant education benefits unused — not from lack of eligibility, but from not knowing the programs exist, assuming the paperwork is too complicated, or believing (often incorrectly) that the benefit only covers traditional four-year degrees. IT certifications are frequently fundable through these same programs, and stacking several certs can be a faster, cheaper path into the field than a full degree program — especially if your goal is to start working sooner rather than later.

Concept

The major programs, at a glance

ProgramWho it's generally forWhat it can cover
Post-9/11 GI BillVeterans and some active-duty members meeting service requirementsTuition, housing allowance (BAH), and — important for this audience — licensing & certification exam reimbursement
Tuition Assistance (TA)Active-duty service members, by branch programCourses and, in some programs, certification training while still serving
VR&E (Chapter 31)Veterans with a service-connected disability affecting employabilityTraining, certification costs, and career counseling support — often more comprehensive than people expect
State veteran education benefitsVaries — many states offer additional programs on top of federal benefitsVaries by state; worth checking your state's department of veterans affairs directly

Note specifically: the Post-9/11 GI Bill's licensing and certification reimbursement is separate from its tuition coverage and is often overlooked — it can directly fund the exam fee for things like CompTIA (Computing Technology Industry Association) Security+, AWS (Amazon Web Services) certifications, or Cisco credentials, without needing to be enrolled in a degree program at all.

Hands-on

A practical first-steps checklist

# 1. Confirm your eligibility and remaining benefit balance
#    — VA.gov and your installation's education center are the
#    authoritative sources; don't rely on secondhand information

# 2. Ask SPECIFICALLY about certification/licensing reimbursement
#    — this is frequently a different process than degree-program
#    tuition, with its own forms and approval steps

# 3. Confirm the certification you want is on an approved list
#    — most IT vendor certs (CompTIA, AWS, Microsoft, Cisco) are
#    commonly approved, but verify with your education office BEFORE
#    you pay out of pocket and try to get reimbursed after the fact

# 4. Keep every receipt, confirmation email, and exam voucher
#    — reimbursement claims move much faster with complete
#    documentation than with "I'm pretty sure I paid for that"

# 5. Talk to your installation's education office or a
#    Veteran Service Organization (VSO) — they exist specifically
#    to help you navigate this, and the help is free
Mindset

Assume makes an ass out of you and me — verify your specific situation

Benefit rules change, vary by branch, by discharge status, by years of service, and by which specific program you're using — what worked for a friend who left the service three years ago may not apply to you today. Don't assume you're ineligible because of something you heard secondhand, and don't assume a benefit covers something without confirming it in writing first. A 20-minute conversation with an education counselor or VSO representative can save you from either missing out on something you qualified for, or getting an unpleasant reimbursement surprise after the fact.

Beginner Networking Events Without the Awkwardness – A Veteran's Field Guide
Why this matters

"Networking" sounds fake until it gets you a job

Plenty of capable people — veterans especially, coming from a culture that often values humility and "letting your work speak for itself" — find the idea of "networking" deeply uncomfortable, even slightly dishonest-feeling. Reframe it: networking is just having genuine conversations with people who do interesting work, and staying in touch. The job-finding part is a side effect of doing that consistently — not the awkward main event it's often made out to be.

Concept

Where IT networking actually happens (most of it isn't fancy galas)

VenueWhat it actually looks likeWhy it works
Local user groups / meetupsA monthly gathering of people who use the same tools (Linux, AWS, cybersecurity) — usually free, usually casualLow-pressure, recurring — you become a familiar face over a few months without ever "selling" yourself
Veteran-focused tech programsOrganizations specifically built to connect transitioning service members with IT employers and mentorsEveryone there already understands your background — no translation needed, no awkward "so, tell me about your service" small talk
Online communitiesSubreddits, Discord servers, LinkedIn groups focused on specific certifications or technologiesLower social pressure than in-person events — you can build relationships entirely on the strength of your questions and contributions
Certification study groupsPeople preparing for the same exam, often meeting weekly to quiz each otherBuilt-in shared goal — conversation starts itself, friendships form naturally around mutual struggle and progress
Hands-on

A simple script for your first conversation (genuinely, not cynically)

# The goal of a first conversation is NOT "get a job out of this person."
# It's "have one genuine, useful exchange." That's it. Everything
# else follows from doing that consistently, many times, over months.

# An opener that works because it's true and specific:
"Hey, I'm transitioning out of [your role] and getting into IT —
 working through my Security+ right now. What got you started
 in this field?"

# A good follow-up that shows you're actually listening:
"That's interesting — how is that different day-to-day from
 what I'd expect coming from [your background]?"

# The single most useful thing you can do afterward — not
# during the conversation, but within 48 hours of it:
"Hey [name], really enjoyed talking about [specific thing they
 said] — wanted to say thanks for the perspective. I'll let you
 know how the Security+ exam goes!"

# That last message is the entire "secret" — most people never
# follow up at all, which means simply doing it puts you ahead
# of nearly everyone else they met that night.
Mindset

Not my circus, not my monkey — but this circus is worth joining

It's tempting to think "I'll network once I actually have something impressive to show" — but that backwards thinking keeps a lot of talented people invisible right when visibility would help them most. The people who'll eventually want to help you get hired are the same people you'd be meeting now, while you're still learning. Showing up consistently while you're a beginner — asking good questions, being genuinely curious, following up — builds the exact relationships that turn into opportunities later. You don't need to be impressive yet. You need to be present, and real.
