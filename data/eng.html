📈 Scalability 101 — Vertical vs Horizontal System Design ▶
Concept
Two Ways to Handle More Load
Vertical scaling (scale up) means a bigger machine — more CPU/RAM. Simple, but there's a ceiling and a single point of failure. Horizontal scaling (scale out) means more machines behind a load balancer — effectively unlimited and fault-tolerant, but only if your app is designed for it. The design requirement is statelessness: any server can handle any request, so session/state lives in a shared store (Redis, DB), not in one server's memory.
Vertical (up)Horizontal (out)
HowBigger boxMore boxes
CeilingHardware limit~Unlimited
FailureSingle pointRedundant
ComplexityLowNeeds LB + stateless design
Real systems do both: scale up the database (hard to shard) and scale out the stateless app tier. Shared-nothing architecture — nodes share no memory/disk — is what makes horizontal scaling clean.
The Decision
Scale Up Until It Hurts — Horizontal Isn't Free, and It's Usually About Failure, Not Throughput
“Scale horizontally” is the reflexive answer, and it skips the cheaper option. A bigger machine is dramatically simpler because it avoids the entire distributed-systems tax — no consensus, no partial failure, no data-locality puzzles — and modern hardware is enormous: hundreds of cores and terabytes of RAM (Random Access Memory) on one box. Horizontal scaling buys an effectively unlimited ceiling and fault tolerance, but it charges every distributed-systems problem in return. So the honest order is scale up until it genuinely hurts — which takes longer than people expect — then scale out. And notice the real reason you usually go horizontal: not that one machine can't keep up, but that one machine is a single point of failure. Redundancy, not raw capacity, most often forces the move.
Reach for…When
A bigger box (scale up)The default first move — simplest, no distributed tax, a lot of runway on modern hardware
More boxes (scale out)You need fault tolerance/redundancy, or have genuinely outgrown one machine
BothScale the stateless app tier out; scale the hard-to-shard database up
So statelessness is the enabling design, but the sequencing is the judgement: exhaust the simple single-machine option before taking on the operational cost of a fleet, and be honest about why you are going horizontal. If the answer is “for redundancy,” you need at least two — not necessarily many; if it is “one box can't cope,” make sure that is true before you pay the distributed-systems tax to fix it.
⚖️ Load Balancing & Sharding System Design ▶
Concept
Spreading Requests, Then Spreading Data
A load balancer spreads incoming requests across servers; sharding spreads the data across databases when one can't hold or serve it all. Both are "divide the work," at different layers.
▸ LOAD-BALANCING ALGORITHMS
AlgorithmSends request to…
Round robinEach server in turn
Least connectionsThe least-busy server
WeightedBigger servers get more
IP (Internet Protocol) hash / stickySame client → same server
▸ SHARDING STRATEGIES
StrategySplit byWatch out
RangeKey ranges (A–M, N–Z)Hot ranges
Hashhash(key) % NResharding pain
Consistent hashingRing of hashesAdding nodes moves few keys — the standard
The enemy is the hot shard — one key or range getting disproportionate traffic (a celebrity user). Choose a shard key with even distribution and no cross-shard joins on the hot path.
The Decision
The Shard Key Is a One-Way Door; the Load Balancer's Real Job Isn't the Algorithm
Two corrections to where attention usually goes. On load balancing, teams debate the algorithm when round-robin is fine for most workloads; the parts that actually cause outages are health checks and connection draining. A balancer with a bad health check keeps routing to a dead node; one without draining kills in-flight requests on every deploy. Those, not the algorithm, are what page you. On sharding, the shard key is close to irreversible: pick it wrong and you are re-sharding a live system — the hardest migration there is — and once sharded you lose cross-shard transactions and joins, so the data model must avoid them on the hot path. Sharding is a one-way door; walk through it as late as you can.
Where attention goesWhere it should go
The load-balancing algorithmHealth checks (route away from sick nodes) and draining (finish in-flight on deploy)
Sticky sessions for convenienceStatelessness, so any node serves any request — sticky is a scaling ceiling
Picking a shard strategyPicking a shard key with even distribution and no cross-shard joins on the hot path
Sharding early “to be safe”Sharding as late as possible — a one-way door and a brutal live migration
So spread requests with the simplest algorithm plus real health checks and draining, and treat the shard key as the expensive, near-permanent decision it is: defer sharding until a single scaled-up database genuinely cannot cope, and when you must, choose the key as if you can never change it — because in practice you almost can't.
🧮 Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation System Design ▶
Reference
Numbers Every Engineer Should Know
Before designing, sanity-check scale with rough math. Knowing that memory is ~1000× faster than disk, and a cross-region round trip is ~150 ms, tells you where the bottleneck will be without building anything.
▸ LATENCY NUMBERS (orders of magnitude)
Operation~Time
L1 cache reference~1 ns

SSD (Solid State Drive) random read~100 µs

▸ CAPACITY MATH (worked)
1M daily active users, 10 requests/user/day = 10M req/day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ ~115 req/s average. Peak is typically 2–3× average → design for ~300 req/s. Storage: 10M writes/day × 1 KB × 365 × 5 yr ≈ ~18 TB. These two numbers (QPS + storage growth) drive most of the architecture.
The artefact
One Worked Calculation, All the Way Through
The technique is only convincing when you watch it happen. Question: can we store a year of events for 5 million users? Nothing here is precise, and precision is not what the calculation is for — it is for deciding whether the answer is “obviously fine”, “obviously not”, or “we need to think”.
StepWorking
1. Events per user per dayGuess 20. Round numbers only — 20, not 17
2. Events per day5M × 20 = 100M/day
3. Per second, average100M / 86,400 ≈ 1,200/s. Peak is 3–5× average, so ~5,000/s
4. Size per event~1 KB with indexes. So 100 GB/day, ~36 TB/year
5. The verdict36 TB is not “one Postgres table”. It is fine as partitioned cold storage or a warehouse, and 5,000 writes/s needs a real design
Two habits make this fast enough to do in a meeting. Memorise a handful of anchors — 86,400 seconds in a day, ~105 for rough work, 1 KB per small row, 1 MB per second is ~2.5 TB a month — and round aggressively and say you are rounding. The output is never the number; it is which of the three verdicts you are in, and that conclusion is robust to being wrong by a factor of two.
🛟 Designing for Failure System Design ▶
Concept
Everything Fails — Plan for It
At scale, hardware dies, networks partition, and dependencies time out constantly. Robust systems assume failure and stay up anyway. The core moves: eliminate single points of failure, contain damage, and degrade gracefully instead of collapsing.
TechniqueWhat It Buys
Redundancy (N+1)No single point of failure — replicate across AZs (Availability Zone)
Health checks + failoverRoute away from dead instances automatically
BulkheadsIsolate resources so one overloaded feature can't sink the rest
Graceful degradationServe a cached/limited response instead of an error (show stale prices, hide recommendations)
Blast-radius limitsCell-based / sharded design so an outage hits 5% of users, not 100%
Pair with the resilience patterns (circuit breaker, retry-with-backoff, timeout) at the call level. The mindset: "what happens when this dependency is down?" should have an answer for every dependency.

“Design for failure” is agreed with universally and practised rarely, because it is stated as a principle rather than an exercise. The exercise takes twenty minutes and works on one dependency at a time. 🧩 The System Design Interview — A Framework Career ▶
Reference
A Repeatable Structure for an Open-Ended Question
"Design Twitter" has no single right answer — interviewers assess how you think. A structure keeps you from freezing and shows senior judgment: drive requirements, estimate, sketch, then go deep where it matters.
StepDo
1. ClarifyNail functional + non-functional requirements. Who uses it? Read/write ratio? Consistency vs availability?
2. EstimateQPS (Queries Per Second), storage, bandwidth — back-of-envelope (previous card). Sets the scale.
3. API (Application Programming Interface)Define the core endpoints/contract — forces concreteness.
4. Data modelEntities, access patterns → SQL (Structured Query Language) vs NoSQL choice.
5. High-level designBoxes & arrows: clients → LB → services → DB/cache/queue.
6. Scale & deepenFind bottlenecks; add caching, sharding, replicas, CDN (Content Delivery Network), async queues.
7. Trade-offsName what you gave up. There is no perfect design — only fit-for-purpose.
The senior signal is talking trade-offs out loud and tying decisions back to the requirements — not memorizing one "correct" architecture.
The Fingerprint
Most Candidates Fail the Same Way — Designing the Wrong System, Beautifully
The framework tells you the steps; the failures are where interviews are actually lost, and they are the same three every time. The biggest: diving into components before scoping the requirements — you architect a gorgeous system for a problem the interviewer didn't ask about, because you never nailed the read/write ratio, the scale, or the one feature that matters. Second: going quiet — the interview measures how you think, so a candidate who designs silently and presents a finished diagram has hidden the entire thing being assessed. Third: over-engineering — reaching for sharding, microservices and multi-region on a system a single database would serve, which reads as inexperience, not sophistication. The through-line: the interview simulates the real job's first hour — a vague ask you must scope before you build — so failing it is failing the actual skill, not a trick.
The failureWhat it looks likeThe fix
Design before scopingA beautiful system for the wrong requirementsSpend real time on step 1; restate the problem back
Going silentA finished diagram, no visible reasoningNarrate every decision and its alternative
Over-engineeringSharding/microservices for a small stated scaleLet the estimate justify each addition
No trade-offs named“This is the design,” no cost statedEnd every choice with what it gave up
So the framework is necessary but the discipline is what's scored: scope before you build, think out loud so the thinking is visible, and let the numbers — not habit — justify each piece of machinery you add. The candidate who says “at this scale a single Postgres is fine, here's when I'd shard” beats the one who reaches for Cassandra on slide one, because naming the trade-off is the seniority, and grabbing the biggest tool is its absence.
🏗️ Monolith vs Microservices vs Modular Monolith Architecture ▶
Concept
The Most Consequential Architecture Choice
A monolith is one deployable unit — simple to build, test, and deploy, but hard to scale teams and selectively scale parts. Microservices split the system into independently deployable services — great for large orgs and independent scaling, but you trade code complexity for operational complexity (networking, data consistency, observability). The modular monolith is the underrated middle: one deployable, but with strict internal module boundaries — so you get simplicity now and can extract services later if you actually need to.
MonolithMicroservices
DeployOne unitMany, independent
ScaleWhole appPer service
Team fitSmall teamsMany teams
DataOne DB, easy joins/txnsDB-per-service, distributed txns
FailureAll-or-nothingIsolated (if done right)
Ops costLowHigh
Default advice: start with a (modular) monolith. Microservices solve organizational scaling problems you probably don't have yet — adopting them too early buys distributed-systems pain for no benefit.
The bill
The Threshold Is Team Count, Not Request Rate
Microservices solve an organisational problem that presents as a technical one: too many people editing the same deployable, blocking each other on releases. Below that pain there is nothing to buy, and the bill arrives regardless — network calls that used to be function calls, and every failure mode that comes with them.
SignalWhat it actually means
One team, any traffic levelStay a monolith. Scale it vertically and horizontally; that is far cheaper than a network
Several teams, one release trainThis is the real threshold — the cost is people waiting, and splitting buys deployment independence
Services that deploy togetherA distributed monolith: every cost of the split, none of the independence
Shared database between servicesNot a split. The schema is the coupling, and the coupling was the problem
One component with a wildly different loadExtract that one. A single carved-out service is not microservices
The honest default for most teams is a modular monolith: enforce module boundaries inside one deployable, with no shared tables across modules, and split later along a seam you have already proved. It gets you Conway-friendly ownership without paying for distributed transactions, service discovery, and a tracing stack you do not yet need.
📡 Event-Driven Architecture Architecture ▶
Concept
React to Facts, Don't Call in Lockstep
Instead of service A calling B calling C, components emit events ("OrderPlaced") to a broker, and interested services react. This decouples producers from consumers: add a new consumer (email, analytics, fraud) without touching the producer. It's the architecture behind responsive, extensible systems — at the cost of harder debugging and eventual consistency.
StyleHowTrade-off
ChoreographyEach service reacts to events independentlyLoose coupling; flow is emergent/hard to trace
OrchestrationA coordinator directs the stepsClear flow; the orchestrator is a coupling point
Watch for: event ordering, duplicate delivery (make consumers idempotent), and schema evolution (version your events). Pairs with the Message Queues topic and CQRS/Event Sourcing below.
The bill
You Trade the Stack Trace for a Correlation ID
Synchronous code hands you the whole causal chain in one stack trace. Publish an event and that goes away: the producer does not know who consumed it, the consumer does not know what caused it, and “why did this customer get two emails” becomes an archaeology project across services, retries and a broker's redelivery semantics.
What you loseWhat has to replace it
The stack traceDistributed tracing, with trace context propagated through every broker hop
Knowing who consumes whatA schema registry or catalogue — otherwise nobody can safely change an event
Exactly-once deliveryConsumers must be idempotent. Most brokers are at-least-once whatever the marketing says
OrderingGuaranteed only within a partition — so the partition key is a design decision, not a default
One place to see the whole flowA diagram somebody maintains, or the flow lives only in four people's heads
Adopt it where the decoupling is the point — fan-out to many consumers, absorbing load spikes, integrating systems on different release cycles. Do not adopt it to make a request/response call asynchronous while the caller still waits for the answer: that is the same coupling with a broker in the middle, and now the failure is invisible.
⬡ Clean / Hexagonal Architecture Architecture ▶
Concept
Keep Business Logic Independent of Frameworks
Clean Architecture (and its cousin Hexagonal / Ports & Adapters) organizes code so your business logic sits at the center, knowing nothing about the database, web framework, or UI (User Interface). Those outer concerns are adapters that plug into ports (interfaces) the core defines. Swap Postgres for Mongo, REST (Representational State Transfer) for gRPC, without touching the core.
The key rule is the dependency rule: dependencies point inward. The domain never imports the framework; the framework imports the domain. Concretely: the core defines a UserRepository interface; a PostgresUserRepository in the outer layer implements it. This makes the core trivially unit-testable (mock the ports) and resistant to framework churn — the business rules outlive the tech stack.
LayerContains
Entities / DomainCore business rules (no dependencies)
Use casesApplication-specific orchestration
AdaptersDB, web, external APIs (Application Programming Interface) — the replaceable edge
The bill
The Adapter You Never Swap Is Pure Overhead
Ports and adapters buy one thing: replacing an outside dependency without touching the core. The bill is paid on every feature, forever — an interface, an implementation, a mapper between the domain type and the outside type, and a test double. If the swap never happens, that is the full price for a benefit you did not collect.
QuestionHonest answer, most of the time
Will we change the database?No. Teams say yes and mean it, and it is still no ten years later
Will we change the web framework?Rarely, and when it happens it is a rewrite anyway
Will we swap the payment provider?Often yes — this is where a port genuinely pays
Can we test the core without I/O (Input/Output)?This is the real benefit, and it arrives immediately rather than someday
How many files does one new field touch?Count it. Four or five is normal, and that number is the thing to weigh
Put ports where the outside world is genuinely uncertain — payment providers, notification channels, third-party APIs whose contracts you do not control — and let the database be the database. Fast, I/O-free tests of the core are the benefit that pays from day one; swappability is a lottery ticket, and it is worth buying only where you can name the alternative you might swap to.
✂️ CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) & Event Sourcing Architecture ▶
Concept
Split Reads from Writes; Store Events, Not State
CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) uses separate models for writing (commands) and reading (queries) — so each can be optimized and scaled independently (e.g. a normalized write DB + denormalized read replicas). Event Sourcing goes further: instead of storing current state, store the full sequence of events that produced it. Current state is a replay of the log.
PatternGainCost
CQRSIndependent read/write scaling & modelsTwo models to keep in sync (eventual consistency)
Event SourcingFull audit trail; time-travel; rebuild any viewComplexity; schema/versioning; snapshots for speed
Powerful together, but not free — they add real complexity. Use them where audit, temporal queries, or extreme read/write asymmetry genuinely demand it (banking, ledgers), not by default.
The bill
An Append-Only Log Is a Promise You Cannot Take Back
Event sourcing's appeal is that nothing is ever lost. Its bill is the same sentence read the other way: nothing can ever be removed. A bad event, a personal detail written by mistake, a schema you now regret — all permanent by design, and all your problem for the lifetime of the system.
BillWhat it costs in practice
Events are immutableFixing data means a compensating event; there is no UPDATE and no quiet cleanup
Event schemas evolveYou must read every version you ever wrote, forever, or migrate the whole history
Erasure requests“Delete my data” against an immutable log needs crypto-shredding or a redaction design, chosen up front
Rebuilding projectionsReplay time grows with history — snapshotting stops being optional around year three
Eventual consistency is visibleThe user saves and does not see it. That is a product decision, not an implementation detail
CQRS without event sourcing is a much smaller trade and is often the whole win: separate read and write models, keep a normal database. Reach for the log itself when the history is the product — ledgers, audit, anything where “how did we arrive at this balance” is a question somebody will ask under oath. If nobody will ask, you have bought permanence and only paid for it.
1️⃣2️⃣ The Twelve-Factor App Architecture ▶
Reference
The Checklist for Cloud-Native Services
The Twelve-Factor methodology is a battle-tested set of rules for building services that deploy cleanly to modern platforms and scale horizontally. You don't need to memorize all twelve — internalize the handful that bite hardest.
FactorRule
ConfigStore config in the environment, never in code
Backing servicesTreat DBs/queues as attachable resources (via URL)
ProcessesStateless & share-nothing — state goes to a backing service
Port bindingExport services via a port; self-contained
DisposabilityFast startup / graceful shutdown (scale & recover fast)
Dev/prod parityKeep environments as similar as possible
LogsTreat logs as event streams to stdout — the platform routes them
Follow these and your app runs the same on a laptop, in a container, or across 100 nodes — the foundation for autoscaling, twelve-factor is why containers and PaaS (Platform as a Service) "just work."
The Fingerprint
The Factors Aren't Equal — Statelessness Is Load-Bearing, and It's the One Teams Quietly Break
The list reads as twelve rules of equal weight; they are not. Stateless, share-nothing processes is the load-bearing factor, because it is what makes horizontal scaling, disposability and autoscaling actually work — every other benefit rests on any instance being able to handle any request and being safe to kill at any moment. And it is precisely the one teams violate without noticing, because state creeps back in quietly: an in-memory session, a local file cache, an upload written to the container's disk, a cron job scheduled inside the web process, a “sticky sessions” toggle on the load balancer. Each reintroduces state, and then the mystery is “why does scaling to two instances break login / lose files / run the job twice.” The fingerprint of a broken twelfth factor is a bug that only appears once there is more than one instance.
The quiet state leakWhat breaks at 2+ instances
In-memory session / cacheLogin and cached data are per-instance — inconsistent
File written to local diskThe upload is on one box; the next request hits another
Cron / scheduler in the web processThe job runs N times, once per instance
Sticky sessions to paper over itUneven load, and a lost instance loses its users' state
So of the twelve, hold statelessness hardest: push every scrap of state to a backing service — sessions to Redis, files to object storage, schedules to a dedicated worker — and test with more than one instance, because a single instance hides every violation. The rest of the factors make deployment clean; this one is what makes scaling and recovery possible at all, which is why “it works on one node” tells you almost nothing.
🗂️ Domain-Driven Design (DDD) Design ▶
Concept
Model the Business, Not the Database
DDD is an approach to complex software that puts the business domain at the center. Developers and domain experts build a shared ubiquitous language — the same words in conversation and in code — and split the system into bounded contexts where each term has one precise meaning ("account" means something different to Billing vs Auth).
ConceptMeaning
Bounded contextA boundary where a model & language are consistent (often → a service)
Ubiquitous languageShared vocabulary used identically by experts & code
EntityObject with identity that persists over time (a User)
Value objectDefined only by its attributes, immutable (an Address, Money)
AggregateA cluster of objects with one root that guards invariants
Bounded contexts are why DDD and microservices pair so well — a well-drawn context is a natural service boundary. Start with the language; the model follows.
The bill
Without the Domain Expert, It Degrades Into a Folder Layout
DDD's value is in the conversation — the ubiquitous language, the argument about where a boundary falls, the discovery that two teams were using “order” to mean different things. The tactical patterns are the residue of that conversation. Copy the residue without the conversation and you get a directory called domain/ and no shared understanding.
PreconditionIf you do not have it
Regular access to a domain expertThe language gets invented by developers, and it will be wrong in ways nobody notices for a year
Genuine domain complexityCRUD (Create, Read, Update, Delete) with ceremony — aggregates and repositories over a table with six columns
Authority to redraw boundariesBounded contexts drawn around the existing team structure, which is where they already were
Room for the first model to be wrongThe first model is always wrong. With no room to revise, the first guess ships as architecture
The cheap part of DDD is worth doing everywhere and costs nothing: name things the way the business names them, and notice when one word means two things. The expensive part — aggregates, repositories, context maps — earns its keep when the rules are genuinely intricate and getting them wrong is costly. Insurance, logistics and finance qualify; a booking form does not.
🧱 SOLID (Single responsibility, Open-closed, Liskov substitution, Interface segregation, Dependency inversion) Principles Design ▶
Reference
Five Rules for Maintainable OO Code
SOLID is five principles that keep object-oriented code flexible and testable. You don't apply them dogmatically — you reach for them when code becomes rigid or hard to change.
PrincipleMeans

The throughline is manage dependencies: isolate what changes, program to interfaces, and keep the direction of dependency pointing at stable abstractions (this is the engine behind Clean Architecture).
The bill
An Interface With One Implementation Is a Guess About the Future
Applied as rules rather than as pressure, SOLID reliably produces a particular codebase: dozens of one-method classes, an interface for every class, a dependency-injection container wiring it together, and no way to read a feature end to end. Each of those interfaces is a prediction, and predictions about which axis a system will vary along are usually wrong.
LetterRead as a rule — the failureRead as pressure — the use

The cheap test before adding an interface: name the second implementation. If you can name it, add the abstraction. If you cannot, you are speculating — and the concrete class is easy to extract later, once the second case exists and has told you what the abstraction should have been. Removing speculative generality is far harder than adding it.
🔗 Coupling & Cohesion Design ▶
Concept
The Two Words That Describe Good Structure
Almost every design principle reduces to two ideas. High cohesion: things that change together live together (a module does one job well). Low coupling: modules depend on each other as little as possible, through narrow interfaces. Aim for both — you want tight-inside, loose-between.
PropertyGoodBad
CohesionHigh — focused moduleLow — grab-bag of unrelated code
CouplingLow — few, clean dependenciesHigh — change one, break many
The Law of Demeter ("only talk to your neighbors" — avoid a.getB().getC().doThing()) is a practical way to keep coupling low. When a change ripples across many files, that's high coupling telling you the boundaries are wrong.
The Fingerprint
Low Coupling Is Not Free — Couple Along the Axis of Change, Not Against It
“Low coupling, high cohesion” is repeated so often it becomes a reflex, and the reflex over-corrects. A developer who has learned that coupling is bad reaches for indirection everywhere — an interface with a single implementation, an event bus for a direct call, a config flag for a constant — and produces something technically loose and genuinely harder to change, because one behaviour is now smeared across five files you must hold in your head at once. The point the two words miss: coupling is not uniformly bad — what matters is whether the coupled things change together. Coupling along the axis of change (the tax rules and the tax calculator, versioned as one) is fine; coupling across it (the invoice layout reaching into the calculator's internals) is the pain.
The moveWhen it helps, and when it is just ceremony
Extract an interfaceHelps with two real implementations or a genuine test seam; hurts as ritual around a single one
Introduce an event / messageHelps when the publisher truly shouldn't know its subscribers; hurts when it hides a simple, traceable direct call
Split a moduleHelps when two responsibilities change on different clocks; hurts when it separates things that always change together — low cohesion by another name
Add an abstraction layerHelps once the axis of change is known; hurts when added before it, guessing wrong
So the honest rule under the two words is: decouple things that change on different clocks, and leave coupled the things that change together — an abstraction guessed before you know the axis is just coupling you cannot see. When a change ripples across many files, ask first whether those files should have been one: sometimes the fix for high coupling is more cohesion, not more indirection.
📐 API-First & Contract-Driven Design Design ▶
Concept
Design the Interface Before the Implementation
API-first means the API (Application Programming Interface) contract (e.g. an OpenAPI or Protobuf spec) is written and agreed before anyone builds the backend. Frontend, backend, and consumers all code against the same contract in parallel, and you can generate clients, mocks, and docs from it. The interface becomes the source of truth, not an afterthought.
BenefitDetail
Parallel workTeams build against the spec + a mock, not a finished server
CodegenClients, server stubs, docs, validation from one spec
Consumer-driven contractsConsumers publish expectations; a broken change fails CI (Pact)
GovernanceConsistent conventions, versioning, and review across APIs
This is what makes microservices and public platforms manageable — the contract is a stable boundary that lets teams move independently without constant breakage (ties to the API Design topic).
The artefact
The Contract, and the Three Questions to Ask of It
“Design the contract first” is agreed with and then skipped, because the contract is described rather than shown. It is short. Here is one endpoint, and then the review that matters — most API mistakes are visible in the contract before a line of code exists.
ContractWritten
The operationPOST /v1/exports → 202 Accepted, body { id, status: "pending", href }
Because it is slowAsynchronous by design. GET /v1/exports/{id} polls; the client never holds a connection open for minutes
Errors, typed400 validation with a field array; 409 if an export is already running; 429 with Retry-After
IdempotencyIdempotency-Key header; a repeat within 24h returns the original id rather than starting a second export
Pagination and versioningCursor-based (?cursor=), never offset. /v1/ in the path, and additive changes only within a major version
The three review questions that catch most of it: what does a client do when this times out? (if the answer is "retry and hope", it needs an idempotency key); what breaks if this field is added? (if anything does, the client is too strict and the contract should say so); and how does a caller page through a million of these? (offset pagination silently duplicates and skips rows under concurrent writes). All three are answerable before any code exists, which is the entire argument for writing the contract first.
🧮 Idempotency & Exactly-Once — Safe Retries Reliability ▶
Concept
The Property Distributed Systems Live and Die By
In any networked system, messages get lost, duplicated, and retried. An operation is idempotent if doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once — the single most important property for correctness under retries. "Charge $10" is dangerous to retry; "set balance to $90" is safe.
TechniqueHow
Idempotency keyClient sends a unique ID; server dedupes and returns the original result
Natural idempotencyDesign ops as "set to X" (absolute) not "add X" (relative)
Dedup storeTrack processed message IDs; skip repeats
Outbox patternAtomically write state + the event to publish, so neither is lost
True "exactly-once" delivery is famously near-impossible; the practical answer is at-least-once delivery + idempotent consumers = effectively-once processing. Build every consumer and API (Application Programming Interface) assuming it will see duplicates.
The Fingerprint
The Idempotency Key Is the Easy Part — the Race and the Window Are Not
An idempotency key looks like the whole answer, and then production finds the two ways a naive one fails. First the race: check-then-act. Two retries of the same request arrive close together, both look up the key, both find nothing, both proceed — and you charged twice with a perfect idempotency key in place. The fix is not a lookup but an atomic guard: a unique constraint on the key or a conditional write, so the second one loses. Second the window: dedup records cannot live forever, so a duplicate that arrives after the record expires is processed as new — set the window shorter than your longest realistic retry horizon and you have reintroduced the bug. And “effectively-once” holds only if the effect and the dedup marker commit together; a crash between them either double-applies or forgets, which is exactly what the outbox pattern exists to prevent.
FailureWhy the naive version breaksThe fix
Concurrent duplicatesCheck-then-act — both see “not processed”Atomic guard: unique constraint or conditional write, not a lookup
Late duplicateThe dedup record already expiredWindow ≥ the longest realistic retry horizon
Crash mid-operationEffect and marker in separate commitsOne transaction, or the outbox pattern
Leaky downstreamAn “idempotent” op calls a non-idempotent serviceIdempotency is end to end or it is nothing
So idempotency is not a key you attach; it is an invariant you enforce atomically. The question to ask of any “idempotent” handler is the adversarial one: what happens when two copies run in the same millisecond, and what happens when the retry comes an hour late? If both answers are safe it is idempotent; if either rests on a lookup that could race, it is not yet.
✍️ Clean Code & Naming Craft ▶
Concept
Code Is Read Far More Than It's Written
You write a line once; it's read dozens of times over its life. Clean code optimizes for the reader — clarity beats cleverness. The highest-leverage skill is naming: a good name removes the need for a comment.
PrincipleDetail

The Boy Scout Rule: leave the code a little cleaner than you found it. Small, constant improvements beat big rewrites.
The Fingerprint
Clean Code Has a Failure Mode: Over-Applied, the Rules Invert Into Their Opposite
The principles are right, and each taken too far becomes the problem it was meant to solve. Small functions are good until there are so many tiny ones that following the logic means jumping through ten files — at which point “just inline it” is the readable move. DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) is right until it's applied to coincidental duplication: two things that merely look alike get merged into one abstraction, and now a change for one breaks the other — a false abstraction is worse than the duplication, because the duplication was at least honest about being two things. “Comments explain why” decays into no comments and “the code is self-documenting” as an excuse. And the costliest naming failure is not a bad name but a name that lies — a variable whose meaning drifted while its name stayed, which you trust precisely because it reads clean.

So treat clean code as a means — readability for the next person — not a rulebook to maximise: when a “clean” rule makes the code harder to follow, the rule is wrong for that case. The test is not “did I follow the principles” but “can the next person read this in one pass,” and the two come apart exactly at the extremes, where a rule pushed past its point of usefulness inverts into the mess it was meant to prevent.

The Same Point, Written Three Ways
Advice about review comments is easy to agree with and hard to act on. Here is one real situation — a pull request adds a database call inside a loop — written three ways. The difference is not tone. It is how much work the author has to do after reading it.
VersionAs typed
Unhelpful“This is an N+1.” Correct, and the author now has to work out where, why it matters, and what you want instead.

💳 Technical Debt — Recognize & Pay It Down Craft ▶
Concept
Borrowing Speed Now Against Effort Later
Technical debt is the implied future cost of a quick-and-dirty choice. Like financial debt, a little is a useful tool (ship now, fix later); too much, unmanaged, and the "interest" — slower changes, more bugs — eventually consumes all your capacity.
TypeExample
Deliberate"We'll hardcode this to hit the deadline" (fine, if tracked)
AccidentalLearned a better design too late; code lagging behind understanding
Bit rotDependencies age, the world changes, code decays
Manage it: make it visible (a backlog, not tribal knowledge), pay it down opportunistically (Boy Scout Rule) plus dedicated time, and prioritize debt in code you touch often. Debt in a stable, untouched corner rarely matters.
The Decision
“Tech Debt” Is Two Different Things, and the Word Is How Neither Gets Fixed
The metaphor is useful right up to the point it becomes an excuse, and two abuses are common. One: “technical debt” used to mean “code I would have written differently” — an aesthetic disagreement dressed as a liability, which spends credibility on changes that carry no interest. Two: debt as the justification for a doomed grand gesture — a “tech-debt sprint” that loses every prioritisation fight against features, or a big rewrite that quietly throws away what the ugly code was worth. Because debt only bites where it taxes a change you are actually making, the framing that survives a product roadmap is not a debt backlog competing for its own sprint; it is paying the debt down inside the feature that has to cross that code.
The moveWhy it stalls or failsWhat works instead
A “tech-debt sprint”Loses every prioritisation fight against featuresFund paydown inside the feature that must touch that code
The big rewriteDiscards years of embedded bug fixes; ships nothing for monthsStrangler fig — replace incrementally behind the running system
A debt backlog nobody readsDebt with no live cost never rises to the topTrack only debt in code you touch; leave the stable corners
“This is debt” meaning “I dislike it”Spends credibility on taste, not costName the interest, or concede it is not debt
So the test for real debt is whether you can name its interest: the specific change it makes slower, riskier, or more bug-prone. Debt you cannot cost that way is either taste or a stable corner, and both are fine left alone. The debt worth paying announces itself — it is the file you keep having to reopen — and the cheapest time to pay it is the next time a feature sends you there anyway.
📝 ADRs (Architecture Decision Record) & Design Docs Craft ▶
Reference
Write Down the Decision and the Why
Six months later, no one remembers why the team chose Postgres over Mongo, and the reasoning gets re-litigated. An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) is a short doc capturing one decision: the context, the options, the choice, and the consequences. A design doc (or RFC) does the same before building something significant.
ArtifactPurpose
ADR1-page record of a single decision (context/options/decision/consequences)
Design doc / RFC (Request for Comments)Propose & get feedback before building; align the team
C4 modelDiagrams at 4 zoom levels: Context, Container, Component, Code
Keep them short and in the repo (version-controlled next to the code). The value is the trail of reasoning — it onboards new people and stops old debates from restarting.
The artefact
A Complete ADR, Short Enough That Someone Will Write One
ADRs fail by being described rather than shown, and then by being too long to write. The whole thing fits on one screen. Here is a real one, and the format is the point: Context, Decision, Consequences, Status, with the context doing most of the work.
HeadingFilled in
ADR-014: Use Postgres for the events tableStatus: Accepted, 2026-03-11. Supersedes ADR-009
ContextEvents reach ~40M rows/year. We already run Postgres and nobody on the team has operated Cassandra. Read patterns are unknown; the product team expects ad-hoc queries
DecisionUse Postgres with monthly range partitions on occurred_at. Revisit if sustained writes exceed 5k/s or a single partition exceeds 100 GB
ConsequencesWe accept a single writer node. Ad-hoc queries stay easy. Partition maintenance needs a scheduled job. If the write ceiling is reached, migration is a project, not a config change
What is missing on purposeThe alternatives considered in detail. Add them only when the choice was genuinely close — otherwise it is padding nobody reads
Two properties make an ADR worth the ten minutes. It records the context that made the decision reasonable, so the reader in 2029 can tell whether that context still holds — which is the only question they will actually have. And it names the condition that would reverse it: "revisit if sustained writes exceed 5k/s" turns an opinion into something checkable. A decision with no reversal condition is a preference.
The format's real value
The Decision Matters Less Than the Options You Rejected
An ADR that records only what was chosen answers a question nobody asks. The code already says what was chosen. What the reader in two years needs is what else was considered and which constraint killed it — because constraints expire, and when one does, the ADR is the only artefact that says the decision should now be revisited.
RecordedWhat it is worth later
“We chose Postgres”Nothing. Anyone can see that
“We considered Cassandra”A little — at least the option was seen
“Rejected Cassandra: nobody on the team had operated it”A great deal. That constraint changes when you hire, and the reader can tell
“Rejected managed search: not available in our region”Regions open. This is a dated fact, not a principle
“Rejected X because it is bad”Nothing, and worse than nothing — it forecloses re-examination with no reason to weigh
The test for a rejection line is whether it names something that could stop being true. “Too expensive at our volume”, “no in-house experience”, “unavailable in this region” and “the team is three people” all pass; “not a good fit” does not. Write the constraint, not the conclusion — and when an ADR is superseded, say which constraint expired, because that is the sentence the next reader is looking for.
🔌 Resilience Patterns — Circuit Breaker, Retry & Timeout Reliability ▶
Concept
Stop One Failure From Cascading
In a distributed system, one slow or dead dependency can drag down everything that calls it — threads pile up waiting, and the failure cascades. Resilience patterns contain that at every remote call.
PatternProtects against
TimeoutWaiting forever on a hung dependency
Retry + backoff + jitterTransient blips — but back off so you don't stampede
Circuit breakerA failing dependency — "trip open" and fail fast instead of hammering it
BulkheadOne overloaded feature sinking the rest (isolate resource pools)
FallbackDegrade gracefully — cached/default response over an error
Careful with retries: naive retries during an outage create a retry storm that keeps the dependency down. Always pair retries with backoff, jitter, and a circuit breaker; only retry idempotent operations.
The bill
Retries Cause the Outage They Were Added to Survive
A retry is extra load applied at exactly the moment a system is least able to take it. A dependency slows, every caller retries three times, offered load triples, and the dependency that was struggling is now down — a retry storm, and a classic self-inflicted outage. Worse, retries stack: three layers each retrying three times is twenty-seven requests for one user action.
RuleWhy
Retry at one layer onlyRetries multiply through the stack. Decide where, and make every other layer fail fast
Exponential backoff with jitterWithout jitter every client retries at the same instant — you have built a synchronised load test
Retry budget, not retry countCap retries as a share of traffic; a per-call count has no idea what the rest of the fleet is doing
Only retry idempotent operationsOtherwise a timeout on a call that actually succeeded charges the card twice
Timeout shorter than your caller'sA longer one is decoration — they gave up before you did
The circuit breaker exists to stop the storm: after enough failures it fails fast without calling, gives the dependency room to recover, then lets one probe through. Pair it with a fallback that is honest — stale cache, reduced feature, a clear message — because the realistic alternative to a slow correct answer is a fast partial one, not an error page.
💰 Distributed Transactions — Saga & Outbox Reliability ▶
Concept
Consistency Across Services Without 2PC
When a business action spans multiple services/databases (place order → charge card → reserve stock), you can't wrap it in one ACID (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) transaction. Two-phase commit (2PC) technically can, but it's slow and fragile — so modern systems avoid it.
PatternHow
SagaA sequence of local transactions; on failure, run compensating actions to undo
OutboxWrite state + the "event to publish" in one local transaction, so the event can't be lost
2PCCoordinator locks all participants to commit atomically — avoided at scale (blocking, SPOF)
Sagas trade atomicity for availability: the system is briefly inconsistent, then converges (or compensates). "Charge failed → cancel the order" is a compensating transaction. Pair with idempotency (E3) so retried steps are safe.
The bill
Compensation Is Not Rollback, and Every Partial State Needs an Owner
A saga does not undo anything. It runs a second business operation intended to offset the first, and the difference matters: a refund is not an un-charge, cancelling a shipment that already left is a different process, and an email that has been sent cannot be unsent. Every step you add multiplies the partial states somebody has to have thought about.
RealityWhat it demands
There is no rollbackWrite the compensating action for every step, as product behaviour rather than error handling
Some steps cannot be compensatedOrder them last, or make them reversible — send the email after the money has moved
Partial states are user-visibleThe order that is paid but unshipped needs a screen and a support answer
Compensation can fail tooNow you need retries, a dead-letter path, and a human queue
Outbox is the mechanical halfWrite the event in the same transaction as the state change, publish separately
Before reaching for a saga, try much harder to make the operation local. Two services that always change together are frequently one service that was split too early, and merging them replaces an entire distributed protocol with a database transaction. Sagas are right when the boundary is genuinely real — a payment provider, a warehouse, another company — and expensive whenever it is not.
🌊 Backpressure & Flow Control Reliability ▶
Concept
Don't Accept More Than You Can Handle
When work arrives faster than a system can process it, unbounded queues grow until memory runs out and everything crashes. Backpressure is the system signaling "slow down" upstream, so load is shed or throttled rather than silently piling up.
TechniqueDetail
Bounded queuesFixed size; reject/block when full instead of growing forever
Load sheddingDrop low-priority work under overload (protect the core)
Rate limitingCap intake at a sustainable rate (token bucket)
Reactive streamsConsumer signals demand to the producer (pull, not push)
The counter-intuitive lesson: a system that rejects excess load cleanly stays up; one that politely accepts everything falls over. Fail fast, shed early.
The Fingerprint
Backpressure Doesn't Remove the Pileup — It Moves It to Whatever You Forgot to Bound
Adding a bounded queue to a service feels like solving the problem, and at that service it does. But backpressure does not absorb load, it relocates it: the moment A starts rejecting or blocking, the pileup moves to A's caller — and if B has its own unbounded buffer, or blocks its own callers in turn, the pressure simply travels upstream until it reaches the one link nobody bounded. That link is usually the least visible: a connection queue on the load balancer, a broker's disk, the user-facing edge holding sockets open. So backpressure is a system property, not a local one — a single unbounded buffer anywhere is where all the pressure ends up. Two traps live inside it: blocking backpressure can deadlock (A waits on B while B waits on A through a shared pool), and retries turn overload into a storm (a timeout triggers a retry, the retry adds load — positive feedback a retry budget with jitter exists to break).
The trapWhat actually happens
Bound one service, not the chainThe pileup moves upstream to the first unbounded buffer — often the load balancer or the edge
Bounded + block, in a cyclic dependencyDeadlock — each side waits on the other through a shared resource
Retry on timeout, no budgetRetry storm — overload causes timeouts, timeouts cause more load
Shed indiscriminatelyYou drop health checks or the wrong tier and take down more than you saved
So the discipline is end to end: trace a unit of work from the edge to the datastore and find the one hop with an unbounded queue — that hop is where every overload lands, whatever you did elsewhere. Prefer bounded-and-reject to bounded-and-block where dependencies can form a cycle, give every retry a budget and jitter, and shed by priority, not at random. Fail fast is right; fail fast everywhere is the whole rule.
🎛️ Consistency in Practice — Quorums & Tunable Reads Reliability ▶
Concept
Dialing the CAP Trade-off Per Operation
The CAP theorem is abstract; in practice distributed databases give you knobs. With quorums, a value is written to/read from a majority of replicas — and by tuning how many must respond (W + R > N), you choose consistency vs latency per query.
TermMeaning
N / W / RReplicas / write acks required / read acks required
W + R > NGuarantees a read sees the latest write (strong)
Low W, low RFast + available, but reads may be stale (eventual)
Read repair / anti-entropyReplicas reconcile in the background
So a shopping-cart read can be fast-and-eventual (low R) while the final checkout uses a strong quorum — the same store, tuned per operation. This is how Dynamo-style systems make CAP practical (ties to the CAP Theorem topic).
The Fingerprint
W + R > N Is a Staleness Bound, Not Linearizability — Conflating Them Loses Data
The quorum inequality is routinely read as “strong consistency,” and it is not — the gap is where data gets lost. W + R > N guarantees a read overlaps a replica that saw the latest acknowledged write, which gives recency, but it says nothing about ordering concurrent writes: two clients writing different values can both reach quorum, and without a conflict strategy (vector-clock versioning, last-write-wins, or true consensus) you get lost updates or the resurrected delete — the item that reappears because a replica's tombstone expired before anti-entropy propagated it. Real linearizability — one global order every client agrees on — needs a leader and a consensus protocol (Raft, Paxos), and that costs exactly the latency and partition-availability quorum tuning existed to avoid.
You wantQuorum (W+R>N) givesThe real thing needs
Recent readsYes — a read sees the latest acked write(sufficient)
Concurrent writes orderedNo — both can win; conflicts happenVersioning + merge, or a single writer
No resurrected deletesNot guaranteed — tombstone/anti-entropy racesTombstone GC tuned to the repair window
One global order (linearizable)NoA leader + consensus (Raft/Paxos)
So tune quorums for staleness, but do not mistake W + R > N for a total order: decide explicitly how concurrent writes reconcile — a merge function, last-write-wins with its known data-loss risk, or a single writer — and reach for a consensus-backed store only where you genuinely need linearizability, because that is the guarantee quorums structurally cannot give, and paying for it brings back the latency and partition fragility you tuned quorums to escape.

☁️ Cloud-Native & Serverless Architecture Patterns Architecture ▶
Concept
Designing for the Platform, Not Just on It
Cloud-native architecture assumes elastic, disposable infrastructure and managed services. Serverless takes it further — you write functions and the platform handles scaling to zero and back. Both change how you decompose a system.
PatternDetail

Trade-off: serverless minimizes ops and cost at low/spiky load but can get expensive and awkward at steady high volume (and vendor lock-in rises). Match the model to the traffic shape — ties to the "Choosing Compute" cloud card.
The Decision
Serverless Moves the Operational Cost, It Doesn't Remove It — and the New Costs Aren't Priced In
The “no ops” pitch is the trap. Serverless genuinely removes server management, but the operational burden reappears in forms nobody budgets for. Observability gets harder, not easier: a request fans out across dozens of ephemeral functions with no shared log, so distributed tracing stops being optional from day one. You lose in-memory state and connection pooling — every invocation is cold and stateless, so a database with connection limits gets hammered until you put a pooler in front of it. Local development becomes emulation of the platform. And cost couples to traffic in a way that punishes success: the endpoint that would have been free on a fixed-size server now bills per invocation, so a viral moment is also a surprise invoice. None of these show up in the “just write a function” demo; all of them show up in month two.
The “serverless removes it” claimWhere the cost reappears
No servers to manageDistributed tracing, cold-start tuning, quota/limit management
Scales automaticallyYour database's connection limit doesn't — you need a pooler/proxy
Just write a functionState and connection reuse are gone; local dev is emulation
Pay only for what you useCost scales with success; steady high volume is dearer than a VM (Virtual Machine)
So match the model to the workload, not the marketing: serverless is right for spiky, unpredictable, event-driven and glue work, where scale-to-zero and per-event billing are wins. It is the wrong default for steady high-throughput (cheaper on reserved capacity), latency-sensitive paths (cold starts), and long-running or stateful jobs. The operational simplicity is real at the edges and partly an illusion in the middle — it comes back as debugging, connection management, and a bill you now have to watch.

Concept
Why Software Estimates Are Always Wrong
Estimation is hard because building software is discovering the problem, not repeating a known task. Mature teams estimate relative effort and manage uncertainty rather than pretending to predict exact dates.
IdeaDetail

Senior move: break big work into small, independently shippable slices — small things estimate accurately, and you get feedback early. Padding a giant estimate just hides the risk.
The artefact
An Estimate, With Its Assumptions Showing
“About three weeks” is not an estimate; it is a number with the reasoning removed, and it will be treated as a commitment. PartAs written
The range“3–5 weeks for the export feature, 80% confidence”

What is not“Not included: scheduled exports, or anything about permissions on shared reports”
Assumptions“Assumes the reporting API (Application Programming Interface) returns paginated results — if it does not, add a week”
The biggest unknown“The riskiest part is export size. If files exceed ~200 MB we need streaming, which is the difference between 3 and 5”
The most useful line is the last one, because it converts an estimate into a plan for reducing uncertainty: spend two days finding out the real export size and the range collapses. Ranges also survive better than points politically — a single number invites negotiation about the number, while a range invites a conversation about scope, which is the conversation worth having.

📐 Measuring Developer Productivity Without Doing Harm ENG • Decisions ▶
The Inversion
Every Individual Metric in This Space Is Gameable, and Most Are Actively Harmful
The request “measure our developers' productivity” sounds reasonable and has no good answer at the individual level, because every per-person metric optimises for the wrong thing the moment people know it is watched. Count commits and you get smaller commits; count lines and you get more lines; count story points and you get inflated estimates. The metric does not measure the work — it changes the work, and the change is always toward the number and away from the outcome.
Individual metricWhat you actually get
Lines of codeMore code — the opposite of the goal
Commits / PRs per weekTrivial commits; PRs split to pad the count
Story points completedPoint inflation; estimation becomes negotiation
Hours / activity trackedPresence theatre; the deep work that has no keystrokes disappears
This is Goodhart's Law with a salary attached: a measure under individual pressure stops being a measure. The honest position to hold with a manager who wants a leaderboard is that the leaderboard itself is the harm — it manufactures the behaviour it claims to detect.
The Decision
What Can Be Measured Is the System, Not the Person
There is a legitimate version of the question, and it points at the delivery system rather than the individual. The industry's research-backed measures — delivery flow and team health — are useful precisely because they are hard to game at the individual level and describe the pipeline everyone shares. They diagnose where work gets stuck, which is the thing a manager actually needs to know.
System metricWhat it reveals
Lead time for changeHow long an idea takes to reach production
Deployment frequencyHow small and safe releases have become
Change failure rate & recovery timeWhether speed is costing stability
Review latency; time to first commit for a new joinerWhere the human bottlenecks and onboarding gaps are
Measure the four delivery-flow metrics to improve the system, and pair them with a qualitative read of team health rather than a fifth number to rank people by. The verdict a manager needs to hear: you can measure the pipeline to make it faster for everyone, or you can rank individuals and make it slower for everyone — the same data cannot do both, and the second use poisons the first.
💵 CapEx vs OpEx — Why the Same Purchase Is Two Different Decisions IT Finance ▶
Concept
The Money Comes From Different Places and Behaves Differently
Capital expenditure buys an asset that is depreciated over years; operating expenditure is consumed in the period it is incurred. To an engineer the distinction looks like accounting trivia, and it decides which proposals get approved. The two come from different budgets, are approved by different people on different timelines, and are judged by different measures — so the identical technical solution can be trivially fundable in one form and impossible in the other.

📊 Building an IT Budget — Run, Grow, Transform IT Finance ▶
Concept
Three Buckets, and Only One of Them Is Discretionary
IT spend divides into keeping the lights on, improving what exists, and doing something new. Splitting a budget this way immediately exposes the number every IT leader is asked about: the share consumed by "run". A department spending 80% on run has almost no capacity to change anything, and no amount of enthusiasm about transformation alters that arithmetic.
BucketContainsCut it by
RunLicences, support, hosting, salaries, refreshConsolidation, automation, retiring things
GrowCapacity, upgrades, incremental capabilityDeferring — the usual first casualty
TransformNew capability the business asked forChoosing fewer initiatives, not smaller ones
The trap is that "grow" is what gets cut when money is tight, and grow is what keeps run from rising. Deferring upgrades for three years produces an estate whose run cost has quietly climbed, which is then cited as evidence that IT is expensive.
Technique
Defending a Line Item
Every line will eventually be questioned by someone who does not know what it does. The answer that survives is not technical justification — it is a statement of what stops happening if the line goes, in the language of the person asking.
WeakStrong

"The licences went up 12%""Vendor increase; here are the two alternatives and what switching costs"

"We need more storage""Full in September at current growth; ordering in June avoids the emergency premium"
Every strong version names a consequence and a date. That is what turns a request into a decision someone else can own — and the forecasting that produces the date is in Forecasting Capacity — Trend, Seasonality & Headroom.

Budgets are set on a calendar, and a brilliant proposal arriving in month eight competes for money that was allocated in month two. Knowing the cycle — when planning opens, when numbers are submitted, when they are locked — is worth more than the quality of any individual business case, because it is the difference between competing for the pot and asking for an exception to it.

📝 The Business Case — One Page an Executive Will Actually Read IT Finance ▶

⏱️ The Cost of Downtime — Modelling It Credibly Enough to Fund Resilience IT Finance ▶
Technique
Build the Number From Components, Not From a Headline
Industry averages for the cost of an outage minute are useless in a specific conversation: they are enormous, unsourced, and everybody in the room knows it. A number built from your own components is smaller and infinitely more persuasive, because each part can be checked. 
# Per hour of outage, for a specific service
   lost revenue      = revenue/hour through that service × the share genuinely lost
   idle labour       = affected staff × loaded hourly cost × the fraction truly blocked
   recovery effort   = responders × hours × loaded cost
   knock-on          = overtime, expedited shipping, credits owed, penalties
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Then, separately and NOT as a number:
   reputational and regulatory consequences

# "The share genuinely lost" matters: many orders are placed later,
# not lost. Claiming otherwise is the fastest way to lose the room.
Concept
Expected Annual Loss Is What Justifies Spend
A single outage cost is a story. Multiplying it by how often it happens turns it into a budget line that can be compared with the cost of preventing it — which is the only form in which resilience gets funded. 
   expected annual loss  =  cost per event  ×  events per year

# Worked
   £40k per outage  ×  3 outages/year   =  £120k/year exposure
   redundancy costs £45k/year, removes ~2 of the 3
   ->  avoided loss £80k against £45k spend

# And say what it does NOT cover — the third outage is a different cause.
Naming what the spend does not fix is what makes the rest credible. A proposal that claims to eliminate all outages is read, correctly, as a proposal that has not been thought through.
Concept
This Is Where RTO (Recovery Time Objective) and RPO (Recovery Point Objective) Come From
Recovery targets are usually handed to IT as requirements and treated as arbitrary. They are not: an RTO is the point at which accumulating outage cost exceeds what the business will spend to avoid it, and an RPO is the same judgement about lost data. Presenting the cost curve alongside the price of each recovery tier turns "we need zero downtime" into a conversation with an answer — usually a more modest one, chosen by the people who own the money. The tiers themselves are in BCP / DR — Key Metrics & Recovery Strategies in the Operations domain.

♻️ Hardware Refresh Economics — When Replacing Beats Maintaining IT Finance ▶
Concept
The Cheapest Year to Own a Machine Is Not the Last One
Keeping hardware another year looks free — the asset is already paid for and no invoice arrives. The costs are real and simply land in other people's budgets: support premiums, failure rates that climb, energy per unit of work that falls behind, and the productivity lost to slow machines, which is invisible precisely because it is spread across everyone.
Cost of keepingWhere it lands
Extended support past standard warrantyThe IT budget, and it rises steeply per year
Rising failure rateThe service desk, and the affected user's day
No security or firmware updates after end of supportThe risk register, and eventually an audit finding
Spare parts scarcityRecovery time, which lengthens quietly
Energy and rack space per unit of workFacilities, so IT never sees it
Staff time on slow machinesEverywhere, and it dwarfs the rest at fleet scale
The last row is the one to quantify for laptops. Five minutes a day per person across a thousand people is a very large number, and it is the argument that funds a refresh when "they still work" is the counter-position.
Technique

Buying an entire fleet at once creates a spike in spend now and an identical spike three or four years later, plus a single enormous migration project both times. Refreshing a fraction of the estate each year flattens the budget line, keeps the deployment process practised, and spreads the risk of a bad model choice.

Rolling 25% per yearFlat and predictableA bad choice affects a quarter, and is corrected next cycle

Whichever cycle is chosen, the disposal end matters as much as the purchase: certified data destruction, WEEE-style disposal obligations, and residual value from resale or trade-in that most organisations simply forfeit.
🗂️ Portfolio & Prioritisation — Deciding What Not to Do, Defensibly IT Finance ▶
Concept
A Priority List Where Everything Is Priority One Is Not a List
Prioritisation is not ranking work by importance; every stakeholder can argue their item is important and they are usually right. It is deciding what will not happen this year, and being able to say why to the person whose item it was. A portfolio that has never declined anything is not prioritised — it is a queue, and the sequencing is being done implicitly by whoever pushes hardest.
SymptomWhat it means
Everything is high priorityNothing is; the real order is being set by escalation volume
Projects start but rarely finishWork in progress exceeds capacity — starting is free, finishing is not
The list only growsNothing is ever formally declined, so nothing can be
Estimates are always wrong the same wayCapacity is being counted as if nobody runs anything
The second row is the most treatable. Limiting work in progress finishes more per year than starting more does, and it is free — but it requires saying "not yet" out loud, which is the part organisations find hard.
Technique
Scoring That Makes the Argument About Inputs
The value of a scoring model is not that it produces the right ranking — it will not. It is that it moves the debate from "my project matters" to "you scored the effort at 8 and I think it is 3", which is a question with an answer.
InputAsk
ValueRevenue, cost avoided, risk removed — in the same units across items
UrgencyDoes the value decay if it slips? A compliance date is not a preference
EffortIncluding the run cost it adds forever, not just the build
ConfidenceHow sure are we of the three above? Low confidence favours a small first slice

Run the exercise with the stakeholders in one room rather than collecting scores separately. Most of the value is in the conversation where two people discover they meant different things by the same project.
Concept

A portfolio planned to 100% of capacity has no room for the security fix, the regulatory change or the incident that will certainly appear, so those arrive as disruption and something planned silently slips. Committing only a portion of capacity to planned work is not slack — it is the acknowledgement that unplanned work is a permanent feature, and reserving for it is what makes the plan survive the year. 🎚️ Delegation — The Levels, and Why "I'll Just Do It" Is a Trap Leading Teams ▶
Reference
Delegation Is a Dial, Not a Switch
Most delegation failures come from treating it as binary — either you do it or they do, entirely. Naming the level explicitly, out loud, prevents both classic outcomes: the person who thought they had authority and did not, and the person waiting for direction you thought you had handed over.
LevelSounds likeUse when
1. Do exactly this"Run these steps and tell me when done"Emergencies; genuinely new people

   You do it:        2 hours, every time, forever.
   They do it:       6 hours the first time, 3 the second, 2 thereafter.
                     Plus 1 hour of your time teaching.

   Break-even:       around the third occurrence.
   After a year:     you have 20+ hours back, and a second person who can do it.

# And the part the arithmetic misses: they were bored, and now they are not.

Technique

🧩 Team Shapes & Conway's Law — Getting the Architecture You Want Leading Teams ▶
Concept
The Org Chart Is an Architecture Diagram
Conway's law observes that systems come to mirror the communication structures of the organisations that build them. It is not a curiosity — it is the most reliable prediction available about a design. Two teams that must coordinate constantly will produce a tightly coupled interface no matter what the architecture document says, and a boundary drawn between teams becomes a boundary in the system whether or not it belongs there.

🏗️ Where a Build Actually Comes From — Every Input, Including the Ones You Forgot Supply Chain ▶
Concept
Your Source Code Is a Minority Shareholder in Your Artifact
Ask an engineer what goes into their production container and they will describe the repository. Then count what the build actually consumed: a base image from a public registry, a package index resolving several hundred transitive dependencies, a compiler toolchain, a handful of CI actions pulled by tag from third-party repositories, build-time environment variables, and whatever the vendored install script decided to fetch. 📦 Dependency Risk — Transitive Depth, Typosquatting & the Abandoned Package Supply Chain ▶
Concept
You Reviewed the One You Chose; You Installed Several Hundred
A team adopts a library after a genuine discussion — maintainership, licence, activity, whether it does more than needed. That discussion covers one node. Installing it pulls its dependencies, and theirs, typically two orders of magnitude more code than the thing that was evaluated. Nobody chose those. Nobody reviewed them. Each one can execute at install time in most ecosystems, and each one is a maintainer account that can be phished.
The useful metric is not dependency count, which nobody can act on. It is how many distinct maintainers can put code on your production machines without anyone on your team approving it. That number is usually in the hundreds, it is computable from the lockfile, and it changes the conversation because it is a count of people rather than a count of packages.

🪜 SLSA Levels — What Each One Actually Requires You to Change Supply Chain ▶
Concept
A Ladder for Build Integrity, Not a Checklist for Security
SLSA — Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts — grades one narrow question: how much can a consumer of your artifact trust a statement about where it came from? It says nothing about whether your code is any good, whether your dependencies are safe, or whether the thing you built is worth running. That narrowness is the point. It makes the levels achievable, and it makes them mean something specific when claimed.
The framing that makes it click: each level removes one way an attacker could lie about provenance. Level 1 makes a claim exist at all. Level 2 makes the claim come from the build service rather than from whoever ran it. Level 3 makes the build itself hard to tamper with while it runs.
Reference

Most teams on a managed CI platform are close to Level 2 without knowing it, and the remaining step is usually configuration rather than engineering.

2Provenance is signed by the build platformUse a hosted build service that signs; stop publishing from laptops

The single highest-value move on that ladder is the Level 2 one, and it is organisational rather than technical: no artifact reaches a registry except from CI. The release-from-a-laptop path is how most unattributable artifacts come to exist, and closing it is what makes every later control meaningful.

✍️ Signing, Sigstore & Attestation — Proving How an Artifact Was Made Supply Chain ▶
Concept
Signing Answers "Who"; Attestation Answers "How"
A signature binds an artifact to a key, and every major supply-chain compromise of the last decade produced correctly signed artifacts — because the attacker reached the build system that holds the key. Signing alone therefore proves something true and insufficient. An attestation is a signed statement about an artifact: this digest was built from this commit, by this workflow, on this platform, at this time. That is a claim an attacker cannot satisfy merely by holding a key, because it names facts checkable against systems they do not control.
in-toto is the format those statements are written in; SLSA (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts) provenance is one specific kind of in-toto statement. Worth holding onto, because the two names appear together constantly and are frequently assumed to be alternatives.
Concept
Keyless Signing — Removing the Thing That Gets Stolen
The traditional obstacle to signing was never the cryptography; it was owning a long-lived private key. It has to be stored somewhere, reachable by CI, rotated, and recovered when the person who generated it leaves. Most organisations that "do signing" have a key in a variable that has not moved in four years.
Sigstore's keyless flow removes the object entirely. The build proves its identity with a short-lived OIDC (OpenID Connect) token — from GitHub Actions, GitLab, or a cloud workload identity — and receives a certificate valid for minutes, long enough to sign. The certificate, the signature and the identity that requested it are recorded in a public append-only transparency log. Nothing durable exists to steal afterwards.
PieceRole
FulcioIssues a short-lived certificate bound to a verified identity
RekorAppend-only transparency log of what was signed, by whom, and when
CosignThe tool that signs, attests and verifies, usually against a registry
The identityA workflow in a repository — not a person, and not a key
The transparency log carries a second, quieter benefit: a maintainer can detect a compromise by noticing signatures they did not produce. Without it, detection depends on somebody reporting the malicious artifact after the fact.
Troubleshooting
Verify the Identity, Not Merely the Signature
The most common mistake with keyless signing is checking that a valid signature exists. Anyone can produce one — the flow is open by design. What matters is whose identity signed, and which workflow it ran in. 
# Says almost nothing: someone, somewhere, signed this
cosign verify $IMAGE

# The real check: this identity, from this issuer, and no other
cosign verify $IMAGE \
  --certificate-identity-regexp '^https://github.com/acme/api/.github/workflows/release.yml@refs/tags/' \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com
Two details decide whether this is a control or a ritual. Anchor the identity pattern to the start of the string — an unanchored match against a repository name is satisfied by a repository whose name merely contains it. And verify by digest rather than by tag: a tag is a mutable pointer, so verifying :latest confirms a signature on whatever the tag meant at that instant, which is not necessarily what gets pulled a second later.
🛂 Verifying at Deploy Time — Admission Policy & Reproducible Builds Supply Chain ▶
Concept
The Control Is the Refusal, Not the Signature
Everything upstream produces evidence. None of it constrains anything until some system declines to run an artifact whose evidence is missing or wrong. That refusal is the entire control, and it is the step most programmes never reach — because generating attestations breaks nothing and enforcing them can stop a deploy at four in the afternoon.
Put the gate where nothing can go around it. A check in the deploy pipeline is a good start and is bypassed by anyone who applies a manifest directly; an admission controller in the cluster sees every path. The policy machinery is the same as for any other admission rule — see Policy as Code — OPA/Rego & Kyverno in the DevOps domain for the mechanics.
Reference
Rolling It Out Without Stopping Deployments
The sequence matters more than the policy language. Every failed rollout of this control looks the same: enforcement enabled globally on a Tuesday, three unrelated teams blocked by Wednesday, exemption granted to everyone by Thursday.
StageActionWhy this order
1Audit mode — log what would be refused, block nothingReveals the real inventory, which is always larger than expected
2Fix the fixable — sign the internal images that simply were notMost of the audit list is not exceptions, it is unfinished work
3Enforce for one namespace that is already cleanProves the mechanism with nothing at stake
4Named, time-boxed exemptions for third-party imagesVendor images you cannot sign are the genuine long tail
5Enforce by default; exemptions must be requestedFlips the default, which is the point of the whole exercise
6Alert on exemption use and expiryOtherwise the temporary list becomes the permanent one
Decide the failure mode deliberately and write it down. If the policy engine or the transparency log is unreachable, does the deploy proceed or stop? Fail-open makes the control absent exactly when infrastructure is unhealthy — which is when an attacker would prefer to act. Fail-closed can turn an outage in one dependency into an inability to deploy the fix. Most teams should fail closed and keep a rehearsed break-glass path.
Concept
Reproducible Builds — The Property Underneath the Claim
A build is reproducible if the same source, built again, produces a bit-identical artifact. That sounds academic until you notice what it enables: anyone can rebuild and compare, so a compromised build system can be caught by a second party who does not trust the first. It turns "trust this build" into "check this build", which is the only claim in the whole supply-chain stack that does not eventually rest on trusting someone.
What breaks reproducibilityUsual fix
Embedded build timestampsSet SOURCE_DATE_EPOCH from the commit date

Absolute paths baked into binariesBuild in a fixed path, or strip the prefix
Unpinned toolchain or base imagePin by digest, including the compiler

Locale, timezone, hostname, random seedsFix them in the build environment
Full bit-for-bit reproducibility is expensive in some ecosystems and cheap in others, so treat it as a direction rather than a gate. Even partial progress pays: most of that list is also what makes a build deterministic enough to debug, and a team that pins its toolchain and drops its timestamps has removed real variance whether or not it ever compares hashes with anyone.
📋 SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) in Practice & VEX (Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange) — Querying It, and Saying "Not Affected" Credibly Supply Chain ▶
Concept
The Question an SBOM Exists to Answer in Under an Hour
A software bill of materials is an inventory of what is inside an artifact. Its value is not the document — it is the answer to one question, asked on a bad morning: which of our services contain this component, at which version, and which of those are actually in production right now? Organisations without an SBOM answer that by asking every team and waiting two days. That delay is the entire cost being avoided.
Which means the test of an SBOM programme is not whether documents are generated. It is whether someone can run that query. An SBOM attached to a registry artifact and never indexed is a file; an SBOM in a store you can query across every deployed version is an incident-response capability. Generation is the easy half and gets all the attention.
Reference
Decisions That Determine Whether It Is Useful
A few early choices decide whether the resulting inventory answers real questions or merely satisfies a contractual clause.
DecisionGet it right by
When to generateAt build, from the build — a scan of the finished image guesses; the build knows
Which formatSPDX or CycloneDX; pick one, and care more about consistency than the choice
How deepInclude transitive dependencies and OS (Operating System) packages, or you have documented the top layer
Where it livesAttached to the artifact by digest, and indexed centrally — both, not either
Identifier disciplinePackage URLs (Uniform Resource Locator) and CPEs; free-text names cannot be matched to advisories
Coverage of what runsTie SBOMs to deployed digests, so the query returns services, not builds
Concept
VEX — The Document That Says "Present, But Not Exploitable"
An SBOM plus a vulnerability feed produces a list dominated by findings that do not apply: the vulnerable function is never called, the affected code path is compiled out, the feature is disabled. A Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange document is a machine-readable statement of that judgement, attached to the artifact, so downstream consumers and scanners stop re-raising it — and so the reasoning survives the person who made it.
StatusMeansObligation
Not affectedPresent, but not exploitable hereState a justification — this is the one that needs defending
AffectedExploitable; action requiredAdvise what consumers should do meanwhile
FixedRemediated in this versionSay which version, precisely
Under investigationNot yet determinedLegitimate, but it is a timer, not an answer
The justifications are a fixed vocabulary — component not present, vulnerable code not in the execution path, inline mitigation already exists, and so on — and that constraint is what gives VEX its integrity. "Not affected" without a justification is an assertion; with one, it is a claim somebody can check and, if wrong, correct. Treat every "not affected" as a statement you will one day have to defend to an auditor or a customer, because you will.
🔁 Dependency Triage & Update Strategy — Reachability, Bumps & the First Hour of a Compromise Supply Chain ▶
Concept
Severity Is About the Vulnerability; Priority Is About You
A dependency scanner returns hundreds of findings sorted by a score computed by someone who has never seen your system. Working that list top-down is how teams spend a quarter on critical-rated issues in code paths their application never executes, while a medium-rated flaw sits in the request handler on the public endpoint. The score is an input to prioritisation, not the prioritisation.
QuestionWhy it outranks the score
Is the vulnerable function reachable from our code?The majority of findings fail this and can be deferred honestly
Is it a build-time or a runtime dependency?A test-only package is not in the deployed artifact
Is the affected component exposed to untrusted input?Decides whether an attacker can reach it at all
Is there a public exploit, and is it being used?Known-exploited status changes the timeline more than any severity band
What is the fix's blast radius?A patch bump and a major-version migration are different pieces of work
Reachability analysis is the highest-leverage tool here, and its limits should be stated plainly: it under-reports on dynamic dispatch, reflection and anything invoked by string name. Use it to justify deferring, never to justify ignoring, and re-run it when the code around the call sites changes.
Reference
An Update Strategy That Does Not Break Main
Automated update tooling either becomes the thing that keeps a codebase current, or a permanent wall of ignored pull requests that trains everyone to ignore dependency alerts. The difference is configuration, not tooling choice.
PracticeEffect
Group patch and minor bumps into one scheduled pull requestOne review a week instead of thirty notifications
Auto-merge patch updates when the full suite passesRemoves the work nobody was ever going to do by hand
Raise major versions individually, with the changelogThese are migrations; they need a human and a moment
Apply a cooling-off delay before adopting a new releaseMost malicious releases are pulled within days of publication
Update continuously rather than in a quarterly sweepSmall diffs are reviewable; a year of drift is a project
Treat a red update pull request as a real signalIt is the earliest, cheapest warning that a dependency changed behaviour
The cooling-off delay and auto-merge look contradictory and are not: hold new versions briefly, then adopt them automatically once they have aged. That combination is what makes pinning safe, and it is the reason the two controls belong in the same conversation.
Troubleshooting
A Dependency You Ship Was Compromised — The First Hour
This is a different exercise from patching a vulnerability. A vulnerability is a weakness that might be exploited; a compromised package is code that already ran, on developer machines and build runners, with whatever those hold.
OrderStep
1Determine exposure from the SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) store — which versions, which services, which are deployed
2Establish the window: when the bad version was published, and when you first resolved it
3Stop the spread — pin or block the version in the internal mirror so no new build picks it up
4Treat CI runners and developer machines that installed it as compromised hosts, not as build infrastructure
5Rotate every secret those runners could reach, on the assumption they were read
6Rebuild and redeploy from a clean resolution; roll forward rather than merely reverting the manifest
7Hunt for what the payload did — outbound connections, new credentials, modified artifacts
Steps 4 and 5 are the ones routinely skipped, and they are the ones that matter. Removing the package restores a clean dependency tree and does nothing about a credential that was exfiltrated an hour after it was installed. The wider incident process is in Incident Response — What to Do When It Goes Wrong.
⚙️ Securing the Pipeline — Secrets, Runners, Fork Pull Requests & Internal Registries Supply Chain ▶
Concept
CI Is Production, and It Is Usually Governed Like a Developer Tool
The build system holds deployment credentials, signing identities, registry write access and cloud roles, and it executes code from every branch anyone pushes. By capability it is one of the most privileged systems in the organisation. By governance it is normally a configuration file that any contributor can edit, reviewed with the same attention as a change to a README.
The mental correction that fixes most of the specific problems: a pipeline definition is privileged code. Reviewing a workflow change is reviewing something that runs with production credentials. Requiring approval for changes under the CI directory, separately from ordinary code review, costs almost nothing and closes the most direct path there is.
Reference
The Failure Modes, Ranked by How Often They Are Real
These are the findings that actually appear in pipeline reviews, in roughly the order they turn up.
FailureFix
Workflows triggered by fork pull requests can reach secretsUse the unprivileged trigger for untrusted code; never check out and run a fork's code in a privileged job
Third-party actions pinned to a mutable tagPin to a full commit SHA (Secure Hash Algorithm); a tag can be repointed by whoever owns it
Long-lived cloud keys stored as CI secretsOIDC (OpenID Connect) federation from the CI provider, scoped to repository and branch
Every job gets every secretScope secrets per environment and per job; most jobs need none
Self-hosted runners reused between jobsEphemeral runners — otherwise one job's leftovers are the next job's inputs
Untrusted input interpolated into a shell stepPass it as an environment variable and quote it; a branch or title can be a command
Caches shared across branchesA poisoned cache entry from any branch reaches the release build
Deployment credentials usable from any branchBind deploy identity to protected branches and tags only
The fork-pull-request row causes the worst outcomes because the vulnerable configuration exists for a good reason — maintainers want CI results on external contributions. The workable answer is running untrusted code without secrets and deferring anything privileged to a separate, approved workflow.
Troubleshooting
Internal Registries and the Dependency-Confusion Class
An internal registry is usually adopted for caching and availability, and it happens to be the control that stops several attacks at once — but only if it is configured to be authoritative rather than merely first in a list.
SettingReason
Never fall back to the public index for a name you ownThe whole dependency-confusion attack is that fallback
Own the namespace publicly too — register the scope or prefixRemoves the possibility rather than mitigating it
Do not merge internal and public packages into one indexA merged view lets a higher public version win on name alone
Cache immutably; never re-fetch a version already storedDefeats republished and tampered versions
Promote between repositories rather than rebuildingThe artifact tested is the artifact released
Make base and golden images rebuild on a scheduleOne place to patch — but only if something rebuilds it
The golden-image row is the one that quietly decays. A shared base image is genuinely the cheapest way to patch a fleet, and it becomes the cheapest way to ship a two-year-old vulnerability everywhere the moment nobody owns rebuilding it. If you adopt one, the rebuild schedule is part of the decision, not a follow-up task. Registry mechanics are in Artifact & Registry Management.
⚖️ Open Source Licences — Permissive, Copyleft & What Actually Obliges You ENG • Governance ▶
Concept
The Question Is Not "Which Licence" — It Is "Are You Distributing?"
Almost every anxious conversation about open-source licensing skips the step that decides the answer. Most obligations in most licences are triggered by distribution — giving software to someone outside your organisation. Running it internally, on your own servers, for your own staff, triggers very little in most licences. Ship a product, an installer, a container image to a customer, or firmware in a device, and the same code carries real requirements. Establish which of those you are doing first; the licence matrix only means something afterwards.
What you are doingTypically triggers
Running it on your own servers, internal usersAlmost nothing, in most licences
Offering it as a network service to othersNothing — except under AGPL-style terms, which is the whole point of them
Shipping a binary, an installer or an imageAttribution, licence text, and for copyleft the corresponding source
Shipping hardware with software on itThe same, plus installation information under some copyleft terms
Publishing your own source that includes theirsThe licence's compatibility rules now bind your project
Internal modifications you never releaseUsually nothing at all
The second row is why the AGPL exists and why some organisations ban it outright: ordinary copyleft was written when software was shipped, so running a modified version as a hosted service created an obligation-free loophole. AGPL closes it by treating network use as distribution. Whether that is a problem for you depends entirely on whether you would modify it and whether you offer it to others.
Reference
The Families, and What Each Asks For
Dozens of licences exist; they cluster into four groups, and knowing the group is most of what a practitioner needs. This is orientation, not legal advice — for anything you ship commercially, the answer comes from a lawyer who has read your actual situation.
FamilyExamplesAsks of you
PermissiveMIT, BSD, ISCKeep the notice and the licence text. That is broadly it
Permissive with a patent grantApache 2.0The above, plus state changes; gives explicit patent rights
Weak copyleftLGPL, MPL 2.0, EPLChanges to that component must be shared; your own code need not be
Strong copyleftGPL 2.0/3.0, AGPLDistribute a combined work and the whole thing carries the licence
Not open source at allSSPL, BUSL, "source available", most vendor community editionsRead them properly — several forbid exactly the use you had in mind
That last row has grown in importance. A number of well-known projects have relicensed to source-available terms that restrict offering the software as a service or require a commercial agreement above a threshold. They are frequently still described as "open source" in blog posts and dashboards. The practical habit: check the licence file in the version you are actually pulling, not the one you remember from three years ago.
Troubleshooting
Compliance Without a Legal Department
For most IT teams this is a small amount of ongoing hygiene rather than a project, and it is much cheaper before an acquisition, an audit or a customer questionnaire than during one.
PracticeDetail
Know what you ship versus what you runThe single distinction that scopes everything else
Generate an SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) and keep the licence fieldMost tooling reports licences alongside versions; that report *is* your inventory
Have a policy with a small allow list"Permissive and weak copyleft, ask before strong copyleft" fits on one page and answers 95% of cases
Ship a notices fileAttribution is the most commonly breached obligation and the easiest to satisfy
Watch for transitive licencesYour permissive dependency may pull a copyleft one four levels down
Re-check on relicensingA dependency changing licence in a minor version has happened repeatedly
Never paste licensed code into your own without checkingIncluding code an assistant suggested — the obligation follows the code, not the route it took
Get advice before shipping copyleft in a productThis is the one where being wrong is expensive
The transitive row is where teams are usually surprised, and it is the same mechanism as the security problem next door — you did not choose that dependency, and you carry it anyway. The inventory that answers both questions is the same artefact: SBOM in Practice & VEX — Querying It, and Saying "Not Affected" Credibly, alongside Dependency Risk — Transitive Depth, Typosquatting & the Abandoned Package. The commercial equivalent of the whole conversation is Licensing Models & Surviving an Audit — The True-Up Conversation.
📄 RFCs (Request for Comments) & Design Docs — Writing to Decide, Not to Record Craft ▶
Core Concept
Written Before the Decision, or It Is Documentation
A design doc circulated after the work has started is a status update wearing a different hat. The test is not the format and not the length — it is whether anyone was ever able to change the outcome by reading it. If the answer is no, the document is a record, which is a fine thing to have and a different thing from a decision-making tool.
SymptomWhat it means
The doc appears with the PRThe decision was made in the PR. The doc is a summary
“Any concerns?” with no alternatives listedThere is nothing to disagree with. Approval is the only available response
Comments are all on wordingReaders can tell the substance is settled, so they review what is still movable
A reviewer's objection changes the planThis is the signal it worked. Rare enough to be worth noticing
Nobody read itEither it was too long, or the audience knew the decision was already made
None of these symptoms mean the document was wasted. They mean it is doing a different job than its author thinks, and that nobody should treat the review as a decision gate. If the decision genuinely is made, say so in the first line — readers spend their attention differently on “deciding Thursday” than on “here is what we are doing”, and both are legitimate.
Structure
The Sections That Make It Reviewable
The exact template matters far less than whether it contains something a reader can push back on. These five do; a document that is all “design” does not.
SectionWhat it has to contain to be useful
ContextWhat is true today that makes this worth doing. The part that dates fastest and matters most later
Goals and non-goalsNon-goals do most of the work — they end the “but what about” comments before they start
Options consideredAt least two taken seriously. One option and a recommendation is a proposal, not a decision
Recommendation, with the trade acceptedSay what you are giving up. A recommendation with no cost reads as unexamined
Open questionsThe section that invites the comments you actually want, and the one usually deleted before circulation
The section people cut for length is non-goals, and it is the one that saves the most time. Half the comments on a design doc are about scope somebody assumed was included; three lines of non-goals removes them before they are written. Keep the whole thing under two pages — a document nobody finishes is not reviewable at any level of rigour.
Practice
Circulation Is a Design Decision Too
Most design docs fail on process rather than content. Sending a finished twelve-page document to a wide list on a Friday produces silence, and the silence gets read as agreement. Silence is the default outcome and it is not consent.
MoveWhy
Circulate at ~70% completeLate enough to be coherent, early enough that comments can change it
Name the decision and the date“Deciding Thursday” converts an open document into a deadline people act on
Ask two specific people directlyGeneral circulation gets general silence. A named request gets a reply
Hold a review meeting only if there is disagreementIf comments converge, the meeting is a ceremony. If they do not, it is the only thing that resolves it
Record the outcome where it will be foundA decided doc becomes an ADR (Architecture Decision Record). See ADRs & Design Docs
The pairing worth keeping straight: the design doc is the deliberation and the ADR is the residue. The doc is long, argumentative and read once; the ADR is short, states what was decided and what would reverse it, and is read for years. Writing only the first leaves nothing findable; writing only the second means the decision was never open to influence.
⚖️ Build vs Buy — The Question Behind the Question Architecture ▶
Core Concept
It Is Never Build Cost Against Licence Cost
The comparison as usually presented — six months of engineering time against an annual subscription — is not the comparison. It is total cost against total cost, and the side that is systematically understated is always the same one, because building has a large recurring cost that does not appear on the estimate that justified it.
What the estimate includesWhat it leaves out
The initial buildMaintenance, forever — and it does not decline
—The on-call burden when it breaks at 3 a.m.
—The person who wrote it leaving, and the six months of nobody understanding it
—Features the vendor ships that you will now be asked for
—Security patching of a thing nobody is watching for CVEs (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures)
The licence cost, accuratelyIntegration effort, and the exit cost when you eventually leave
The rule of thumb that survives contact with reality: the build estimate is the smallest number in the decision, and the maintenance is the largest. A useful discipline is to multiply the build estimate by the number of years the thing will exist and add 20% of the build per year for upkeep — not because that is precise, but because it puts a recurring number next to the recurring number on the other side.
Core Concept
The Question Behind the Question: Is This Your Differentiator?
Cost analysis rarely decides this and should not, because the two totals are usually within a factor of two and the estimates are worse than that. The question that actually decides it is strategic: would a customer ever choose you because of this thing?
If it is...Then
Your differentiatorBuild. Even at a cost premium — this is the thing you are for
Undifferentiated but criticalBuy, and integrate well. Identity, payments, email delivery, observability
Undifferentiated and non-criticalBuy the cheapest adequate thing, and do not think about it again
A commodity you keep customisingThe trap. You are building, slowly, while paying a licence
Something no vendor doesCheck that again. “Nobody sells this” is usually “we have not looked”
The fourth row is where most of the regret is. A bought system customised past recognition has both costs: the licence, and a maintenance burden on integration code that upgrades will keep breaking. When customisation exceeds roughly the effort of building the part you actually need, the decision has already been made and nobody has admitted it.
Practice
Buy Decisions Need an Exit, and Build Decisions Need an Owner
Both directions have one obligation that is routinely skipped, and skipping it is what turns a reasonable decision into a bad outcome three years later.
DirectionThe obligation
BuyKnow the exit. How does the data come out, in what format, and how long would migrating take?
BuyAvoid the deepest integrations where a shallow one suffices — every hook is exit cost
BuildA named owning team, funded past the launch. Unowned internal tools are the most expensive things an organisation runs
BuildA deprecation plan from the start. What would make us stop, and what happens to users then?
EitherRevisit on a date. Both decisions are correct under conditions that change
The asymmetry worth knowing: a buy decision is reversible at a cost you can estimate; a build decision is reversible only by finding somebody to take it over. That makes “buy” the safer default for anything outside your differentiator — not because building is bad, but because the failure mode of a bought system is a migration project, and the failure mode of a built one is an orphan nobody will admit to owning.
🧭 The Staff Engineer Role — What It Is When It Is Not Management Career ▶
Core Concept
The Track Is Real and Badly Defined Nearly Everywhere
Most organisations write a Staff+ ladder by taking the management ladder and removing the people management, which produces a description of a senior engineer with more adjectives. The actual difference is not seniority, it is scope of impact — and specifically, work that requires no direct authority and that nobody else in the organisation is positioned to do.
LevelThe unit of work
SeniorA feature, a service, a project. Delivered well, largely alone or leading a couple of people
StaffA problem that crosses teams and that nobody owns, because it belongs to no single team
PrincipalA problem that crosses the organisation, often multi-year, often about direction rather than delivery
The common mistakeDefining Staff as “a very good senior”, which makes the promotion about tenure
The other mistakeDefining it as “a manager who codes”, which is a fourth job nobody wanted
The test that separates the first two rows cleanly: could a senior engineer on one team have done this, if they were excellent? If yes, it is senior work done well. If it required standing between three teams who disagreed, or seeing a problem none of them could see from inside, that is the other track — and it is a difference in kind, not in degree.
Practice
What the Work Actually Is
The day looks nothing like a senior engineer's, and the surprise for people entering the role is how little of it is writing code — without it being management either.
ActivityWhy it belongs to this role
Writing the design doc nobody was going to writeA cross-team decision has no owner by definition. See RFCs & Design Docs — Writing to Decide, Not to Record
Reviewing other teams' designsConsistency across an estate is an outcome only somebody spanning it can produce
Unblocking, not deliveringTwo days spent making four teams faster beats two days of your own output
Being the person who says the uncomfortable thingWithout authority, and therefore credibly. A manager saying it is politics; an engineer saying it is engineering
Actually writing code, sometimesPrototypes that settle an argument, and the hard part nobody else will pick up
The proportion that surprises people is the third row. Most of the value is other people's output, not yours, which makes the role uncomfortable for anyone whose sense of a good week is measured in merged pull requests — and that discomfort, rather than the technical difficulty, is what makes the transition hard.
Practice
The Failure Modes, and How the Role Goes Wrong
Three ways this role fails, and all three are structural rather than personal.
FailureWhat it looks like
No scope grantedGiven the title and left on one team. The work requires access to problems, and access is granted rather than taken
Becoming a bottleneckEvery design routed through one person. Influence that does not scale is just a queue
Drifting into management without the titleCoordination expands to fill the day, and the technical judgement that justified the role decays
No visible artefactsInfluence is invisible in a promotion packet unless it left documents behind — see Staff+ Archetypes
LonelinessThe honest one. The role has no peers on the team and no cohort, and organisations rarely build one
The advice that most reliably helps someone in the role: write things down, and make other people faster in ways that outlast the conversation. A design doc, a standard, a tool, a written review — those are how influence compounds and how it becomes visible. Advice given in meetings helps once and is invisible in six months.
🎯 Goals That Survive the Quarter — OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) Without the Theatre Leading Teams ▶
Core Concept
Most Goal Frameworks Fail the Same Three Ways
The framework is rarely the problem. Almost every failed goal-setting exercise fails through the same three mechanisms, and they are independent of whether you call them OKRs, V2MOM or anything else.
FailureWhat it looks like
Outputs written as outcomes“Ship the new dashboard” is a task with a date. “Support answers 30% fewer 'where is my order' tickets” is an outcome
Targets set to be achievableIf every goal lands at 100%, they were commitments. A measure that cannot go the wrong way is not a measure
A review nobody attendsSet in January, read in December. The value is in the mid-quarter conversation, which is the part that gets cancelled
Too manySix goals is a list of everything the team was going to do anyway, relabelled
No named owner“The team” owns it, so nobody reports on it
The second row is the one people resist and it is the load-bearing one. A goal you are confident of hitting carries no information, and a set of them is a plan with a scoring system attached. The point of a target is to make you change what you would otherwise have done.
Practice
Fewer Goals, a Measure That Could Move Against You, a Named Person
The corrective for each failure is small, and all three together take about an hour a quarter.
FixWhy it works
Two or three goals, not sixForces the prioritisation conversation that the list was avoiding
A metric with a plausible bad outcome“p95 latency under 300ms” can fail. “Improve performance” cannot
One named person per goalNot an owner of the work — an owner of reporting the number
A mid-quarter checkpoint in the calendarBooked at the same time the goals are set, or it does not happen
Written down where the work isA goal in a slide deck is invisible; one at the top of the team's board is read weekly
The cheapest of the five is the fourth, and it is the one that makes the others matter: book the mid-quarter review in the same meeting where the goals are written. Goals fail silently between the setting and the scoring, and the only mechanism that catches that is a date already in the calendar when everyone still cares.
Practice
What to Do When a Goal Is Going to Miss
The response to a goal that is failing is where the framework earns its keep or becomes theatre, and the honest options are three rather than one.
SituationThe right response
The goal is right, the plan is wrongChange the plan. This is the intended outcome of the mid-quarter check
The goal is wrong — the world changedChange the goal, and say so in writing. Pretending otherwise wastes a quarter of everyone's attention
The goal is right and unreachable this quarterKeep it, report the miss, carry it. A miss with a number is more useful than a hit with a redefinition
Quietly redefining the metricThe failure mode that destroys the whole exercise — after one instance, nobody believes any of the numbers
Dropping it silentlySame, slower
The cultural precondition is the fourth row and it cannot be added later: if missing a goal is punished, every goal will be set to be met, and the framework converts into an elaborate way of writing down what was going to happen anyway. That is a leadership behaviour rather than a process choice, and no amount of tooling substitutes for it.
