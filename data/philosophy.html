🏛️ Stoicism — The Art of Rational Endurance PHILOSOPHY ▶
CORE
The Dichotomy of Control
Focus only on what is up to you — your judgments and actions — and accept the rest. Live by the four virtues: wisdom, justice, courage, temperance.
FIGURES
The Big Three
Marcus Aurelius (Meditations), Epictetus (Enchiridion), and Seneca (Letters). Roots in Zeno of Citium, c. 300 BCE.
PRACTICE
Daily Exercises
Negative visualization (premeditatio malorum), memento mori (remember mortality), and amor fati (love your fate) build resilience and gratitude.
Ancient Greek · Roman
Core Doctrines & Daily Practice
Founded ~300 BC by Zeno of Citium. Stoics held that virtue alone is sufficient for happiness and that emotions rooted in false judgements must be overcome through reason.
▸ THE FOUR CARDINAL VIRTUES
Wisdom (Sophia)
Knowing what is good, bad, and indifferent. The foundation of all other virtues.
Justice (Dikaiosyne)
Acting rightly toward others. Social duty; fairness in all dealings.
Courage (Andreia)
Facing hardship, pain, and death without flinching. Moral as well as physical.
Temperance (Sophrosyne)
Self-discipline and moderation. Mastery over appetites and impulses.
▸ CORE PRINCIPLES
Principle Meaning Practice
Dichotomy of Control Some things are "up to us" (prohairesis) — judgements, impulses, desires. Everything else is external and indifferent. Ask "Is this in my control?" before reacting to any event.
Memento Mori "Remember you will die." Mortality awareness as a tool for gratitude and urgency. Morning reflection: treat each day as a gift, not a given.
Amor Fati "Love of fate." Accept and embrace everything that happens — not mere tolerance, but affirmation. Reframe obstacles as opportunities; say "it was meant to be" and mean it.
Negative Visualization (Premeditatio Malorum) Deliberately imagine loss — of health, relationships, possessions — to appreciate what you have. Evening: briefly imagine losing what you value most, then feel gratitude for its presence.
Sympatheia All things are interconnected in one rational whole (the Logos). We are citizens of the universe. When frustrated by others: "They act from ignorance, not malice. I would do the same without better judgement."
▸ KEY FIGURES & TEXTS
Figure Era Key Work Contribution
Marcus Aurelius 121–180 AD Meditations Roman Emperor. Private journal of self-discipline; applied Stoicism in governance.
Epictetus 50–135 AD Enchiridion, Discourses Former slave. Focused on inner freedom regardless of external circumstance.
Seneca 4 BC–65 AD Letters to Lucilius, On the Shortness of Life Statesman and playwright. Practical moral essays on time, wealth, and death.
Zeno of Citium 334–262 BC — Founder of the Stoic school in Athens' painted porch (Stoa Poikile).
Start with Epictetus, not Marcus Aurelius. The Meditations are a private journal — brilliant, unsystematic, and written by somebody already fluent — whereas the Enchiridion was written to be taught from, which is why it survives as the entry point. Seneca is the one to read when the abstraction stops helping: the letters are practical, and concern money, illness, grief and time.
▸ DAILY STOIC PRACTICE
AM
Morning Journal
What obstacles might I face today? How will I respond virtuously? Rehearse the day — adversities and all.
MID
Pause & Label
When emotion arises, name it: "I notice anger." The gap between stimulus and response is where virtue lives.
PM
Evening Review
What did I do well? What could I have done better? No self-flagellation — just honest assessment. (Seneca's method)
♟️ Machiavellianism — Power, Realism & Strategy POLITICAL PHILOSOPHY ▶
SOURCE
The Prince (1532)
Niccolò Machiavelli analyzed power as it is, not as it should be. A ruler must master virtù (skill/decisiveness) to manage fortuna (chance).
IDEAS
Realpolitik
Outcomes can justify hard means; it is safer to be feared than loved if you cannot be both. Pragmatic statecraft over idealism.
NOTE
Two Meanings
Distinguish the political philosophy from the psychology term 'Machiavellianism' — a dark-triad trait describing manipulative, self-serving behavior.
Niccolò Machiavelli · 1469–1527
The Prince & the Discourses — Realpolitik Framework
Machiavelli separated political science from ethics. His work describes how power actually operates, not how it ideally should. Often misread as endorsing cruelty; actually a clinical observer of political reality.
▸ CORE CONCEPTS
Concept Meaning
Virtù Not "virtue" but prowess — strength, skill, cunning, willpower. The ruler's capacity to shape events through force of character and action.
Fortuna Fortune/chance — the unpredictable half of all outcomes. Virtù can master ~50% of fate; the rest is Fortuna. She favors the bold.
The Lion & the Fox A ruler must be both: the lion to frighten wolves (force), the fox to recognize traps (cunning). Neither alone is sufficient.
Economy of Violence Cruelties must be committed all at once; benefits distributed gradually. Sudden harshness is quickly forgotten; lingering harm festers.
Appearance vs. Reality It is unnecessary to actually possess virtues — it is sufficient to appear to have them. People judge by what they see, not what is.
Love vs. Fear It is safer to be feared than loved if you cannot be both. But avoid being hated — hatred is fatal; fear can be managed.
▸ THE 48 LAWS — ROBERT GREENE'S SYNTHESIS
Never Outshine the Master
Always make those above you feel superior. Conceal your talent when needed.
Conceal Your Intentions
Keep people off-balance. Reveal purpose only when it cannot be stopped.
Guard Your Reputation
Reputation is the cornerstone of power. A single crack and you are vulnerable.
Play on People's Need to Believe
People crave meaning. Offer a cause, a vision — they will follow willingly.
▸ DARK TRIAD CONTEXT (Psychology)
Trait Core Distinction
Machiavellianism Cynical worldview, strategic manipulation, delayed gratification Calculating and patient; manipulates for long-term gain
Narcissism Grandiosity, entitlement, need for admiration Emotional; manipulates for ego supply and status
Psychopathy Low empathy, impulsivity, callousness Reactive; least strategic of the three
The distinction column is the part with practical use. Manipulation that is patient and strategic looks nothing like manipulation driven by ego or by impulse, and mistaking one for another produces exactly the wrong response — you cannot reason somebody out of a status need, and you cannot appeal to a long game they are not playing. All three describe traits on a spectrum, not diagnoses.
🧠 Psychology — Mind, Behavior & Biases COGNITIVE SCIENCE ▶
SCHOOLS
Major Approaches
Psychodynamic (unconscious drives), behavioral (conditioning), cognitive (thinking patterns), and humanistic (growth & self-actualization).
APPLIED
Useful Frameworks
CBT links thoughts→feelings→behavior. Classical vs. operant conditioning explains learned responses. Maslow's hierarchy ranks human needs. The DSM-5 is the diagnostic standard (for clinicians, not self-diagnosis).
Cognitive BiasWhat It Is
Confirmation biasFavoring info that fits beliefs
AnchoringOver-weighting the first number seen
Dunning-KrugerLow skill, high confidence
Sunk costContinuing because of past investment
AvailabilityJudging by what comes to mind easily
Knowing the names does almost nothing; building one check does. Every bias here remains fully operational in people who can define it, which is why the useful move is structural — a written prediction before the result is known, a devil's advocate with a mandate, a decision log. Pick the one that costs you most and design a small procedure against it.
Applied Psychology
Cognitive Biases · Defense Mechanisms · Frameworks
Psychology studies the mind and behavior. Understanding your cognitive architecture is the first step to overcoming it. "Knowing yourself is the beginning of all wisdom." — Aristotle
▸ KEY COGNITIVE BIASES
Bias Description Example
Confirmation Bias Seek info that confirms existing beliefs; ignore contradicting evidence Only reading news aligned with your politics
Dunning-Kruger Effect Incompetent people overestimate ability; experts underestimate theirs Beginner investor convinced they've outsmarted the market
Sunk Cost Fallacy Continue bad decisions because of past investment (time/money/effort) Finishing a terrible book because you've read half of it
Availability Heuristic Judge probability by how easily examples come to mind Fear flying after seeing news of a crash; ignoring car risk
Fundamental Attribution Error Over-attribute others' behavior to character; under-attribute to situation "They cut me off — they're a bad person" (vs. they're late for hospital)
Anchoring Over-rely on first piece of information encountered $100 item marked down to $70 feels like a deal regardless of value
Negativity Bias Negative events register more strongly than equally positive ones One criticism outweighs ten compliments
Halo Effect One positive trait causes assumption of other positive traits Attractive people assumed to be more competent
▸ EGO DEFENSE MECHANISMS (Freud / Anna Freud)
Mechanism Description
Repression Unconsciously pushing threatening thoughts out of awareness
Rationalization Creating logical explanations to justify unacceptable behavior
Projection Attributing your own unacceptable feelings to others
Denial Refusing to acknowledge a painful reality
Displacement Redirecting emotion from original source to safer target
Sublimation Channeling unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activity (e.g., aggression → sport)
These describe normal functioning, not a list of faults. Everybody uses all six; the question is whether a mechanism is occasional and proportionate, or is doing the work a conversation should be doing. Sublimation is on the list because it is the one that works — the same impulse routed somewhere it produces something.
▸ KEY FRAMEWORKS
Maslow's Hierarchy
Physiological → Safety → Love/Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization. Lower needs must be satisfied before higher ones motivate.
Locus of Control
Internal: outcomes result from your actions. External: outcomes are luck/fate. Internal LoC correlates strongly with resilience and achievement.
Growth vs Fixed Mindset
Carol Dweck. Fixed: ability is innate and static. Growth: ability is developed through effort. Growth mindset predicts higher achievement and resilience.
Flow State (Csikszentmihalyi)
Optimal experience at the intersection of high challenge + high skill. Time distorts. Deep absorption. Intrinsic reward. Cultivated through deliberate practice.
📜 Philosophy — Schools of Thought WESTERN TRADITION ▶
Epistemology · Metaphysics · Ethics · Logic
Major Schools & Key Thinkers
▸ MAJOR SCHOOLS
School Era Core Claim Key Figures
Rationalism 17th–18th c. Knowledge derived primarily from reason, not senses. Innate ideas exist. Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz
Empiricism 17th–18th c. All knowledge derives from sensory experience. The mind starts as a blank slate (tabula rasa). Locke, Hume, Berkeley
Pragmatism 19th–20th c. Truth is what works in practice. Ideas are tools; their value is in their consequences. Peirce, James, Dewey
Existentialism 20th c. Existence precedes essence. Individuals create their own meaning in an indifferent universe. Sartre, Camus, Heidegger, de Beauvoir
Utilitarianism 18th–19th c. The morally right action maximizes overall happiness (utility) for the greatest number. Bentham, Mill
Kantian Ethics (Deontology) 18th c. Act only according to maxims you could will to be universal laws. Duty above consequences. Immanuel Kant
Virtue Ethics Ancient Greek Focus on developing good character (virtues) rather than rules or outcomes. Aristotle, MacIntyre
Nihilism 19th–20th c. Life has no intrinsic meaning, purpose, or value. Traditional morality is groundless. Nietzsche (critic), Cioran
▸ ESSENTIAL CONCEPTS
Concept Definition
Cogito ergo sum "I think, therefore I am." Descartes' foundational certainty — even a deceiver cannot doubt the existence of the doubter.
Categorical Imperative Kant: act only on maxims that could become universal laws. Treat humanity never merely as means but always as ends.
Absurdism Camus: life is inherently meaningless yet humans persistently seek meaning — this conflict is the Absurd. Response: revolt (not suicide, not faith).
Veil of Ignorance Rawls: design a just society without knowing your position in it. Ensures impartiality — you'd design fairness since you might be disadvantaged.
Übermensch Nietzsche's "Overman" — one who creates their own values after the "death of God." Not a racial concept; a call to self-overcoming.
Solipsism Only one's own mind is certain to exist. All else may be illusion. A logical extreme rarely endorsed but philosophically significant.
A school is a set of questions somebody took seriously, not a set of answers to adopt. Read as a menu the table invites picking one and arguing from it, which is how the words become labels; read as a map it shows which problem each tradition thought was the urgent one — what exists, what can be known, what a good life is — and why answering one well constrains the answers to the others. The thinkers listed disagreed with their own schools more often than the row suggests, which is worth remembering before quoting one.
☸️ Buddhism — The Path to Liberation SPIRITUAL PHILOSOPHY ▶
CORE
Four Noble Truths
Life contains suffering (dukkha); craving causes it; it can cease; and the Eightfold Path is the way out. Reality is marked by impermanence and non-self (anatta).
SCHOOLS
Traditions
Theravada (elder teaching), Mahayana (great vehicle), Zen (direct insight), and Vajrayana (Tibetan). Meditation (samatha & vipassana) is central.
Eightfold PathGroup
Right View / IntentionWisdom
Right Speech / Action / LivelihoodEthics
Right Effort / Mindfulness / ConcentrationMental discipline
The grouping is the useful structure: wisdom, ethics, mental discipline. The eight are not a sequence to complete but three mutually supporting kinds of practice, which is why treating the path as a checklist misses it. The ethics group is not preliminary to the meditation group — the tradition holds that each depends on the other.
Siddhartha Gautama · ~563–483 BC
Four Noble Truths · Eightfold Path · Core Concepts
Buddhism is both a religion and a philosophy. Its goal is the cessation of dukkha (suffering) through understanding the nature of mind and reality. Not theistic in the Western sense — the Buddha is a teacher, not a god.
▸ THE FOUR NOBLE TRUTHS (Catvāri Āryasatyāni)
1
Dukkha (Suffering)
Life inherently contains suffering, dissatisfaction, and imperfection. Birth, aging, sickness, death, getting what we don't want, not getting what we want — all are dukkha.
2
Samudāya (Origin)
The origin of suffering is craving (tanha) — for pleasure, for existence, for non-existence. Attachment and aversion are the roots.
3
Nirodha (Cessation)
Suffering can cease. Nirvana is the complete extinguishing of craving — a state of peace, freedom, and liberation.
4
Magga (The Path)
The Noble Eightfold Path is the way to end suffering. Practical and systematic — not metaphysical belief but lived practice.
▸ THE NOBLE EIGHTFOLD PATH
Path Category Meaning
Right View Wisdom Understanding the Four Noble Truths; seeing reality as it is, not as we wish it to be.
Right Intention Wisdom Commitment to non-attachment, non-ill-will, and non-harmfulness.
Right Speech Ethics Abstaining from lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter.
Right Action Ethics Abstaining from killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct.
Right Livelihood Ethics Earning a living that does not cause harm to others or oneself.
Right Effort Meditation Cultivating wholesome states and abandoning unwholesome ones continuously.
Right Mindfulness Meditation Present-moment awareness of body, feelings, mind-states, and phenomena.
Right Concentration Meditation Developing deep meditative absorption (jhanas) as foundation for insight.
Right Livelihood is the row that lands hardest on a modern reader, and the one most often skipped. The others concern conduct and attention; this one asks what your work does in the world, which is a question with consequences a private practice cannot absorb. Whatever your view of the tradition, it is a serious question most secular frameworks leave to somebody else.
▸ CORE CONCEPTS (THE THREE MARKS OF EXISTENCE)
Anicca — Impermanence
All conditioned phenomena are impermanent. Clinging to what is transient is the source of suffering. Release attachment.
Dukkha — Unsatisfactoriness
Even pleasant experiences carry an undercurrent of dissatisfaction because they end. Nothing conditioned can permanently satisfy.
Anatta — Non-Self
The "self" is not a fixed, permanent entity but a changing process of aggregates (skandhas). Liberating when truly understood.
☯️ Taoism — The Way of Nature CHINESE PHILOSOPHY ▶
CORE
The Tao & Wu Wei
The Tao is the natural way underlying everything. Wu wei — effortless action — means moving with nature's flow rather than forcing it.
FIGURES
Texts & Symbols
Lao Tzu's Tao Te Ching and Zhuangzi's writings are foundational. Yin-yang expresses complementary opposites in dynamic balance.
Lao Tzu · ~6th–4th c. BC
Tao Te Ching — The Classic of the Way and Its Power
The Tao (道) — the Way — is the fundamental, unnamed principle underlying all of reality. It cannot be named or fully grasped, only lived. "The Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao."
▸ CORE PRINCIPLES
Principle Meaning Application
Wu Wei (無為) "Non-action" or effortless action. Acting in harmony with the natural flow rather than forcing outcomes. Avoid unnecessary struggle. Like water: yield, go around, persist gently.
Pu (樸) — Simplicity The uncarved block. Original nature before social conditioning. Simplicity as strength. Strip away pretense. Return to what is essential and natural.
Te (德) — Virtue/Power Inner virtue or power that arises naturally when aligned with the Tao. Not morality imposed from outside but integrity from within. Be genuinely good, not performatively good.
Yin-Yang (陰陽) All opposites are interdependent and contain each other's seed. Darkness within light, light within darkness. Change is constant. Stop seeing circumstances as purely good or bad. Both are transforming.
Ziran (自然) Self-so, naturalness. Things should be allowed to follow their own nature. Stop trying to control what is inherently wild and self-organizing.
Wu wei is the row most often mistranslated into passivity, and that reading makes the rest incoherent. Effortless action is action that goes with the grain of a situation rather than against it — the water image is about persistence and shape, not about doing nothing. Held that way, the other four rows read as advice about where effort is wasted.
▸ WATER AS TAOIST METAPHOR
Water is the supreme Taoist metaphor. It is:
· Soft yet wears down the hardest rock (persistence)
· Low — it always seeks the lowest point (humility)
· Yielding — flows around obstacles rather than fighting them
· Essential — all life depends on it, yet it does not demand recognition
"Nothing in the world is as soft and yielding as water, yet nothing is better at dissolving the hard and inflexible."
🌑 Existentialism & Absurdism 20TH C. PHILOSOPHY ▶
CORE
Existence Precedes Essence
We are not born with a fixed purpose; we create meaning through free choices and bear full responsibility for them.
THEMES
Freedom, Absurd, Authenticity
Camus framed the absurd (seeking meaning in a silent universe); the answer is to live authentically rather than in 'bad faith'.
FIGURES
Key Thinkers
Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Sartre, Camus, and Simone de Beauvoir — spanning religious and atheist existentialism.
Sartre · Camus · Kierkegaard · Heidegger
"Existence precedes essence" — Building meaning in a meaningless universe
Existentialism holds that there is no pre-given human nature or purpose. We exist first, then create ourselves through choices. This is both terrifying (radical freedom) and liberating.
▸ KEY CONCEPTS
Concept Thinker Meaning
Radical Freedom Sartre We are "condemned to be free" — there is no escape from choice. Even refusing to choose is a choice. Total responsibility for self.
Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi) Sartre Pretending you have no choice; adopting a fixed identity to escape freedom. E.g., "I can't help it, that's just who I am."
The Absurd Camus The conflict between humans' need for meaning and the universe's silent indifference. Three responses: physical suicide (wrong), philosophical suicide (religion — bad faith), or revolt (the only authentic response).
Authenticity Heidegger Owning your existence — facing death (Being-toward-death) rather than fleeing into das Man (the "they-self" of conformity).
Leap of Faith Kierkegaard Rational thought cannot resolve the deepest questions. Authentic existence requires a passionate, subjective commitment despite uncertainty.
Thrownness (Geworfenheit) Heidegger We are "thrown" into existence — we did not choose our time, place, language, culture. But we are responsible for what we do with this situation.
Bad faith is the concept with the most immediate use, because it names something you can catch yourself doing. "I had no choice" is almost always a claim about the cost of a choice rather than its absence, and noticing the substitution is the practical half of the whole school. The rest of the table is the metaphysics that makes the point; that row is where it touches a Tuesday.
▸ THE MYTH OF SISYPHUS — CAMUS
Sisyphus is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill for eternity — it always rolls back down. Camus argues this is the human condition.

The resolution: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
Not because the task is meaningful in any cosmic sense, but because the struggle itself fills his heart. Revolt against absurdity — continuing to live, create, love — is the only authentic response.
🧭Mental Models & Folk WisdomLifestyle • Practical Wisdom▶
BOUNDARIES
“Not my circus, not my monkeys.”
A Polish proverb (nie mój cyrk, nie moje małpy) meaning: this drama isn’t mine to manage. When a mess belongs to someone else — their emotions, their choices, their chaos — you can still care without taking ownership of it. Before leaping in to fix things, ask whose circus this actually is. It pairs perfectly with the Stoic dichotomy of control: spend your energy on your own monkeys.
VERIFY
“Assume makes an ass out of u and me.”
Break the word apart — ASS·U·ME — and it’s a built-in memory hook: unchecked assumptions end up embarrassing everyone involved. Don’t fill gaps in what you know with guesses; ask, confirm, and write it down. In IT especially, assuming ‘someone else handled it’ or ‘that config is fine’ is how outages are born. Trade “I assumed…” for “Let me verify…”
ACCEPTANCE
“You can’t make someone make the right choice — but you can pick up the pieces afterward.”
You don’t control other people’s decisions, only your response to them. Advise, warn, and offer help — then release the outcome, because it was never yours to steer. And when their choice goes wrong, you can still show up with grace and help them rebuild. It’s the dichotomy of control plus compassion: let go of a steering wheel you were never holding, and keep your hands free to help pick up the pieces.
SayingCore LessonUse It When…
Not my circus, not my monkeysHealthy boundariesYou’re tempted to own someone else’s drama
Assume = ass / u / meVerify, don’t guessYou catch yourself filling in unknowns
Can’t make the right choice…Release + compassionYou’re trying to control another’s decision
The third saying is the hardest and the one that keeps you both sane and employable. Advise clearly, in writing, then accept that the decision was never yours — and stay willing to help when it goes badly. The first two protect your attention; that one protects the relationships, which outlast any particular disagreement.
🧠More Models for a Clear HeadLifestyle • Decision-Making▶
CHARITY
Hanlon’s Razor
“Never attribute to malice what is adequately explained by carelessness or a bad day.” Most slights aren’t attacks — they’re someone rushed, tired, or unaware. Assuming clumsiness over evil lowers your stress and usually turns out to be correct.
SIMPLICITY
Occam’s Razor
When you have competing explanations, start with the simplest one that fits the facts. The server is probably unplugged before it’s a sophisticated hacker. Simple guesses are faster to test — rule them out first.
CONTROL
The Serenity Principle
Sort every worry into two buckets: things I can change and things I can’t. Act on the first bucket; make peace with the second. Almost all anxiety comes from pouring effort into the wrong bucket.
FOCUS
Circle of Control
Picture three rings: what you control (your actions), what you influence (others, by asking), and what you only care about (the weather, the economy). Push your attention inward toward the center — that’s where your power actually is.
PROGRESS
Done Beats Perfect
Perfectionism is procrastination in a nice outfit. A finished, slightly rough thing helps people today; a perfect thing that never ships helps no one. Ship it, then improve it.
🧭Decision-MakingLifestyle • Judgment▶
DOORS
One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors
Some decisions are reversible (a two-way door — try it, walk back if wrong) and some aren’t (a one-way door). Spend real deliberation on the one-way doors; move fast and experiment on the rest. Most choices are two-way.
TRADE-OFFS
Opportunity Cost
Saying yes to one thing is saying no to everything else you could do with that time or money. The real cost of a choice isn’t just its price — it’s the best thing you gave up to make it.
ENOUGH
Good Enough Beats Endless Search
Chasing the absolute best (maximizing) often costs more than it returns. Decide what ‘good enough’ looks like up front, pick the first option that clears the bar, and move on. Done decisions free your mind.
PAUSE
Sleep On the Big Ones
Strong emotion is a bad time to decide. For anything major, give it a night; tomorrow’s calmer brain sees options today’s couldn’t. Urgency is often manufactured — real deadlines survive a good night’s sleep.
🌙 Wicca — Sabbats, Elements & Tools EARTH-BASED PRACTICE ▶
CORE
Rede & Threefold Law
A modern nature-based religion popularized by Gerald Gardner. The Wiccan Rede — 'an it harm none, do what ye will' — and the Threefold Law (energy returns thrice) guide ethics.
PRACTICE
Deity & Ritual
Many honor a Goddess and God and mark eight seasonal sabbats. Ritual tools include the athame, chalice, wand, and pentacle.

Wicca is a modern Pagan religion codified by Gerald Gardner (1954) and further shaped by Doreen Valiente. It centres on reverence for nature, polarity of Goddess and God, seasonal cycles, and magical practice.

Wiccan Rede: "An it harm none, do what ye will."  ·  Rule of Three: Energy sent returns threefold.

☀️ Paganism — Traditions & Core Concepts EARTH-BASED TRADITION ▶
CORE
A Broad Family
An umbrella for nature-centered and often polytheistic or animistic spiritualities outside the Abrahamic mainstream. Reverence for nature and cycles is common.
PATHS
Reconstructionism
Many revive historical traditions: Heathenry / Ásatrú (Norse), Hellenism (Greek), Kemeticism (Egyptian), and Celtic paths.
Overview
Contemporary Paganism — Nature-Based Spirituality
Paganism is a broad category of earth-centred, polytheistic, or animistic spiritual traditions. Common themes: reverence for nature, cyclical time, pre-Christian deities or archetypes, and personal spiritual sovereignty. No single holy text or central authority governs all paths.

The hard-versus-soft distinction inside the polytheism row is the one that changes conversations. Whether deities are understood as literally distinct beings or as archetypes is a genuine difference in kind, and two people using identical vocabulary can mean entirely different things by it. Establish which is meant before assuming either agreement or disagreement.
🌳 Druidism — Grades, Awen & Sacred Concepts EARTH-BASED PRACTICE ▶
CORE
Modern Druidry
A contemporary nature spirituality inspired by Celtic culture, honoring the land, ancestors, and the changing seasons. Awen names creative/divine inspiration.
PRACTICE
Community & Cycle
Practitioners gather in groves and observe seasonal festivals. Modern orders include OBOD and ADF; emphasis is on creativity, ecology, and reflection.
Tradition
Druidry — Celtic Spiritual Path
Druidry draws on Celtic heritage, nature mysticism, and Bardic oral tradition. Modern revival began in the 18th century. Major orders: OBOD (Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids, est. 1964) and ADF (Ár nDraíocht Féin, est. 1983). Awen (Welsh: /ˈaʊ.ɛn/) — divine inspiration flowing through all things; symbolised by three rays of light ☀.

⬜ Minimalism & Intentional Living LIFESTYLE ▶
CORE
Less, But Better
Own and do fewer things, chosen deliberately, so attention and resources go to what matters most. Subtraction as a path to clarity.
PRACTICE
Applying It
Declutter to essentials, resist mindless consumption, and extend the idea to time and screens (digital minimalism: tools serve values, not the reverse).
Modern Practice
Intentionality · Essentialism · Deep Work
▸ CORE PRINCIPLES
Essentialism (Greg McKeown)
The disciplined pursuit of less. Not about doing more things — about doing the right things. "If it's not a clear yes, it's a no."
Deep Work (Cal Newport)
Cognitively demanding tasks performed in a state of distraction-free concentration. The skill of the 21st century. Rare + valuable = leverage.
KonMari Method
Keep only what "sparks joy." Tidy by category, not location. The goal: create an environment that supports who you want to become.
Parkinson's Law
Work expands to fill the time allotted. Impose artificial constraints. Deadlines create focus. Less time + clear priority = better output.
▸ HIGH-PERFORMANCE LIFESTYLE FRAMEWORKS
Framework Key Idea Source
80/20 Principle (Pareto) 80% of results come from 20% of efforts. Identify and double down on the vital few. Ruthlessly eliminate the trivial many. Vilfredo Pareto / Tim Ferriss
Atomic Habits Systems over goals. 1% better every day = 37x better in a year. Identity-based habits: "I am a writer" vs "I want to write." James Clear
Ikigai Japanese: reason for being. The intersection of what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. Japanese philosophy

The fourth row belongs beside the other three rather than after them. Systems, leverage and purpose are all optimisation; remember to live is the counterweight that stops the optimisation becoming the point — and it comes from the same tradition as the memento mori people quote far more often. A framework collection with no counterweight produces a very efficient life nobody wanted.
⚖️ Ethics — The Three Families, and Using Them On a Real Decision WESTERN TRADITION ▶
Concept
Three Questions, Not Three Verdicts
Moral philosophy is usually taught as a contest between theories, with the reader invited to pick a winner. That framing is why most people find it useless: nobody actually reasons from a single theory, and the theories disagree in ways two thousand years have not settled. The version that helps is to treat each as a question worth asking about the decision in front of you — three different lights on the same object, where disagreement between them is information rather than a problem.
FamilyAsksJudges
ConsequentialismWhat outcome does this produce, for everyone affected?The results
DeontologyWhat duties and rights are in play, regardless of outcome?The act itself
Virtue ethicsWhat would a person of good character do — and what does doing this make me?The agent
Where all three agree, the decision is easy and you did not need the framework. Where they conflict is exactly where the hard cases live, and naming which one you are following — and what you are therefore giving up — is most of what ethical reasoning actually consists of.
Reference
What Each One Gets Right, and Where It Breaks
Every family has a case that makes it obviously correct and a case that makes it obviously monstrous. Knowing both is what stops you being captured by whichever you read most recently.
FamilyStrongest whereBreaks where
Consequentialism (Bentham, Mill)Policy and scale — vaccination, road safety, resource allocation. It takes everyone's interests seriously and it can be argued with using evidenceIt will justify sacrificing an individual for an aggregate, and it needs a prediction of the future that nobody has
Deontology (Kant)Rights, consent and promises. It explains why "it would have worked out well" is not a defence for lying to someoneAbsolute rules collide — and when two duties conflict it offers no way to rank them
Virtue ethics (Aristotle)Character over time, and the questions that are not decisions at all: what kind of colleague, parent, professional am I becoming?It gives thin guidance in a specific dilemma at 3 p.m. on a Tuesday
A fourth is worth naming because it is the one people reach for without knowing it: contractualism — could I justify this to everyone affected, in terms they could not reasonably reject? It is the instinct behind "how would I explain this to the person it happens to", and it is often the most usable test of the four.
Concept
Running It On a Decision a Technical Person Actually Faces
You have found a serious flaw in a product used by hospitals. The vendor has not replied in ninety days. Do you publish?
LensWhat it says
ConsequentialistCompare futures. Publishing may force a fix and may arm an attacker; silence may leave it exploited quietly for years. It demands an honest estimate of which is likelier, not a preferred story
DeontologicalWho has a right to know? Patients did not consent to this risk. The vendor's interest in reputation is not a right that outweighs it, and the researcher's promise of confidentiality — if one was made — is a duty that does
VirtueWhat does a serious professional do? Not the person who publishes for attention, and not the one who stays silent to avoid trouble. Somewhere in between there is a person who keeps trying and eventually says so publicly
ContractualistCould you justify your choice to a patient, to the vendor's engineers, and to the next researcher? That test rules out both the loudest and the most convenient options quickly
Notice what happened: the four lenses do not deliver an answer, they generate the considerations you would otherwise have skipped — and they make the trade-off explicit rather than implicit. That is the practical value, and it is why Responsible Disclosure & Bug Bounties — Reporting a Flaw Without Becoming the Story reads as a set of conventions rather than a rule: the conventions are a profession's accumulated answer to this exact argument.
Concept
Three Traps That Look Like Ethical Reasoning
TrapWhat it actually is
"It is legal, so it is fine"Law is a floor negotiated by interested parties, and it lags the technology by years. Every practice later regarded as scandalous was legal at the time
"Everyone does it"A description of a market, not a justification. It also predicts that you will be among the ones caught when the norm changes
"I only built the tool"Sometimes true and worth taking seriously — a tool has many uses. It stops being true when the primary use is foreseeable and the design serves it
"I have no choice"Almost always means the alternatives are costly rather than absent. Say what the cost is; that is a real and defensible position, and it is a different claim
The most useful single habit is to notice when you have switched lenses mid-argument — arguing consequences while the consequences favour you, then switching to rights the moment they do not. It is the commonest failure in these discussions, it is easy to catch in yourself once you know the names, and catching it is worth more than picking a theory.
🧩 Arguments & Fallacies — Validity, Soundness & the Moves That Fake Both WESTERN TRADITION ▶
Concept
Valid and True Are Different Things, and the Difference Does Real Work
An argument is valid when the conclusion follows from the premises — a claim about structure alone, which says nothing about whether the premises are true. It is sound when it is valid and the premises are actually true. Nearly every unproductive argument is two people attacking different halves of that and not saying which. 
Valid, not sound:      All swans are white.  This is a swan.  → It is white.
                       Structure perfect. First premise false.

Sound:                 All squares have four sides.  This is a square.
                       → It has four sides.

Invalid, all true:     Servers need power.  This server is down.
                       → Therefore it has no power.
                       Every sentence true; the conclusion does not follow.
The third case is the one worth recognising, because it is what most bad reasoning looks like from inside: nothing false is said, and the conclusion is still unearned. "Which part are you disputing — the structure or a premise?" is a question that ends a surprising number of circular disagreements.
Reference
Three Kinds of Inference, and What Each Is Entitled To
Most real reasoning is not deductive at all, and expecting deductive certainty from it is a category error that produces both overconfidence and paralysis.
KindMoves fromGives youWhere it shows up
DeductionGeneral rules to a particular caseCertainty, if the premises holdMathematics, formal proof, contract logic
InductionObserved cases to a general patternProbability that grows with evidence and never reaches certaintyScience, capacity planning, "it has failed every Monday"
AbductionAn observation to its best explanationA hypothesis worth testing, and nothing moreDebugging. Diagnosis. Detective work
Abduction is the one people mistake for proof. "The deploy went out at 14:00 and the errors started at 14:02, so the deploy caused it" is a good hypothesis and not a finding. Treating it as a finding is how a team spends a day rolling back a release that was innocent — which is exactly why Debugging — Finding Out Why Your Code Lies to You insists on a mechanism rather than a coincidence.
Reference
The Fallacies That Actually Come Up
Skipping the Latin-heavy list in favour of the handful you will genuinely meet in a design review, a post-incident meeting or an argument online.
MoveWhat it doesSounds like
Straw manReplaces the argument with a weaker one, then defeats that"So you're saying we should have no security at all"
False dilemmaPresents two options where more exist"Either we rewrite it or we live with this forever"
Appeal to authorityCites a position instead of a reason — note that citing a relevant expert's evidence is fine, and citing their seniority is not"The architect signed it off"
Ad hominemAttacks the arguer to avoid the argument"You've never run anything at scale"
Moving the goalpostsRaises the standard of proof each time it is met"Fine, but that's only one environment"
Slippery slopeAsserts a chain of consequences without arguing for any link"If we allow this exception we'll have no policy left"
SurvivorshipReasons from the cases that remained visible"Every successful team I know does this"
Texas sharpshooterDraws the target after seeing where the shots landed"The data clearly shows a pattern here" — found after the fact, in one slice
Motte and baileyDefends a modest claim, then acts on an ambitious one"All I said was we should be careful" — after arguing for a freeze
Naming a fallacy is not an argument either. Declaring "that's a straw man" and stopping is the same move it complains about. The useful form is to restate what the other person actually said, in words they would accept, and then answer that — which incidentally is the strongest signal available that you were listening.
Concept
The Bit Nobody Teaches: Whose Job Is It to Prove?
A large share of unresolvable arguments are really disputes about the burden of proof, conducted without either side noticing that is the disagreement.
PrincipleIn practice
Whoever asserts, supports"Prove it isn't happening" inverts the burden, and no amount of evidence satisfies it
Absence of evidence is weak evidence of absenceHow weak depends entirely on whether you looked. No alerts from a system with no logging says nothing at all
Extraordinary claims need proportionate evidenceNot a licence to dismiss the unusual — a scale for how hard to look
Unfalsifiable claims are not strong, they are emptyA theory that predicts every possible observation has told you nothing about which one to expect
Say what would change your mindThe single most useful question in a stuck technical argument. If neither side can answer it, the disagreement is not about evidence
That last row is the whole card in one move. "What would we have to see for you to think you're wrong?" converts a values-clash or a status contest into an experiment, or reveals that it was never going to be one — and either outcome is progress, which is more than the argument was making before.
🔍 How You Know — Evidence, Certainty & Changing Your Mind WESTERN TRADITION ▶
Concept
Most of What You Know, You Were Told
It is worth being honest about the composition of your own knowledge. A small part is direct observation, a slightly larger part is inference from it, and the overwhelming majority is testimony — things you accepted because of who said them and how confident they sounded. That is not a failing; no individual could verify a fraction of what they need. But it means the practical question is almost never "is this true", which you usually cannot check. It is "how much weight does this source deserve, on this subject", which you often can.
SourceWhat raises its weight
Direct observationRepeated, by more than one person, with the conditions written down. Memory of a single sighting is weaker evidence than people believe
MeasurementA known method, a stated error, and someone who has cause to notice if it drifts
Expert testimonyExpertise in this specific thing, a track record you can check, and a willingness to state uncertainty
ConsensusIndependent routes to the same conclusion. A hundred sources citing one study is one source
Your own reasoningThat someone competent has tried to break it. Unopposed reasoning is the least reliable item on this list and always feels like the most
The consensus row is the one that most often deceives, and the qualification in it is the whole test. A hundred sources repeating one origin is one source, and it feels like overwhelming agreement — so the question is never how many say it, but how many arrived independently. Applied honestly this is uncomfortable: most of what any of us knows is testimony, weighted by a chain we have not checked.
Concept
Confidence Should Be a Number, Not a Mood
Beliefs are usually held as binary — I think this, I do not think that — which makes updating feel like an admission of error and therefore something to avoid. Holding them as degrees removes that: moving from 70% to 40% is not being wrong, it is what learning something looks like from the inside.
Instead ofTryWhat it buys
"The database is the bottleneck""I'd say 70% the database, 20% the network, 10% something we haven't thought of"The last 10% is where the actual answer lives more often than anyone expects
"That won't work""I think that fails, mainly because of X — if X is handled I'd change my mind"Names the crux, so the discussion has somewhere to go
"I was wrong""That evidence moves me a long way"Makes updating routine rather than an event with a cost attached
The discipline that makes this real is writing the number down before the outcome. Remembered confidence is reliably revised after the fact — everyone recalls having been more or less sure than they were — and a note costs nothing. It is also the only way to find out whether your 70% means anything.
Concept
Two Failures That Look Like Rigour
Both are common in careful, technical people, and both are more damaging than ordinary credulity because they come with a sense of being the sceptical one in the room.
FailureHow it presentsWhy it is a failure
Isolated demand for rigourFierce scrutiny of the claim you dislike, easy acceptance of the one you already heldThe standard moved with the conclusion, which means it is not a standard
Symmetric doubt"Nobody really knows", applied to everything equallyUncertainty is not uniform. Treating a well-replicated result and a single blog post as equally unknowable is a way of keeping your prior, not of being careful
The test for the first is simple and uncomfortable: would this objection have occurred to me if the conclusion had gone the other way? If you only ever check the sources of people you disagree with, you are not fact checking — you are choosing which side has to pay for evidence.
Concept
Knowing You Do Not Know
The last distinction is the one that governs how much of the rest applies: some gaps in your knowledge are visible to you, and the dangerous ones are not.
StateFeels likeWhat helps
You know that you knowFluencyOccasional checking; fluency decays quietly
You know that you do not knowDiscomfort, and a question you can askThe easy case. Look it up, or find someone
You do not know that you do not knowConfidenceOnly exposure to other people's work will show you the shape of the gap. This is the case that produces confident wrong answers
You know it without being able to say it"Something's off about this"Real and worth attending to — expert intuition is compressed experience. It is also indistinguishable from prejudice from the inside, so it is a reason to look, never a reason to conclude
The third row is why review exists, why a second pair of eyes finds things the author stared past, and why Imposter Syndrome — You Belong Here is so often a good sign: the discomfort of seeing how much you do not know is the experience of having moved out of the third row and into the second. The people who never feel it are frequently still in row three.
