🔎 Vulnerability Management Lifecycle 6-STEP LIFECYCLE ▶
Lifecycle
Discover → Scan → Prioritize → Remediate → Verify → Report
1
Asset Discovery
Inventory all assets: devices, VMs (Virtual Machine), containers, SaaS (Software as a Service), cloud resources. Maintain CMDB (Configuration Management Database)
2
Vulnerability Scanning
Credentialed vs non-credentialed scans. Tools: Nessus, Qualys, OpenVAS, Rapid7
3
Risk Prioritization
CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) score + asset criticality + exploitability (EPSS) + business context → risk ranking
4
Remediation
Patch, reconfigure, isolate, or formally accept risk. Track SLAs (Service Level Agreement) by severity level
5
Verification
Rescan to confirm fix. False-positive review. Validate patch deployment in CMDB
6
Reporting & Metrics
MTTR (Mean Time To Repair), risk reduction over time, SLA compliance, vulnerability age, executive dashboards
Verify is the step that gets dropped, and dropping it makes the whole loop a report generator. Remediation is handed to another team, the ticket closes on their word, and next month's scan finds the same finding — at which point the programme's own numbers are the only evidence of anything, and they are wrong. The cheap discipline is that a finding is closed by a rescan and never by a reply. Prioritise likewise: severity alone sorts by how bad it would be, and adds nothing about whether anyone is exploiting it this week.
🏢 BCP (Business Continuity Plan) / DR — Key Metrics & Recovery Strategies REFERENCE TABLE ▶

Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery Terminology
BCP ensures business functions continue during disruption. DRP is the tactical recovery plan. Always driven by BIA (Business Impact Analysis) results.
▸ CORE METRICS
RTO
Recovery Time Objective — Maximum acceptable time to restore a system after a disaster. "How fast must we recover?"
RPO
Recovery Point Objective — Maximum acceptable data loss measured in time. "How much data can we afford to lose?"
MTTR
Mean Time to Repair/Recover — Average time to restore a failed component or system to operation
MTBF
Mean Time Between Failures — Average time a repairable system runs before the next failure. Higher = more reliable
MTTF
Mean Time to Failure — Average life expectancy of non-repairable components (used for SSDs, HDDs)
▸ BACKUP & RECOVERY STRATEGIES
Strategy Description Notes
Database Shadowing Real-time exact copy of database mirrored to another location synchronously Zero RPO; high cost
Electronic Vaulting Automated backup of changed files to remote location at scheduled intervals RPO = backup interval
Remote Journaling Sends only transaction log files (not full data) to remote site in near real-time Lower bandwidth than full backup
Full Backup Complete copy of all data; slowest to run; fastest to restore Resets archive bit
Incremental Backs up only data changed since LAST backup (full or incremental) Fast backup; slow restore (needs all incrementals)
Differential Backs up all data changed since LAST FULL backup Slower backup than incremental; faster restore
▸ SITE RECOVERY TYPES
Site Type Readiness Cost RTO
Hot Site Fully operational mirror with live data replication Highest Minutes to hours
Warm Site Hardware ready; software/data must be loaded; partially configured Medium Hours to days
Cold Site Physical space and power only; no hardware pre-installed Lowest Days to weeks
Cloud Site Infrastructure spun up on-demand in cloud; pay per use Variable Minutes (if pre-scripted)
Cost buys minutes, and the only honest way to choose a row is to price the minutes first. Hot, warm and cold are three answers to one question the business has to answer — what does an hour of this being down cost — and a recovery objective chosen without that number is a preference dressed as a requirement. The cloud row is the one that changed the arithmetic: it reads as a warm site at a cold site's standing cost, but only if the build is genuinely scripted and has been run, and an unscripted cloud plan is a cold site with a larger invoice.
🔬 Digital Forensics Process 6-PHASE PROCESS ▶
Process — Source: CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional) + Net+ Cheat Sheets
Identification → Preservation → Collection → Examination → Analysis → Presentation
Digital forensics produces court-admissible evidence. Evidence must be relevant, complete, sufficient, and reliable. Chain of custody documents every person who handled evidence, when, and what they did.
1
Identification
Identify potential evidence sources: devices, logs, network captures, cloud data. First responder secures and documents the scene before anything is touched.
2
Preservation
Protect evidence from alteration (write blockers). Follow Order of Volatility: CPU (Central Processing Unit) registers/cache → RAM (Random Access Memory) → swap → disk → remote logs → paper. Issue Legal Hold.
3
Collection
Create forensic image (bit-for-bit copy) using tools like dd, FTK Imager, EnCase. Hash the image (MD5/SHA-256) immediately for integrity verification. Never work on originals.
4
Examination
Extract relevant data from the forensic image: files, metadata, deleted files (unallocated space), slack space, registry hives, logs, browser history, memory dumps.
5
Analysis
Interpret extracted data to reconstruct events: timeline analysis, correlation with other evidence, identification of IOCs (Indicator of Compromise), attribution of actions to users/systems.
6
Presentation / Court
Produce forensics report documenting methodology, findings, and conclusions. Expert witness testimony must be clear to non-technical judges/jury. E-discovery for legal proceedings.
▸ DISK-BASED FORENSIC DATA LOCATIONS
Location Description What You Find
Allocated Space Space actively used by files recognized by the OS (Operating System) Current files, documents, executables
Unallocated Space Space marked as free; may contain deleted files not yet overwritten Deleted files, carved artifacts
Slack Space Space between end of file and end of last cluster; may contain fragments File fragments, hidden data
Bad Clusters Sectors marked defective by OS; malware can hide data here Hidden malware, anti-forensic artifacts
Everything in this table is destroyed by the instinct to be helpful. Unallocated space, slack space and bad clusters survive only while nothing writes to the disk, so booting the machine "just to check something", running a scan, or copying files off it removes the evidence at exactly the moment somebody decided to investigate properly. Image first, examine the image. The original is a court exhibit from the moment you suspect it is one.

🧒Security Operations for BeginnersCySA+ • Start Here▶
BIG IDEA
What a SOC Does
A Security Operations Center (SOC) is the team that watches the screens — like a building's security guards reviewing cameras 24/7. They notice odd activity, investigate, and respond before small problems become big ones.
ANALOGY
Logs = a Diary
Every device keeps logs: a timestamped diary of what happened (who logged in, what failed, what changed). When something goes wrong, the logs are the story you read back to find out how and when.
ANALOGY
Alerts & False Alarms
Monitoring tools raise alerts the way a smoke detector beeps. Some are real fires; many are burnt toast (false positives). A big part of the job is tuning the detector so it cries wolf less.
PROCESS
Incident Response, Simply
When something bad happens: Prepare → Identify → Contain (stop the bleeding) → Eradicate (remove the cause) → Recover (get back to normal) → Lessons Learned (so it doesn't happen twice). Calm, ordered steps beat panic.
MINDSET
Assume Breach & Back Up
Mature teams assume an attacker will get in eventually, so they limit damage and keep tested backups. A backup you've never restored is just a hope — practice the recovery before you need it.
Tool / TermWhat It's For
SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)Collects all the logs in one searchable place
EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response)Watches each computer for bad behavior
PlaybookA pre-written recipe for handling an incident
TriageSorting alerts by how urgent they are

The tools are the easy half; the playbook is the row that decides how the night goes. Buying a SIEM and an EDR is a procurement decision most organisations can make; having a written recipe for the third- most-likely incident, tested, is a decision about time nobody is ever given. And the card's own opening line is the one to act on first: a backup nobody has restored is a hope. Restore one this quarter, from the real backup, onto real hardware, timed.
🚨A Day in the SOC (Security Operations Center)CySA+ • Start Here▶
THE FLOW
From Alert to Resolution
An analyst’s day is a queue of alerts. For each: triage (is it real and how urgent?), investigate (what actually happened?), respond or escalate, then document so the next person isn’t starting from zero.
TEAMS
Tier 1, 2, 3
Tier 1 triages the flood and handles the routine; Tier 2 digs into the tricky ones; Tier 3 are the specialists and threat hunters. It’s a normal career ladder — most people start at Tier 1 and learn fast.
MEASURE
Why Speed Matters
Two clocks rule the SOC: how fast you notice (detect) and how fast you contain (respond). The longer an attacker has, the more they take — so shaving minutes off those times is the whole point.
TermPlain Meaning
TriageSort by urgency, like an ER
EscalateHand a hard one up a tier
False positiveAn alert that turned out to be nothing
HandoffBriefing the next shift so nothing drops
MTTR (Mean Time To Repair)Mean time to respond — average cleanup speed
The handoff is the row on this list that is nobody's job and loses the most incidents. Triage, escalation and false positives all have owners and metrics; the shift change has neither, and it is where a half- investigated alert quietly becomes a closed one. Write the handoff down in the ticket rather than saying it across the desk — the next shift has no memory of your afternoon, and the attacker's clock does not stop for it.
🔧Troubleshooting Like a ProA+ • Start Here▶
MINDSET
Calm, Curious, Methodical
Good troubleshooting isn’t magic or luck — it’s a repeatable method. Panic and random clicking make things worse; a calm, one-step-at-a-time approach fixes problems and teaches you something each time.
THE METHOD
CompTIA (Computing Technology Industry Association)’s 7 Steps
The industry-standard loop: identify the problem, form a theory of the cause, test that theory, make a plan, fix it, verify it’s really solved (and prevention), then document what happened. Skipping ‘document’ is how the same fire gets fought twice.
FIRST QUESTIONS
Narrow It Down Fast
Start by shrinking the search: What changed recently? Does it affect one person or everyone? Can you reproduce it? ‘It worked yesterday’ plus ‘what changed’ solves a surprising share of problems.
CLASSIC WISDOM
Check the Simple Stuff First
Is it plugged in? Is it on the right network? Did you restart it? Seasoned techs aren’t being condescending — the boring, simple cause really is the answer most of the time. Rule it out before chasing the exotic.
StepIn Plain English
1. IdentifyGather symptoms; what exactly is wrong?
2. TheoryBest guess at the cause (simple first)
3. Test theoryProve or disprove the guess
4. PlanDecide the fix and any risk
5. ImplementApply the fix (one change at a time)
6. VerifyConfirm it’s solved + prevent recurrence
7. DocumentWrite it down for next time
Steps 3 and 5 are where people skip, and skipping either costs the whole method. Not testing the theory means you fixed something and hope it was the thing; changing more than one thing at once means it works and you cannot say why, which is the same as not having fixed it — the next occurrence starts from nothing. The discipline is dull and it is what separates somebody who resolves an incident from somebody present when it stopped.
📊 SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) & Log Analysis — Finding Signals in the Noise OPS • Foundation ▶
WHAT IS A SIEM
Security Information and Event Management
A SIEM collects log data from across your environment — firewalls, servers, endpoints, cloud, applications — into one place. It correlates events across sources to spot attacks that would be invisible in any single log. Common SIEMs: Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel, IBM QRadar, Elastic/SIEM, LogRhythm.

FALSE POSITIVES
Alert Fatigue Is a Real Threat
When a SIEM generates 10,000 alerts per day, analysts tune out. Alert fatigue causes real attacks to be dismissed as noise. Good SOC (Security Operations Center) work involves constantly tuning detection rules — raising thresholds for noisy rules, adding context (business hours, user role, geography), and building baselines for normal behavior.
🚨 Incident Response — What to Do When It Goes Wrong OPS • Critical ▶
NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) IR PHASES
Preparation → Detection → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned
The NIST SP 800-61 Incident Response lifecycle is the industry standard. Each phase has specific goals and outputs.
PhaseGoalKey Actions
PreparationBe ready before incidents happenIR plan, playbooks, tools, training, tabletops
Detection & AnalysisKnow something happenedSIEM (Security Information and Event Management) alerts, IOC (Indicator of Compromise) analysis, triage, severity scoring
ContainmentStop the bleedingIsolate systems, block IPs/hashes, preserve evidence
EradicationRemove the threatDelete malware, patch vulnerability, reset credentials
RecoveryGet back to normalRestore from clean backup, monitor for re-infection
Post-IncidentLearn and improveRoot cause analysis, update playbooks, retrain staff
Preparation is the only phase you can do at your leisure, and it is what determines how the other five go. Nobody writes a playbook during containment; they improvise, and the improvisation is what ends up in the post- incident review. The phase that gets cut in practice is eradication — the pressure to restore service pushes teams from containment straight to recovery, the threat is still resident, and the second incident is the first one continuing.
CONTAINMENT STRATEGIES
Short-Term vs Long-Term
Short-term: immediate isolation — unplug network cable, disable account, null-route IP (Internet Protocol). Buy time without losing evidence.
Long-term: rebuild system from a known-good image, re-image endpoints, force password resets, revoke compromised certificates.
Key decision: preserve evidence vs stop the bleeding. If active data exfiltration is happening, containment wins. If already contained, preserve first.
CHAIN OF CUSTODY
Evidence Must Be Unimpeachable
If the incident leads to legal action or law enforcement, digital evidence must be collected and handled correctly. Document: who collected it, when, what state it was in, and how it was stored. Use write blockers for disk imaging. Hash every piece of evidence (MD5 + SHA-256) and verify hashes later. Mishandled evidence gets thrown out.
💾 Backup & Disaster Recovery — Surviving the Worst Case OPS • Critical ▶
THE 3-2-1 RULE
Three Copies, Two Media, One Off-Site
3 copies of your data (original + 2 backups)
2 different storage types (SSD + external drive, local + cloud)
1 copy off-site (cloud, different building, different region)
Why: ransomware encrypts local + network shares. Fire/flood destroys same-room copies. Off-site survives both. Modern extension: 3-2-1-1-0 (add 1 offline/air-gapped copy and 0 errors verified by testing).
BACKUP TYPES
Full vs Incremental vs Differential
TypeWhat It Backs UpBackup SpeedRestore Speed
FullEverythingSlowFast (one file)
IncrementalChanges since last backup of any typeFastSlow (full + all incrementals)
DifferentialChanges since last FULL backupMediumMedium (full + last differential)
Pick by the restore, not by the backup window. The backup runs at three in the morning when nobody is watching; the restore runs on the worst day of the year with an executive standing behind you, and that is the column to optimise. Incremental is the cheapest to run and the most fragile to depend on — one corrupt link in the chain and everything after it is unreadable. Differential is the safe default for anything you would be asked to bring back the same day.
RTO & RPO (Recovery Point Objective)
Recovery Time and Data Loss Objectives
RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how long can the business tolerate being down? An e-commerce site might have an RTO of 1 hour. A hospital might have an RTO of minutes.
RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much data can we afford to lose? If RPO is 4 hours, backups must run at least every 4 hours.
These drive backup strategy: low RTO/RPO requires continuous replication and hot standby; high RTO/RPO can use daily backups to tape.
TEST YOUR BACKUPS
An Untested Backup Is Not a Backup
Backups regularly fail silently (disk full, permission error, partial write). The only way to know a backup is good is to restore from it in a test environment. Restore tests should be:
• Scheduled (quarterly at minimum)
• Documented (how long did restore take? any errors?)
• Realistic (restore to isolated environment, not production)
Many organizations discover their backups are corrupted during an actual disaster — the worst time to find out.
RANSOMWARE RESPONSE
When Encryption Happens to You
If ransomware strikes:
1. Isolate affected systems immediately — disconnect from network
2. Don't pay if you have clean backups — payment doesn't guarantee decryption
3. Preserve evidence — image affected systems before wiping
4. Restore from known-good backup made before infection
5. Find the initial access before coming back online — if you restore without finding the entry point, you'll be re-infected
📡 Monitoring & Observability — Know Before Users Complain OPS • Foundation ▶
THREE PILLARS
Metrics · Logs · Traces
Metrics — numerical measurements over time: CPU (Central Processing Unit) %, request rate, error rate, latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99). Great for dashboards and alerting thresholds.
Logs — event records: what happened, when, and on which system. Needed to understand the WHY behind a metric spike.
Traces — record the journey of a single request through multiple services. Essential for distributed systems to find which service is causing latency.
KEY METRICS
The Four Golden Signals (Google SRE)
Latency — how long does a request take? Track P95/P99 not just average (average hides slow outliers).
Traffic — how many requests per second? Understand normal baseline so anomalies are visible.
Errors — what percentage of requests fail? Even 0.1% on 1M req/s is 1000 errors/sec.
Saturation — how full is your system? CPU, memory, disk, connections — approaching limits causes cascading failures.
ALERTING PHILOSOPHY
Alert on Symptoms, Not Causes
Alert when USERS are affected, not when internal metrics are interesting. "Error rate > 1% for 5 minutes" is a user-facing symptom. "CPU > 80%" is a cause that may or may not matter. Too many non-actionable alerts → alert fatigue → real alerts ignored. Every alert should have a runbook: what to check, what to do, who to escalate to.
🎫 ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) & Service Management — How IT Runs as a Business OPS • Process ▶
WHAT IS ITIL
A Framework for Delivering IT Services
ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) is the most widely adopted framework for IT Service Management (ITSM). It's a set of best practices for delivering IT as a service to a business — covering how you handle outages, changes, requests, and continuous improvement. You'll encounter ITIL terminology constantly in enterprise IT, especially on help desks and ops teams. Even if your company doesn't "do ITIL," they probably use its vocabulary.
INCIDENT VS PROBLEM VS CHANGE VS REQUEST
The Four Terms People Mix Up
TypeDefinitionGoalExample
IncidentUnplanned interruption or degradation of a serviceRestore service ASAP (even with a workaround)"Email is down"
ProblemThe underlying root cause of one or more incidentsFind & fix root cause permanently"Why does email keep going down? Bad disk."
ChangeAdding/modifying/removing anything that could affect servicesImplement safely with minimal risk"Upgrade the mail server"
Service RequestA user asking for something standard/pre-approvedFulfill efficiently"I need access to the shared drive"
Key distinction: an incident is "it's broken, fix it now." A problem is "why does it keep breaking?" You restore the incident fast (workaround), then investigate the problem to prevent recurrence.
INCIDENT PRIORITY
Priority = Impact × Urgency
Not every incident is equal. Priority determines what gets worked first. It's calculated from impact (how many people / how critical) and urgency (how fast it needs fixing).
PriorityTypical MeaningExample
P1 — CriticalMajor outage, business stoppedEntire company can't access core system
P2 — HighSignificant impact, no workaroundA department is down
P3 — MediumLimited impact or workaround existsOne team's printer is broken
P4 — LowMinor, single user, cosmetic"My mouse settings reset"
The arguments are never about the definitions; they are about who sets urgency. Impact is countable — how many people, which system — and urgency is a judgement the requester always scores at maximum. Publish the matrix, let the service desk set the priority from it, and accept that a good process produces occasional P3s that somebody is furious about. A queue where everything is P1 is a queue with no priority at all, and it is what happens when the caller picks the number.
CHANGE MANAGEMENT
Why You Can't Just Push to Production
Most outages are self-inflicted — caused by changes. Change management adds guardrails so changes are reviewed, approved, scheduled, and reversible. The CAB (Change Advisory Board) reviews risky changes.
Change TypeRiskApprovalExample
StandardLow, pre-approvedNone needed (routine)Routine password reset, known patch
NormalVariableCAB review + approvalMajor version upgrade
EmergencyHigh (but necessary)Expedited (ECAB)Patch an actively exploited zero-day
Every change should have a rollback plan ("if it breaks, how do we undo it?") and a maintenance window (a scheduled low-traffic time). "It's just a small change" is the most dangerous phrase in ops.
SLAs (Service Level Agreement), SLOs (Service Level Objective), SLIs (Service Level Indicator)
Measuring Service Quality
TermFull NameWhat It Is
SLIService Level IndicatorThe actual measurement (e.g., 99.95% uptime measured)
SLOService Level ObjectiveYour internal target (e.g., "we aim for 99.9%")

📈 Performance & Capacity — Finding and Fixing Bottlenecks OPS • Essential ▶
THE FOUR RESOURCES
Everything Is CPU (Central Processing Unit), Memory, Disk, or Network
When a system is slow, the bottleneck is almost always one of four resources. The skill is identifying which one — guessing wastes time. Start broad (which resource is saturated?), then drill into the offending process.
ResourceSymptomLinux Tool
CPUHigh load, slow computationtop, htop, mpstat, uptime
MemorySwapping, OOM kills, slownessfree -h, vmstat, top
Disk I/O (Input/Output)High iowait, slow reads/writesiostat, iotop, df, du
NetworkLatency, packet loss, saturationiftop, ss, nload, mtr
Measure which of the four before touching anything, every time. The instinct on a slow system is to act on the last thing that changed, and roughly half the time that is unrelated; five minutes with the tools in the right-hand column tells you which resource is saturated and reduces the problem from "the system is slow" to a question with an answer. The row people forget is memory, because the symptom is disk — swapping presents as I/O wait and sends you to the wrong tool.
READING THE VITALS
Load Average and Resource Commands

# load average: 2.50, 1.80, 1.20
# Rule of thumb: compare to CPU core count.
# load 2.5 on a 4-core box = fine. On 1 core = overloaded.

# "available" is the number that matters, not "free"

THE USE METHOD
A Systematic Troubleshooting Framework
Brendan Gregg's USE method: for every resource, check three things. It's a checklist that prevents you from missing the obvious.
U-S-EQuestion
UtilizationHow busy is the resource? (% time active)
SaturationIs there a queue/backlog of work waiting?
ErrorsAre there error events? (dropped packets, disk errors)
Saturation is the column that finds what utilisation hides. A resource at 70% utilisation looks healthy and can have a queue thirty deep if the work arrives in bursts, and the user experiences the queue, not the average. Check all three for each resource in turn and the method's value shows up: it is a checklist precisely so that under pressure you do not skip the one that would have answered it.
CAPACITY PLANNING
Don't Wait Until It's On Fire
Capacity planning means predicting future resource needs based on growth trends, so you scale before you run out. Track utilization over time, identify the trend, and add capacity with lead time. The key metrics: current utilization, growth rate, and headroom (how much buffer before you hit limits). A disk filling at 2%/week with 20% free has ~10 weeks — plan now, not at 99%.

Known Unknowns vs Unknown Unknowns
Monitoring answers questions you already know to ask ("is CPU high?"). Observability lets you ask new questions about your system's behavior without shipping new code — crucial for debugging novel failures in complex distributed systems. Monitoring tells you something is wrong; observability helps you figure out why.

GITOPS
Git as the Single Source of Truth
GitOps applies software-development practices to operations: the desired state of your infrastructure and apps lives in a Git repo, and an automated agent continuously makes reality match the repo. The test of a GitOps setup is whether a change made by hand in the cluster is reverted automatically within minutes. If it survives, you do not have GitOps, you have a repository of intentions.
🎚️ SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) — Engineering Reliability with Data, Not Heroics OPS • Modern ▶
WHAT IS SRE
"What Happens When a Software Engineer Runs Operations"
Site Reliability Engineering (SRE), pioneered at Google, applies software-engineering thinking to operations. Instead of manually firefighting, SREs use code, automation, and data to make systems reliable at scale. The core insight: reliability is a feature you can measure and budget for — not an absolute. 100% reliability is the wrong target (it's impossibly expensive and users can't even tell).
ERROR BUDGETS
Reliability You Can Spend
If your SLO (Service Level Objective) is 99.9% availability, then 0.1% unreliability is your error budget — about 43 minutes/month you're "allowed" to be down. This brilliantly resolves the eternal dev-vs-ops conflict: as long as you're within budget, ship features fast. Blow the budget? Freeze features and focus on reliability. It turns reliability into a shared, data-driven decision instead of an argument.
SLODowntime/monthDowntime/year
99% (two nines)~7.3 hours~3.65 days
99.9% (three nines)~43 minutes~8.8 hours
99.99% (four nines)~4.3 minutes~52 minutes
99.999% (five nines)~26 seconds~5.3 minutes
Pick the row your users would actually notice, then defend it against the instinct to add a nine. Each step down this table costs roughly an order of magnitude more in architecture, on-call and change friction, and nobody asking for five nines has costed the change freeze that comes with 26 seconds a month. The budget only works if it is genuinely spent: a team that ends every month with the whole budget unused is not reliable, it is shipping too slowly and has not noticed.
TOIL
The Enemy of Engineering
Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales linearly with growth and produces no lasting value — resetting passwords by hand, manually restarting services, copy-pasting between dashboards. SRE culture caps toil (often at ~50% of time) and invests the rest in automation and engineering that eliminates toil. If you're doing something tedious for the third time, that's a signal to automate it.
CHAOS ENGINEERING
Break It on Purpose, on Your Terms
Chaos engineering deliberately injects failures (kill a server, add latency, drop a dependency) into systems — ideally in production, carefully — to discover weaknesses before they cause real outages. Netflix's "Chaos Monkey" randomly kills instances to force engineers to build resilient systems. The philosophy: you don't truly know your system is resilient until you've tested it failing. Start small, in a controlled blast radius, with a hypothesis and a way to abort.
🧯 Incident Command — Running a Major Incident Without Chaos OPS • Critical ▶
WHY STRUCTURE
Big Incidents Fail on Coordination, Not Tech
In a major incident, the technical problem is often not the hardest part — coordination is. Without structure you get chaos: everyone debugging the same thing, no one talking to stakeholders, conflicting changes, decision paralysis. Tech companies borrowed the Incident Command System (ICS) from emergency services (firefighters) to bring order. The key idea: assign clear roles so the responders can focus while someone else coordinates and communicates.
THE ROLES
Who Does What in a Major Incident
RoleResponsibility

Operations / RespondersThe hands-on engineers actually investigating and fixing

In a small incident one person may wear several hats — but even then, declaring who is IC prevents the "everyone assumes someone else has it" failure.
RUNNING IT
The Flow of a Well-Managed Incident
PhaseAction
DeclareCall it an incident, assign an IC, open a dedicated channel/bridge
AssessEstablish severity & impact; who/what is affected
MitigateRestore service first (workarounds OK); root cause can wait

Communicate is the row that is technically unnecessary and decides how the incident is remembered. Mitigation restores the service; the updates every twenty minutes are what stop six executives opening six side- channels and pulling engineers out of the work. Silence is read as "nothing is happening", so send the update even when it says you know nothing new — and separate the incident commander from the person typing commands, because one human cannot do both jobs and will drop the one nobody is chasing.
GOLDEN RULES
Hard-Won Incident Wisdom
Restore first, diagnose later — users want service back, not your root-cause theory. One person changes things at a time — coordinate through the IC to avoid conflicting fixes that make it worse. Communicate more than feels necessary — stakeholders fill silence with worst-case assumptions. It's OK to escalate / wake people up — a prolonged outage costs far more than someone's sleep. And recall the mindset thread: stay calm, work the process, own what's yours, and don't let panic (or blame) drive decisions. The calm, structured responder is worth ten frantic geniuses.
🐘 Beginner PostgreSQL Administration – Database Ops 101 ▶
Core Concept
What Is a Database Server?
A database server stores data in structured tables and answers queries from applications. PostgreSQL (Postgres) is the most popular open-source relational database. As an ops person you are responsible for keeping it running, backed up, and fast.

Quick orientation:
• Cluster – one running Postgres instance (port 5432).
• Database – a namespace inside the cluster.
• Schema – a namespace inside a database (default: public).
• Table – rows & columns, like a spreadsheet.
• Role – a user or group (Postgres uses one object for both).
Daily Commands
psql – The Postgres CLI (Command Line Interface)
psql is the interactive terminal for Postgres. Master these and you can do 80 % of admin tasks. 
# Connect as the postgres superuser
sudo -u postgres psql

-- Inside psql (lines starting with -- are SQL comments)
-- List all databases
\l

-- Connect to a specific database
\c myapp_db

-- List tables in current schema
\dt

-- Show table structure
\d users

-- Run a query
SELECT id, email FROM users LIMIT 5;

-- Show currently running queries
SELECT pid, now() - query_start AS duration, query
FROM  pg_stat_activity
WHERE state = 'active'
ORDER BY duration DESC;

-- Kill a stuck query (use pid from above)
SELECT pg_terminate_backend(12345);

-- Quit psql
\q
Backup & Restore
pg_dump and pg_restore
You can't make someone make the right choice, yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards — a developer may drop a table by accident. Your job is to have a backup ready so you can restore it. 
# Backup a single database to a compressed file
pg_dump -U postgres -Fc myapp_db > myapp_db_$(date +%F).dump

# Backup all databases (SQL text format)
pg_dumpall -U postgres > all_databases_$(date +%F).sql

# Restore from a compressed dump
pg_restore -U postgres -d myapp_db --clean myapp_db_2026-06-01.dump

# Restore from SQL text dump
psql -U postgres < all_databases_2026-06-01.sql

# Automate nightly backup via cron (runs at 2 AM daily)
# Add to crontab with: crontab -e
0 2 * * * pg_dump -U postgres -Fc myapp_db > /backups/myapp_$(date +\%F).dump
Performance
VACUUM, ANALYZE, and Indexes
Postgres uses Multi-Version Concurrency Control (MVCC) — deleted rows are not immediately removed, they become "dead tuples." VACUUM reclaims that space. ANALYZE updates statistics so the query planner makes good choices.

Autovacuum handles this automatically in normal operation — never disable it.

Indexes speed up queries on large tables (like a book's index). Add one when a column appears frequently in WHERE clauses, but don't over-index — every index slows down INSERT/UPDATE. 
-- Manual vacuum + analyze (safe to run any time)
VACUUM ANALYZE users;

-- Check table bloat (dead tuple ratio)
SELECT relname, n_dead_tup, n_live_tup,
       round(n_dead_tup::numeric / nullif(n_live_tup,0) * 100, 2) AS dead_pct
FROM  pg_stat_user_tables
ORDER BY dead_pct DESC NULLS LAST;

-- Add an index on the email column
CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY idx_users_email ON users(email);
-- CONCURRENTLY = no table lock, safe on production

-- Explain a query (show execution plan)
EXPLAIN ANALYZE SELECT * FROM users WHERE email = 'alice@example.com';
🛡️ Beginner Web Server Hardening – nginx, Apache & TLS (Transport Layer Security) Done Right ▶
Core Concept
The Default Config Is Not the Secure Config
Out of the box, web servers prioritise compatibility over security — they'll happily serve old TLS versions, leak version information, and allow risky HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) methods. Hardening means deliberately tightening these defaults.

The five hardening layers:
1. TLS configuration — which protocol versions and ciphers are allowed.
2. HTTP security headers — instructions to the browser about how to treat your site.
3. Information disclosure — hide server version and technology stack from attackers.
4. Rate limiting — slow down brute-force and scraping attempts.
5. Certificate management — automated renewal so sites never go down from an expired cert (a surprisingly common self-inflicted outage).
nginx Hardening
A Hardened nginx Server Block
This configuration covers the essentials most security scanners and compliance checks look for. 
# /etc/nginx/sites-available/example.com
server {
    listen 443 ssl http2;
    server_name example.com;

    # ── TLS: only modern protocols and strong ciphers ──
    ssl_certificate     /etc/letsencrypt/live/example.com/fullchain.pem;
    ssl_certificate_key /etc/letsencrypt/live/example.com/privkey.pem;
    ssl_protocols       TLSv1.2 TLSv1.3;          # no SSLv3, TLSv1.0, TLSv1.1
    ssl_ciphers         HIGH:!aNULL:!MD5:!3DES;
    ssl_prefer_server_ciphers on;
    ssl_session_cache   shared:SSL:10m;

    # ── Hide version info (reduces fingerprinting) ──
    server_tokens off;

    # ── Security headers ──
    add_header Strict-Transport-Security "max-age=63072000; includeSubDomains" always;
    add_header X-Frame-Options "SAMEORIGIN" always;
    add_header X-Content-Type-Options "nosniff" always;
    add_header Referrer-Policy "strict-origin-when-cross-origin" always;
    add_header Content-Security-Policy "default-src 'self'" always;

    # ── Rate limiting (defined in http{} block, applied here) ──
    limit_req zone=login_limit burst=5 nodelay;

    location / {
        proxy_pass http://127.0.0.1:8080;
        proxy_set_header Host $host;
        proxy_set_header X-Real-IP $remote_addr;
    }
}

# Redirect all plain HTTP to HTTPS
server {
    listen 80;
    server_name example.com;
    return 301 https://$host$request_uri;
}

# In the http{} block — define the rate limit zone:
# limit_req_zone $binary_remote_addr zone=login_limit:10m rate=5r/m;
Headers Explained
What Each Security Header Actually Does
HeaderWhat it tells the browser
Strict-Transport-Security"Always use HTTPS (HyperText Transfer Protocol Secure) for this site, never fall back to HTTP"
X-Frame-Options"Don't let other sites embed me in an iframe" (stops clickjacking)
X-Content-Type-Options"Don't guess file types — trust what I declared" (stops MIME sniffing attacks)
Content-Security-Policy"Only load scripts/styles/images from these approved sources"
Referrer-Policy"Don't leak the full URL (Uniform Resource Locator) to other sites when users click links"
Four of these are one-line wins; Content-Security-Policy is a project, and that is the honest split. HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security), X-Frame-Options, X-Content-Type-Options and Referrer-Policy can be set this afternoon and break almost nothing. CSP done properly means enumerating every script source your site actually uses, which is why so many sites ship one so permissive it stops any attack it names. Set the four, then start CSP in report-only mode and read the reports for a fortnight before enforcing.

"Assume" makes an ass out of you and me — never assume adding these headers won't break anything. Content-Security-Policy especially can silently break legitimate site features (third-party widgets, inline scripts). Test in report-only mode first: Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only logs violations without blocking anything.
Verification
Testing Your Hardening Actually Worked
You can't make someone make the right choice, yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards — but you can also verify the choice was made correctly in the first place. Don't trust your config — test it.

# https://www.ssllabs.com/ssltest/  → grades A+ to F

# Check from the command line with testssl.sh
docker run --rm -ti drwetter/testssl.sh https://example.com

# Check which protocols/ciphers your server actually offers

📗 Beginner Writing Runbooks That People Actually Follow ▶
Concept

A runbook is a conversation with your future self at 3 AM

A runbook is a step-by-step procedure for handling a specific operational situation — restarting a stuck service, failing over a database, rotating an expired certificate. The test of a good runbook isn't whether the author understands it; it's whether a half-asleep on-call engineer who has never touched this system can follow it correctly under pressure, at 3 AM, without calling anyone for help.

Concept

The anatomy of a runbook that survives contact with reality

SectionPurposeWhat goes wrong without it
Trigger / symptomsHow do you know this runbook applies right now?Engineer wastes 20 minutes confirming they're even looking at the right problem
Blast radius / impactWho and what is affected if this goes wrong further?Nobody escalates because nobody realized how big the problem actually was
PreconditionsWhat access, tools, or state do you need before starting?Engineer gets halfway through and discovers they don't have the right permissions
Numbered steps with exact commandsRemove all guesswork — copy, paste, run"Restart the service" becomes a 45-minute detour into "which service, on which host, with which flag?"
Verification stepHow do you confirm the fix actually worked?Engineer declares victory, closes the ticket, and the issue recurs an hour later
Rollback / escalation pathWhat do you do if this *doesn't* work?Engineer freezes, unsure whether to keep pushing or wake someone up
Trigger and verification are the two sections that turn a document into a runbook. Without a trigger nobody knows this is the right page at three in the morning; without a verification step the engineer declares victory on the basis that the command exited zero. The rollback path is the one that gets left as "escalate to the team lead", which is not a path — write down what the escalation is for and what the person receiving it will need.
Hands-on

A short runbook excerpt, written the right way

# RUNBOOK: API gateway returning 503s

# TRIGGER: PagerDuty alert "gateway-503-rate-high" fires when error
# rate > 5% for 5 minutes straight.

# IMPACT: All customer-facing API traffic degraded. Treat as SEV-2.

# STEP 1 — confirm the alert is real, not a monitoring blip
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" https://api.internal.example.com/healthz
# Expect: 200. If you see 503 here too, this is real — continue.

# STEP 2 — check whether it's one node or all of them
kubectl get pods -n gateway -o wide
# Look for pods stuck in CrashLoopBackOff or 0/1 Ready

# STEP 3 — restart only the unhealthy pods (NOT the whole deployment)
kubectl delete pod <pod-name> -n gateway
# Kubernetes will reschedule it automatically — this is non-destructive

# VERIFY — error rate back under 1% for 5 consecutive minutes
watch -n 10 'curl -s https://api.internal.example.com/metrics | grep error_rate'

# IF STILL FAILING after step 3 — STOP. Escalate to #platform-oncall.
# Do not attempt a full deployment restart without a second engineer present.
Mindset

You can't make someone make the right choice...

...yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards. No matter how clear your runbook is, someone under pressure will eventually skip the verification step, run the command on the wrong host, or improvise instead of escalating. That's not a reason to stop writing good runbooks — it's the reason to also write the recovery procedure for "someone did the wrong thing." The best operational teams plan for both the happy path and the moment a tired human deviates from it.

📈 Beginner Capacity Planning – Answering "Will We Run Out of Room?" Before You Do ▶
Concept

Capacity planning is forecasting, not firefighting

Capacity planning is the practice of looking at how a resource — disk space, bandwidth, server load, license seats — is being consumed over time, and projecting forward to answer "when will this become a problem, and what should we do about it before it does?" The opposite approach — waiting for an alert that says "disk 98% full" — turns a planning exercise into a 2 AM emergency. Good capacity planning quietly prevents most of the outages that bad capacity planning causes people to brag about firefighting.

Concept

The basic forecasting loop

StepWhat you're doingExample question it answers
1. Measure current usageEstablish a baseline — what does "normal" look like today?"We're using 620 GB of our 1 TB volume"
2. Track the trend over timeIs usage flat, growing steadily, or growing faster than before?"We've added about 15 GB per week for the last two months"
3. Project forwardAt the current rate, when do you cross the danger threshold?"At 15 GB/week, we hit 900 GB (our alert threshold) in about 19 weeks"
4. Plan the interventionWhat needs to happen before that date — and how long does it take to do it?"Provisioning additional storage takes 6 weeks of lead time, so we need to start the request now, not in week 18"
Step 4 is the one that makes the other three worth doing. Plenty of teams measure and trend and can tell you precisely when the disk fills; the forecast is only useful if it is compared against the lead time of the fix, and procurement, budget approval and change windows routinely run longer than the runway. The number to publish is not "we hit the threshold in twelve weeks" but "we must start in six".
Hands-on

A simple growth projection in Python

# Given a few historical measurements, estimate when you'll hit a threshold
from datetime import date, timedelta

# (date, usage_in_gb) samples taken weekly
history = [
    (date(2026, 5, 4), 590),
    (date(2026, 5, 11), 605),
    (date(2026, 5, 18), 622),
    (date(2026, 5, 25), 636),
]

# Average weekly growth across the sampled period
days_elapsed = (history[-1][0] - history[0][0]).days
total_growth = history[-1][1] - history[0][1]
weekly_growth = total_growth / (days_elapsed / 7)

threshold_gb = 900
current_gb = history[-1][1]
weeks_remaining = (threshold_gb - current_gb) / weekly_growth
projected_date = history[-1][0] + timedelta(weeks=weeks_remaining)

print(f"Growing ~{weekly_growth:.1f} GB/week")
print(f"Projected to hit {threshold_gb} GB around {projected_date}")
print(f"That's roughly {weeks_remaining:.0f} weeks from the last measurement")

This is deliberately simple — straight-line ("linear") projection. Real-world usage often grows in bursts (a new product launch, a seasonal spike), so mature capacity planning layers in seasonality and known upcoming events on top of the trend line. But even this basic version turns a vague worry into a concrete date you can act on.

Mindset

Not my circus, not my monkey — until the monkey runs out of room

"That's the database team's disk, not mine" works fine right up until their volume fills up and takes down a service your team depends on. Capacity problems rarely stay neatly inside team boundaries — a full log partition can crash an application server, a maxed-out license pool can block an entire department's work. Part of good capacity planning is simply knowing which shared resources you depend on, even ones you don't directly own, so "not my circus" doesn't quietly become "my outage."

🔬 Beginner Writing a Postmortem People Actually Learn From ▶
Concept

A postmortem is a story about a system, not a trial of a person

A postmortem (or "post-incident review") is a written analysis of what happened during an outage or incident, why it happened, and what will change as a result. The single most important design decision in any postmortem process is whether it's blameless — focused on "what conditions made this mistake possible and likely?" rather than "who do we blame for this?" Get that one decision right, and people will tell you the truth about what happened. Get it wrong, and every future postmortem will quietly omit the details that actually mattered.

Concept

The shape of a postmortem that actually gets read

SectionWhat it answersCommon mistake to avoid
SummaryWhat happened, in 3-4 sentences a busy executive could read in 20 secondsBurying the headline under technical preamble nobody reads first
ImpactWho was affected, for how long, and how badly — with real numbersVague language like "some users experienced issues" instead of "approximately 4,200 users over 38 minutes"
TimelineWhat happened, in order, with timestamps — including detection and responseStarting the timeline at "the fix" instead of at the first sign something was wrong
Root cause(s)Why it happened — usually more than one contributing factor, not a single villainStopping at "a bad config was deployed" instead of asking "why was it possible to deploy that config unreviewed?"
Action itemsSpecific, owned, dated changes that reduce the chance of recurrenceVague commitments like "we'll be more careful" — that's not an action item, it's a hope
Action items are where the document either changes something or does not. Everything above them is analysis, and analysis is comfortable; a specific change with a named owner and a date is a commitment somebody has to be held to. Two owned actions beat fifteen aspirations, and a review that produces "we will be more careful" has produced nothing. Check the previous postmortem's actions at the start of the next one — that single habit is what stops the same incident recurring with a different date.
Hands-on

The "five whys" — finding the real root cause

# A simple technique for digging past the surface-level explanation.
# Keep asking "why" until you reach something you can actually fix.

Problem: The payment service was down for 40 minutes.

Why #1: Why was it down?
   -> The service crashed when it ran out of memory.

Why #2: Why did it run out of memory?
   -> A new code path loaded an entire customer table into memory at once.

Why #3: Why did that code path do that?
   -> The developer tested only against a small sample database in staging.

Why #4: Why didn't testing catch this before production?
   -> Staging databases aren't seeded with production-scale data volumes.

Why #5: Why isn't that part of the standard testing process?
   -> There's no documented requirement for load-realistic staging data.

# Notice where this ended up: not "the developer made a mistake"
# (Why #1-3), but "our process doesn't ensure realistic testing
# conditions" (Why #5) — a SYSTEM problem with a fixable solution,
# not a PERSON problem with only blame to offer.
Mindset

Not my circus, not my monkey — except blameless culture is everyone's circus

It's easy to think "writing good postmortems is the SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) team's job" — but a blameless culture is built or destroyed by everyone in the room, in the moment someone says "well, obviously they should have known better." One unkind comment in a review meeting can undo months of careful culture-building, and it teaches everyone present — not just the person being criticized — that honesty here has a cost. Protecting that culture, even in a meeting that isn't "yours," is part of keeping the whole system honest enough to actually improve.

🐒 Chaos Engineering — Breaking Things on Purpose Reliability ▶
Concept
Find the Weakness Before It Finds You
Chaos engineering is the discipline of deliberately injecting failure into a system — killing servers, adding network latency, exhausting disk — to discover how it actually behaves under stress, rather than how you hope it does. The insight: distributed systems fail in ways no one predicted, so you'd rather trigger those failures in a controlled experiment (business hours, watching closely) than at 3 a.m. during a real outage. Netflix pioneered it with Chaos Monkey, which randomly kills production instances to force teams to build resilience in.
Method
Running an Experiment
StepWhat You Do
1. Steady stateDefine a measurable "healthy" metric (e.g. checkout success rate > 99%).
2. Hypothesis"If we kill one payment node, checkout success stays above 99%."
3. Blast radiusStart tiny — one instance, small % of traffic — to limit harm.
4. Inject & observeIntroduce the failure; watch the metric and your alerts.
5. Learn & fixIf the hypothesis breaks, you found a real weakness — fix it, then re-run.
Always have an abort switch to stop the experiment instantly, and never run one you're confident will cause a major outage — the goal is learning, not self-inflicted downtime.
Reference
Common Failures to Inject
FailureTests Whether…
Kill an instanceLoad balancing & auto-healing actually replace it
Add network latencyTimeouts & retries are tuned; slow ≠ down
Drop a dependencyCircuit breakers & fallbacks kick in (graceful degradation)
Fill the disk / CPU (Central Processing Unit)Resource limits & alerts fire before a crash
Zone/region outageFailover to another region works end-to-end
Tooling: Chaos Monkey / Simian Army, Gremlin, LitmusChaos and Chaos Mesh (Kubernetes).
Prerequisites
Don't Start Here
Chaos engineering pays off only once you already have solid monitoring and alerting (or you won't see what breaks) and the basics of resilience — retries, timeouts, health checks, redundancy. Injecting failure into a system you can't observe just causes an outage with no lesson. It's an advanced reliability practice that builds on observability and SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) fundamentals, not a starting point.
🧵 Distributed Tracing & OpenTelemetry Observability ▶
Concept
Following One Request Across Many Services
In a microservice system, a single user click might touch a gateway, an auth service, three back-ends, and a database. When it's slow, which hop is to blame? Logs from each service in isolation can't tell you. Distributed tracing stitches the whole journey together: it tags the request with an ID that travels with it, so you can see the end-to-end path and exactly where the time went.
Reference
Traces, Spans & Context
TermMeaning
TraceThe whole journey of one request, identified by a trace_id.
SpanOne unit of work within the trace (an HTTP call, a DB query) — with a start, duration, and metadata.
Parent/childSpans nest — a service call's span contains the spans of what it triggered.
Context propagationThe trace_id + span info passed between services (via headers like W3C traceparent) so the trace stays connected.
Rendered as a waterfall, a trace shows each span as a bar — one long bar instantly reveals the bottleneck.
Reference
The Three Pillars & Where Tracing Fits
PillarAnswers
Metrics"Is something wrong?" — aggregate numbers, cheap, great for alerts.
Logs"What happened here?" — detailed events from one component.
Traces"Where is it slow / failing?" — the request's path across services.
You use all three together: a metric alerts you, a trace localizes the slow hop, and logs on that span explain why.
Standard
OpenTelemetry — One SDK (Software Development Kit) to Instrument Them All
OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the vendor-neutral standard for generating traces, metrics, and logs. You instrument your code once with the OTel SDK (much of it automatic for popular frameworks), and it exports to whatever backend you choose — Jaeger, Tempo, Zipkin, or a commercial tool — with no code change to switch. That freedom from lock-in is why it won.
Practical noteDetail
SamplingTracing everything is expensive at scale — sample a fraction (head or tail sampling) while keeping error traces.
CollectorThe OTel Collector receives, batches, and routes telemetry so apps don't talk to backends directly.
Deploy the collector even when you think you do not need it, and decide sampling before the bill teaches you. Exporting straight from the application to a backend hard-codes the vendor into every service, which is the exact thing the standard exists to prevent — the collector is the seam that lets you change backend without redeploying anything. And sample by tail rather than by head where you can: keeping a fixed one per cent of traces reliably discards the slow ones, which are the only traces anybody ever opens.
🤖 SOAR — Security Orchestration, Automation & Response SecOps ▶
Concept
Automating the SOC (Security Operations Center)'s Busywork
A security team drowns in alerts — thousands a day, most of them the same repetitive triage: look up an IP (Internet Protocol)'s reputation, check if a user clicked the phishing link, isolate a host. SOAR platforms automate those workflows with playbooks: codified, if-this-then-that response procedures that run in seconds instead of an analyst doing 20 manual steps. The goal is to let humans focus on the hard decisions and let machines handle the rote work.
Reference
The Three Letters
CapabilityWhat It Does
OrchestrationConnect all your security tools (SIEM, EDR, firewall, threat intel, ticketing) so they act together via APIs (Application Programming Interface).
AutomationRun response steps without a human — enrich an alert, block an IP, disable an account.
ResponseManage the incident end to end — case tracking, timelines, and collaboration.
Orchestration is what most buyers get and automation is what they were sold. Connecting the tools is a project with a clear end; deciding which responses run without a human is a governance question that never finishes, and the platform will sit half-used until somebody is willing to own that decision. Start by automating enrichment — pulling the reputation, the asset owner, the user's recent logins into the alert — because it saves an analyst ten minutes per alert and cannot do damage if it is wrong.
Example
A Phishing Playbook
# Trigger: a user reports a suspicious email
1. Extract URLs, attachments, sender from the email
2. Enrich → check URL/hash against threat intel (VirusTotal, etc.)
3. IF malicious:
     - Search mail server for other copies → quarantine them
     - Check who else clicked (proxy/EDR logs)
     - Reset creds / isolate any host that visited the link
4. Open a case, attach all evidence, notify the analyst
5. ELSE: close as false positive with a note
Steps 1–2 and 5 run fully automatically; the risky containment in step 3 can require a human approval gate — a common "automate enrichment, gate the destructive action" pattern.
Reference
SOAR vs SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) & What to Watch
Detail
SIEM findsCollects & correlates logs to detect and alert.
SOAR actsTakes those alerts and responds via playbooks. They're complementary — SIEM feeds SOAR.
PayoffLower MTTR (Mean Time To Repair) (mean time to respond), consistent handling, less analyst burnout.
Watch outAutomating a bad decision at machine speed scales the mistake. Start with enrichment, gate containment, and test playbooks carefully.
The last row is the one to design around. Automation multiplies whatever judgement is encoded in the playbook, so a rule that isolates a host on a noisy detection will one day isolate the finance director mid-quarter-end, at three in the morning, correctly according to the playbook. Put a human approval in front of any action that is disruptive and hard to reverse, and automate freely everything that only gathers information.
📈 Load & Performance Testing Reliability ▶
Concept
Find the Breaking Point Before Your Users Do
A system that's fast for one user can collapse for ten thousand. Performance testing simulates realistic (and unrealistic) traffic to answer questions you can't answer by clicking around: How many users can we handle? Where's the bottleneck — CPU (Central Processing Unit), database, a single service? What happens at 2× expected peak? You'd much rather learn this in a test than during a product launch or a viral moment.
Reference
Types of Test
TestQuestion It Answers
LoadHow does it behave at expected peak traffic?
StressWhere does it break, and how? (Push past capacity.)
SpikeCan it survive a sudden traffic surge (a launch, a link going viral)?
Soak / enduranceDoes it degrade over hours/days? (Catches memory leaks, resource exhaustion.)
ScalabilityDoes adding servers actually increase throughput linearly?
Soak is the one that gets skipped and the one that finds the bug you actually ship. Load, stress and spike run for minutes and answer questions about capacity; memory leaks, connection-pool exhaustion and log-disk growth only appear after hours, which is exactly the failure that reaches production and takes a week to diagnose. Run one long test before a major release even if you run nothing else.
Metrics
Measure Percentiles, Not Averages
The classic mistake is reporting average latency. Averages hide pain: if 99% of requests are 50 ms but 1% take 5 seconds, the average looks fine while your worst-affected users suffer. Report percentiles.
MetricMeaning
ThroughputRequests/sec the system handles (RPS).
Latency p50 / p95 / p99The value below which 50/95/99% of requests fall. p99 = your unluckiest 1%.
Error rate% of failed requests — watch it climb as load rises.
SaturationHow full the resources are (CPU, memory, connections) — the leading indicator of trouble.
Alert on p99, report p50 beside it, and never publish a mean. The percentile is not a nicety — for a page that makes twenty backend calls, a one-in- a-hundred slow response means most page loads contain one, so p99 of a service is closer to the median experience of a user than p50 is. Averages survive in dashboards because they are reassuring, which is the reason to remove them.
Practice
Doing It Well
GuidelineWhy
Test like productionRealistic data volumes and traffic mix — a test on an empty DB (Database) lies.
Ramp up graduallyIncrease load in steps to see where metrics bend, not just where they snap.
Watch the whole stackCorrelate the test with server metrics/traces to find which component is the bottleneck.
Mind the environmentA scaled-down staging env gives relative, not absolute, numbers. Note the difference.
Toolsk6, JMeter, Gatling, Locust generate the traffic; pair with your observability stack.
The environment row is the one that invalidates results people then act on. A test against a scaled-down staging environment tells you the shape of the curve and nothing trustworthy about the number where it bends, and the number is what gets quoted in the capacity plan six months later. Either test against production-like capacity or publish the result as relative only — and ramping gradually is what makes even a relative result readable, because the point where the metrics bend is the finding.
🔥 Prometheus & Grafana — Metrics & Dashboards Observability ▶
Reference
The Open-Source Monitoring Standard
Prometheus scrapes metrics from targets, stores them as time-series, and lets you query with PromQL (Prometheus Query Language). Grafana visualizes them; Alertmanager routes the alerts. It's the de-facto stack for cloud-native metrics. 
# PromQL: 5xx error rate over the last 5 minutes, per service
sum by (service) (rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]))
  / sum by (service) (rate(http_requests_total[5m]))
PieceRole
PrometheusPull-based scraping + TSDB + PromQL
Exportersnode_exporter, cAdvisor — expose metrics to scrape
AlertmanagerDedup, group & route alerts to Slack/PagerDuty
GrafanaDashboards over Prometheus, Loki, and more
Scale note: Prometheus is single-node by design; Thanos/Mimir/Cortex add long-term storage and HA (High Availability). Instrument with OpenTelemetry to stay portable.
Silently wrong
Two PromQL Mistakes That Produce Confident, Wrong Dashboards
Averaging percentiles. The 99th percentile of a fleet is not the average of each instance's 99th percentile — percentiles do not aggregate, and avg(p99) is a number with no meaning that happens to look plausible. With Prometheus histograms the correct form aggregates the buckets first and computes the quantile last: histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(bucket[5m]))). Get it backwards and a dashboard understates the tail exactly when the tail is what you are being paged about.
Label cardinality. Every distinct combination of label values is a separate time series held in memory, so one label carrying a user id, a request id, a full URL (Uniform Resource Locator) path or an email address does not make the metric more useful — it multiplies the series count until the server OOMs. The fingerprint is a Prometheus that restarts under memory pressure shortly after a deploy, and the cause is one line of instrumentation. High-cardinality dimensions belong in logs or traces, which are built for them.
And remember the model is pull, which decides what you can monitor. A target that is not scraped has no data, and a short-lived job finishes between scrapes and is simply absent — which is what Pushgateway exists for, and why it should be reserved for batch jobs rather than used as a general ingress. The corollary is the useful one: up == 0 is a first-class signal, so a missing target is detectable in a way that a push-based system has to work at.
🚦 Golden Signals + RED & USE — What to Measure Observability ▶
Concept
Three Small Frameworks That Cut the Noise
You can graph a thousand metrics and still miss the outage. These frameworks tell you the few signals that actually indicate user pain — so alerts fire on symptoms, not on every twitch.
FrameworkMeasureBest for
Golden SignalsLatency, Traffic, Errors, SaturationAny service (Google SRE)
REDRate, Errors, DurationRequest-driven services
USEUtilization, Saturation, ErrorsResources (CPU, disk, queues)
Rule of thumb: alert on symptoms users feel (latency/errors), not causes (high CPU) — a busy CPU (Central Processing Unit) that still serves fast isn't an incident. Page on the golden signals; keep the rest for debugging.
The Fingerprint
The Frameworks Say What to Measure, Not Where — and the Gap Is the Last Mile
Golden Signals, RED and USE tell you which signals matter; they are silent on the measurement point, and that silence is where teams get burned. A service can report a wall of 200s and low latency while users are failing, because the metrics are taken behind the load balancer, the CDN (Content Delivery Network) and the retry layer — and the real errors live in the last mile the server never sees: DNS (Domain Name System), TLS (Transport Layer Security) handshakes, a bad CDN edge, the client's own network. “Alert on symptoms” is right, but the symptom that counts is the user's, which means measuring as close to the user as you can reach — real-user monitoring and synthetic probes from outside — not only at the service. The second trap: these signals are per-service, but one user request fans out across many services, so every service can look green while the end-to-end journey is broken.
Where you measureWhat it can and cannot see
Inside the serviceIts own errors and latency — blind to the edge and the network
At the load balancer / gatewayAggregate rate and errors — hides per-user and last-mile failures
Real-user monitoring (RUM)The actual experience, including client and network
Synthetic probes from outsideCatches last-mile and regional failure before users report it
So measure the golden signals at more than one altitude: keep the per-service view for debugging, but let the alert that pages sit as close to the user as you can get it — RUM and external synthetics — and define at least one signal on the whole journey, not just each hop. A dashboard of green services above an angry user is the classic sign you measured where it was easy, not where the pain is.
🎯 SLIs (Service Level Indicator), SLOs (Service Level Objective) & Error Budgets SRE ▶
Concept
Turning Reliability Into a Number You Can Spend
100% reliability is the wrong target — it's impossible and ruinously expensive. SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) instead sets an explicit reliability goal and treats the gap as a budget you can spend on shipping features.
TermMeaning
SLIThe measurement — e.g. % of requests < 300ms & 2xx
SLOThe target for that SLI — e.g. 99.9% over 30 days
Error budget1 − SLO = allowed unreliability (0.1% ≈ 43 min/month)
SLA (Service Level Agreement)The contractual promise (with penalties) — looser than the SLO
The policy: budget left → ship fast; budget burned → freeze features and fix reliability. Burn-rate alerts (spending the budget too fast) are the modern replacement for threshold alerts. This is the SRE card, made operational.
Silently wrong
An SLO Measured at the Load Balancer Is a Promise About the Load Balancer
The definition is the hard part, and the commonest failure is measuring where the data is convenient rather than where the user is. A server-side latency SLI starts counting when the request arrives and stops when the response is written — so it excludes DNS (Domain Name System), connection setup, the mobile network, and every request that never arrived because the load balancer was unreachable. That last one is the serious one: a total outage upstream of the measurement point produces a perfect SLI, because there are no failed requests, because there are no requests.
Definition choiceWhat it quietly excludes
Measured server-sideAnything that failed before reaching the server, including the outage
Averaged, not a ratio of good eventsThe tail — and an average of a bimodal distribution describes nobody
Per-request weightingThe user who made forty failed requests counts forty times; the one who gave up counts once
A 30-day rolling windowAn incident stays in the budget for 30 days, so a freeze can outlast the fix by weeks
One SLO for a whole serviceThe one endpoint that matters, averaged into the ninety that do not
Pick the smallest set of user journeys the service exists for, and measure those as close to the user as you can afford — real-user monitoring or a black-box prober outside your network, not the application's own view of itself. And pair every SLO with a synthetic check that fails when nothing is arriving, because the alert you most need is the one your own metrics cannot generate. How to choose the journeys is in Choosing SLIs & Setting an SLO That Survives.
🪵 Structured Logging & Log Pipelines Observability ▶
Concept
Logs You Can Actually Query
A log line like User 42 failed login from 1.2.3.4 is human-readable but painful to search at scale. Structured logging emits key-value/JSON so every field is filterable, and correlation IDs stitch a request's logs together across services. 
# Structured (JSON) — machine-queryable, ties to a trace
{"level":"warn","event":"login_failed","user_id":42,
 "src_ip":"1.2.3.4","trace_id":"abc123"}
PieceRole
Correlation / trace IDFollow one request across every service & log
CollectorFluent Bit / Vector / OTel (OpenTelemetry) Collector ship & enrich logs
StoreLoki, Elasticsearch, or a cloud logging service
Structured logs feed both ops and security (SIEM) — the same pipeline that helps you debug latency helps the blue team hunt. The three pillars — metrics, logs, traces — correlate via that shared ID.
Silently wrong
Structured Means the Fields Are Now Contractual — Including the Ones You Should Not Have Logged
Turning a sentence into fields makes the log queryable and makes its shape an interface. Two consequences follow immediately. A dashboard, an alert and a SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) rule now depend on event being called event — renaming it in a refactor breaks queries silently, because a query for a field that no longer exists returns zero results rather than an error, and zero results reads like good news. Version the schema, or at least treat field names as a public API (Application Programming Interface) with the same deprecation courtesy.
And a structured field is far easier to log by accident. Serialising a request object, a user record or an exception's local variables puts tokens, session ids, card numbers and personal data into a store that is replicated, backed up and retained for a year — and once there it is subject to every deletion obligation the rest of the estate has. Redaction belongs at the emitter, because a redaction rule in the collector fails open the day a new field appears.
DecisionThe failure it prevents
Deny-list at the emitter, plus a test that asserts on the payloadSecrets and personal data in a store you cannot easily purge
Sample high-volume success, keep all errorsA bill dominated by the logs nobody reads; sampling errors is how the one occurrence vanishes
One timestamp format and one time zone (UTC, RFC 3339)Correlating two services whose clocks disagree by an hour and a format
Propagate the trace id at the boundaryThe correlation id that exists in every service and is regenerated at each hop, correlating nothing
The question that decides log volume is what you would query. A log written and never read costs storage, ingest, and the attention of whoever scrolls past it during an incident. Write the query first — "which endpoint returned 500 for tenant X in the last hour" — and log exactly the fields that answer it; anything else belongs in a metric, which is orders of magnitude cheaper per event.
📟 On-Call Done Humanely SRE · Culture ▶
Concept
Sustainable Operations, Not Hero Burnout
On-call is necessary but corrosive when done badly — pages at 3am for non-issues drive burnout and attrition. Good on-call is designed: few, actionable alerts and a culture that fixes root causes.
PracticeDetail
Actionable alerts onlyIf a human can't act on it, it's a dashboard, not a page
RunbooksEvery alert links to "what to check & do"
Sane rotationsFollow-the-sun or weekly; comp time; not the same 2 people
Blameless postmortemsBlame systems, not people — psychological safety fixes more
Toil budgetCap manual work; automate what pages you repeatedly
The feedback loop that matters: every real page should make the next one less likely — via a fix, better alerting, or automation. That's the "Feedback" and "Learning" of the Three Ways, applied to operations.
The Fingerprint
Alert Fatigue Is a Death Spiral, and the Fix Is Deleting Alerts, Not Trying Harder
Every practice above fails ahead of one specific failure mode worth naming on its own. Alert fatigue is not tiredness; it is a death spiral. Noisy alerts train responders to mute, batch, or reflexively acknowledge without looking — so the one page that mattered lands in a channel nobody trusts and is missed. The usual response to the miss is to add more alerts “to catch it next time,” which worsens the noise, which deepens the reflex to ignore. The counterintuitive exit is to delete alerts aggressively: a responder who trusts that every page is real will answer every page, and that trust is worth more than any single alert. The numbers that locate you are the actionability ratio — what fraction of pages led to a human action — and pages per shift.
SymptomWhat it really meansThe move
Responders ack without investigatingThe channel has cried wolf; trust is goneDelete and tune until every page is real
“Add an alert so we catch it next time”Defensive noise that deepens the spiralAlert on the symptom, not another cause
Pages per shift in the dozensThe system pages on twitches, not incidentsMove causes to dashboards; page on user pain
Low page-to-action ratioMost pages are not actionableTreat each non-actionable page as a bug to remove
So past a point, on-call reliability is inversely related to alert count: the goal is the fewest, highest-signal pages you can get away with, because a muted responder is a bigger outage risk than any alert you deleted. Measure the actionability ratio, treat every non-actionable page as a defect, and run with fewer alerts than feels safe — the safety was an illusion the moment people stopped reading them.
🧨 Automation Risk & Discipline — Blast Radius, Approval Gates & the Kill Switch Ops • Automation ▶
Concept
Automation Multiplies Your Competence and Your Mistakes Equally
The reason to automate a task is that it does the same thing quickly, at scale, without tiring. That is also the reason a broken automation is so much worse than a broken human: a person disabling accounts by hand notices something is wrong around account five; a script notices nothing and finishes all ten thousand. Every automation worth trusting is designed around that asymmetry — the goal is not just to make it work, but to bound what it can do when it goes wrong.
Reference
The Controls That Bound the Damage
ControlWhat it prevents
Dry run / -WhatIfShows what would change before anything does — the cheapest safety there is
Sanity cap on scope"Refuse to act on more than N objects" — the guardrail that stops a bad filter from hitting everything
Approval gateA human confirms before irreversible or large-scale actions
IdempotencySafe to re-run; re-running after a partial failure does not double-apply
Staged rolloutAct on a pilot slice, verify, then widen — never the whole fleet at once
Kill switchA way to stop a running job now, and a known way to halt the next scheduled run
The sanity cap is the single highest-value line of code. Most automation disasters are a query that matched far more than intended; a script that aborts when the target set is implausibly large turns a catastrophe into a caught error.
Trust
An Automation Nobody Can Audit Is a Liability, Not an Asset
The difference between a trusted automation and a time bomb is whether anyone can answer "what did it do, and why did it do that" after the fact. That answer comes from logging every consequential action with enough context to reconstruct the run — inputs, the set it acted on, the result, and who or what triggered it. An unlogged script that touches production is a decision to fly blind, made silently.
RecordSo you can answer
Trigger & identityWho or what started this run, and as whom
The target setExactly which objects it acted on
Outcome per itemSucceeded, skipped, failed — and why
Version / sourceWhich revision of the script ran
The target set is the row that lets you answer the only question anyone asks afterwards. "Which accounts did it disable last Tuesday" is unanswerable from a log that records the run and not the objects, and that is the question that arrives with a lawyer attached. Log the set before acting on it as well as the outcome per item — the difference between the two is where a filter that matched more than intended becomes visible.
Lifecycle
From Script to Service — the Part Everyone Skips
A script that started as one person's time-saver quietly becomes load-bearing, and then that person leaves. An automation that touches production has an owner, lives in version control, carries a header that explains what it does and how to stop it, and has a named retirement plan for when it is no longer needed. "It's just a script" is how critical infrastructure ends up depending on a file on someone's desktop that no one else understands — the same shadow-dependency risk, in automation form.
🧾 Compliance Evidence as an Operational Output OPS • Governance ▶
The Inversion
Auditors Want Proof a Control Ran, Not a Description of It
The annual audit scramble happens because teams document what a control is and then, when asked, cannot show that it ran. An auditor does not want the policy that says access is reviewed quarterly; they want the four dated review records with names against them. Evidence is a byproduct of operating a control — but only if the control was designed to emit it. Designed to emit nothing, it produces a frantic reconstruction every audit, and reconstruction is exactly what an auditor is trained to distrust.
What teams haveWhat the auditor asks for
“We review access quarterly”The four reviews, dated, with who did them
“Changes go through approval”This change's approval record, before it shipped
“We patch monthly”The patch report for each month, showing coverage
“Backups are tested”The last restore test, with its result
The gap is always the same: the left column is a claim, the right column is an artefact with a date. A control that runs but leaves no dated artefact is, to an auditor, a control that cannot be shown to have run — which is indistinguishable from one that did not.
The Decision
Design the Evidence at the Same Time as the Control
The move that converts the annual scramble into a non-event is to decide, when you build or change a control, what artefact it will produce and where that artefact will live — a ticket, a signed approval, a dated report, a log query with a saved definition. The evidence then accumulates as a side effect of the work, and audit becomes a matter of collecting what already exists.
For every control that matters, answer one question up front — “what dated proof does this leave, and where?” — and wire it into the process so nobody has to remember. This is The Audit Process — Proving Controls Actually Work seen from the operations side: the auditor's job gets easy when the evidence was an output of running the control, not a project to find it afterwards.
🎯 Incident vs Problem vs Change vs Request — The Distinction That Routes Everything Service Management ▶
Concept
Four Words, Four Different Clocks and Four Different Goals
These are treated as ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) vocabulary and dismissed as bureaucracy, which is a mistake with a cost. They are four different kinds of work with genuinely different success conditions, and conflating them is why service desks fix the same thing forty times and why real improvements never get funded. The distinction is not paperwork; it is what decides who works the ticket, how fast, and what "done" means.
TypeGoalDone whenClock
IncidentRestore serviceThe user is working again — even on a workaroundUrgent; the SLA (Service Level Agreement) is running
ProblemFind and remove the causeThe underlying fault is understood and fixed or acceptedDeliberate; measured in weeks
ChangeAlter something safelyThe alteration is live and verifiedScheduled, with approval
RequestDeliver a standard thingThe person has what they asked forPredictable; a catalogue promise
The failure this table prevents is the incident that closes without a problem record. Restoring service is urgent and satisfying; the fault that caused it is nobody's this week, so the same incident returns monthly and each occurrence is closed inside the SLA. If your service desk looks healthy on incident metrics and everyone knows which three things keep breaking, you are running the first row well and not running the second row at all.
Concept
Restoring Service Is Not Fixing the Cause — and That Is Correct
The most useful consequence of the split: during an incident, a workaround that gets people working is a complete success, even if nobody yet knows why it broke. The instinct to keep digging while users are down is well-meant and wrong. The digging is the problem record, raised from the incident and worked on a different clock by people who are not being paged.

Same incident for the fourth time this monthThe problem record is overdue; the repeat count is the business case

The last row matters more than it looks: requests logged as incidents corrupt every availability metric the service desk reports, because nothing was ever down.
Reference
Known Error — The Record That Saves the Most Time
A known error is a problem whose cause is understood but not yet fixed, recorded with its workaround. It is the highest-value artifact a service desk produces and the one most often skipped, because writing it feels like admitting defeat rather than banking knowledge.

⚖️ Priority, Impact & Urgency — A Matrix People Actually Apply Service Management ▶
Concept
Priority Is Derived, Not Chosen
When priority is a free choice, everything becomes high, because every requester believes their thing is urgent and no agent wants the argument. Deriving it from two independently assessable questions removes the negotiation: impact is how much of the business is affected, urgency is how fast the damage grows. Neither is a matter of who is asking.

Making the Two Axes Answerable Without Judgement Calls
A matrix only removes arguments if its inputs are concrete. Define both axes in terms an agent can check in thirty seconds, and write the definitions into the ticket form itself.
AxisAsk
ImpactHow many people cannot work? Is it a service on the critical list? Is money or safety involved?
UrgencyIs it getting worse? Is there a deadline it will miss? Does a workaround exist?
Write both questions into the ticket form, in these words. A matrix in a policy document is a matrix nobody consults at the moment of triage; the same two questions as required fields are answered by whoever raises the ticket, and "how many people cannot work" is a number rather than an opinion. That single change moves priority from a negotiation into a calculation, which is the whole point of having a matrix.
Concept
The VIP Problem, Named Honestly
Every organisation has people whose tickets move faster than the matrix says. Pretending otherwise makes the process a fiction that agents route around. The workable answer is to make it explicit and bounded: seniority may raise urgency where the person's work genuinely gates others, and it never changes impact. A blanket VIP flag that jumps every ticket to P1 teaches everyone that priority is political, and the matrix stops meaning anything.
🎫 Writing a Ticket Someone Else Can Solve Service Management ▶
Concept
The Ticket Is Read by Someone Who Was Not There
Resolution time is decided less by technical difficulty than by whether the next person has to go back and ask. Every round trip to the user costs hours of wall-clock time and a chunk of their goodwill. A ticket written for a stranger — which is what the next reader is — eliminates most of them.

"Already tried" is the field that saves the most hours and is left blank the most often. Without it every subsequent engineer repeats the first twenty minutes, and the user is asked to reboot for the third time — which is where their goodwill goes. The exact error text matters for the same reason: it is searchable, and "doesn't work" is not. Write every ticket as though the next reader is a stranger, because on any queue worth the name they are.
Technique

Users do not write bad tickets out of carelessness; they do not know what matters. These three questions, asked in this order, produce a workable ticket from almost anyone, and they fit on a form.

🔀 Triage & Categorisation — Routing Correctly the First Time Service Management ▶
Concept
A Misroute Costs More Than a Slow Answer
A ticket sent to the wrong queue does not merely wait — it waits, gets read by someone who cannot help, gets bounced with a one-line note, and arrives at the right team with its clock already spent and its context degraded. Two or three of those and a routine issue has breached its target without anyone doing anything wrong.
Triage decisionMade from

Route from the service catalogue, not from the technology named in the ticket. Users describe the tool they were touching, and the tool is frequently owned by a team that does not own the service — that mismatch is what produces the bounce. The other quiet win here is the fourth row: searching known errors before routing resolves a real share of tickets at first contact, and every one of those never enters anybody's queue at all.
Technique
Categorise by Service, Not by Technology
Category trees built from technology — "Network", "Windows", "Database" — require the person categorising to already know the cause, which is precisely what they do not know at triage. Trees built from the user-visible service — "Email", "Printing", "Finance system" — can be chosen from the symptom, which is all anyone has at that moment.

⬆️ Escalation — Functional vs Hierarchical, and Handing Over Without Losing Context Service Management ▶
Concept
Two Escalations That Are Constantly Confused
Functional escalation moves a ticket sideways to people with different skills. Hierarchical escalation moves it upward to people with different authority. They solve different problems, and using the wrong one is why "escalating" sometimes means a manager is now watching a ticket nobody has any new ideas about.

Both misuse rows describe the same underlying act: escalating instead of deciding. Functional escalation used to move a ticket you do not want is visible within a week and corrodes trust between teams; hierarchical escalation used as pressure teaches managers that shouting works and produces a queue prioritised by volume. Technique
The Handover That Does Not Lose the Thread
Most of the value destroyed in escalation is context. The receiving engineer re-derives what the last one already knew, and the user is asked the same questions again. A handover note with five fields costs two minutes and saves an hour — and it is the same note that makes a shift change survivable.

The last field is the one engineers omit and the one that prevents the worst outcome: two people giving a stressed user two different stories. During a major incident this discipline is formalised — see Incident Command — Running a Major Incident Without Chaos.
✅ Closing Well — Resolution Notes, Root Cause & the Article That Follows Service Management ▶
Concept
The Close Is Where a Ticket Either Compounds or Evaporates
At the moment of closing, the agent holds everything: the symptom, the false leads, the actual cause, the fix. Ten minutes later it is gone, and the next occurrence starts from zero. "Resolved" as a resolution note is the single most expensive habit on a service desk, because it converts solved work into work that must be solved again.
Resolution note containsSo that

Anything unusual about this environmentThe exception is documented, not rediscovered
The workaround row is the one with consequences beyond the ticket. "Fixed" and "worked around" look identical in a queue and mean opposite things to the problem record: the honest note is what lets somebody count how often this recurs and justify fixing the cause. Ten minutes at the close is the cheapest documentation anybody will ever write, because it is the only moment when the false leads are still remembered.
Technique

Most closure code lists are unusable because every option means "fixed". A short list that distinguishes genuinely different outcomes turns the close field into the service desk's most honest data source.

Concept

🐌 "Why Is My Laptop Slow?" — The Commonest Ticket, Worked Properly Service Management ▶
Core Concept
"Slow" Is Four Different Tickets Wearing One Word
"My computer is slow." It is the commonest sentence a service desk reads, and it is not a symptom — it is a feeling. The desk that treats it as a symptom starts guessing: a reimage here, a RAM (Random Access Memory) upgrade there, a reboot that appears to work. The computer in front of you has one of four unrelated problems, the fixes are mutually exclusive, and one question separates them before you touch anything — what were you doing when it felt slow? Ask it of a desktop, a laptop or a virtual desktop; the four are the same everywhere and only the fixes differ.
They describeWhich ticket it really isWhat that rules out
"It takes forever to get going in the morning"Slow to start — boot, logon, startup apps, policy processingNothing about the machine's speed once it is up
"It just freezes for a second while I type"Slow to respond — a resource is saturated during workBoot time; the machine may start perfectly
"It's fine except when I open that"Slow at one thing — one application, one file, one add-inThe whole machine. This is an app ticket wearing a hardware costume
"Everything on the shared drive crawls"Slow on the network — or the server at the other endThe endpoint entirely. It is very likely not this user's problem at all
The fourth row is the one that wastes the most engineer-hours. A file-server or VPN (Virtual Private Network) problem arrives at the desk one laptop at a time, each ticket plausible on its own, and it gets diagnosed as four slow machines before somebody notices that all four are slow at the same hour. Triage & Categorisation — Routing Correctly the First Time is what catches it; the question above is what makes triage possible.
Reference
The Four Resources, From the User's Chair and From the Counter
Every "slow to respond" ticket is one of four resources running out. They feel almost identical to the person using the machine and look completely different in Task Manager, Activity Monitor or top — which is why the tool comes out before the promise does.
ResourceWhat the user saysWhat the counter showsUsual cause on an endpoint
CPU (Central Processing Unit)"The fan never stops"Sustained high utilisation, one process owning most of itA runaway browser tab, an indexing or antivirus scan that never finished, or the machine throttling because it is hot

Disk"Everything pauses together"Active time pinned near 100%, long queueA failing drive, a nearly full SSD (Solid State Drive), or real-time scanning on a folder that changes constantly

Memory pressure is the one people misread, and it misreads in both directions. A modern operating system deliberately fills unused memory with cache, so "95% used" on a machine that feels fine is correct behaviour and not a finding. What matters is whether the machine is paging — trading disk for memory it does not have — which shows up as disk activity while the user swears they are not saving anything. Read the pair, never the percentage. The server-side version of this framework is Performance & Capacity — Finding and Fixing Bottlenecks.
Silently wrong

The decision
Fix, Upgrade or Replace — and Writing It Down So It Is Decided Once
Diagnosis ends in a decision that is mostly not technical: whether the remaining life of this machine is worth the part. The engineer is the only person who can price it, and the ticket is where that pricing has to be written or the question arrives again next quarter.

A ticket that records "45 minutes plus a £60 drive against a £900 replacement" turns the next identical case into a five-minute decision by somebody more junior, and it is the evidence a refresh budget is actually built from. That is the same argument as Writing a Ticket Someone Else Can Solve pointed at the finance conversation rather than at the next engineer.
🌊 Working a Queue — Prioritisation, Batching & Not Drowning Service Management ▶
Technique
The Order That Beats Working Top to Bottom
A queue worked strictly in order is worked badly: quick wins sit behind long jobs, tickets about the same outage get investigated three times, and the oldest ones age past their target while everyone is busy. A short pass over the whole queue before starting is worth more than the time it costs, every time.
#PassWhy first
1Scan for anything that is actually a P1A major incident hiding in the queue is the worst thing that can happen to a shift
2Group duplicates and same-cause ticketsOne investigation, many closures
3Clear the two-minute jobsQueue length drops, and so does the number of people waiting
4Unblock anything waiting on youSomeone else's clock is running on your reply
5Then work by priority and ageThe remaining work, in the order the matrix says
Pass 1 is the one that justifies the whole routine. A major incident sitting unread in a queue is the worst outcome on any service desk, and it arrives looking like an ordinary ticket from one user who noticed first. Everything after it is throughput; that pass is risk. Do the scan at the start of every shift and after every lunch, and treat a cluster of similar tickets in pass 2 as a P1 candidate rather than as five closures.
Technique
Batching, and the Interruption Arithmetic
Context switching is the hidden tax on service-desk work. Handling tickets of a kind together — all the access requests, all the printer issues — reuses the mental context and often the same tooling. The counter-pressure is real: batching delays individual tickets, so it belongs to routine work and never to anything with a live clock.
PracticeEffect
Batch routine requests into blocksTwo or three times the throughput on that category
Separate a "triage" role from a "solve" role on busy shiftsNew tickets get routed fast without derailing deep work
Set a status that means something"Waiting on user" is not "in progress"; the metrics depend on the difference
Timebox the ticket that is eating the dayAt the limit, escalate or park it — one ticket must not consume a shift
Timeboxing is the row that protects the other three. Batching, split roles and honest statuses all assume somebody is working the queue; one ticket allowed to eat a day removes the person from it entirely, and the day is usually lost to a problem that needed a different skill an hour in. Set the limit in advance — two hours, say — because at the moment you hit it you will feel closest to solving it, and that feeling is the least reliable signal on the desk.
Concept
A Permanently Full Queue Is a Staffing Fact, Not a Personal Failure
Individual technique has a ceiling. If the queue grows regardless of how well it is worked, the arrival rate exceeds the service rate, and no amount of prioritisation changes that — only more capacity, fewer arrivals, or a smaller scope will. Agents who do not know this conclude they are the problem, and burn out proving otherwise.
Evidence to gatherArgues for
Arrival rate vs closure rate over weeksThe gap, stated as a number rather than a feeling
Top categories by volumeThe shift-left or self-service candidate
Repeat tickets on the same causeA problem record that would remove the arrivals

The personal cost of running at capacity indefinitely is covered by Burnout — Recognizing It Before It Breaks You in the Mind & Wellbeing domain. The data above is how you make the case before it gets that far.
🗣️ Explaining Technical Things to Non-Technical People — A Repeatable Method Service Management ▶
Technique
Impact First, Then Cause, Then Detail Only If Asked
Technical people explain in the order they investigated: cause, mechanism, then consequence. The listener — a manager, a customer, a colleague from another department — needs the reverse. Lead with what it means for them, follow with a one-line cause, and stop — offering detail rather than delivering it. The person who wants the mechanism will ask, and the person who does not has already got what they needed.
OrderSentence
1. Impact"Email is down for the Manchester office."
2. Status"We know the cause and we are fixing it now."
3. Time"We expect it back within the hour; I will update you at 3pm either way."
4. What they should do"Use your phone for email until then."
5. Cause, briefly"A network change this morning had a side effect."
Point 3 is the one that buys the most goodwill and the one most often left out. A promised update at a stated time — kept even when there is no news — is worth more than a faster fix delivered in silence.
Concept
Analogies Help Until They Are Load-Bearing
An analogy is a bridge to a decision, not a model to reason from. It works when the listener only needs to grasp the shape of the situation; it fails when they start deducing consequences from the analogy rather than from the system. Use one, then return to the actual thing before anyone acts on it.
Instead ofSay
"DNS (Domain Name System) resolution is failing""The system that turns names into addresses is not answering, so nothing can find the server"
"We need to fail over to the secondary""We are switching to the backup system; there will be a short interruption"
"It's a certificate expiry""A security credential expired overnight — the fix is quick, but it must be done carefully"

😤 Difficult Conversations — Angry Users, VIP Pressure & Saying No Service Management ▶
Technique
Anger Is Almost Always About Being Stuck, Not About You
By the time someone is angry at the service desk — on the phone, at the desk, or in a ticket written in capitals — they have usually lost time, missed something, and been unable to do anything about it. The anger is about helplessness, and the person who answers the phone is simply the first one who can affect it. Reacting to the content rather than the tone is both kinder and more effective, and it is a learnable sequence rather than a personality trait.
#MoveSounds like
1Acknowledge the cost, not the emotion"You have lost the whole morning to this — understood."
2Show you have the facts"It started at nine, and rebooting did not help."
3Say what happens now"I am doing X, and I will know within twenty minutes."
4Commit to a next contact"I will call you by eleven whether or not it is fixed."
5Do exactly that—
Never say "calm down", and never defend the department. Both are heard as "your problem is not important", which is the thing that made them angry in the first place.
Technique
Saying No Without Saying "No"
Most refusals at a service desk are not refusals of the goal — they are refusals of the specific route. Separating the two turns a confrontation into a redirect, and the phrasing that works is a statement of what you can do, followed by the path for the rest.
SituationResponse
Asks for admin rights"I cannot grant that, but I can install what you need now, or get standing approval through X."
Wants a policy exception"That decision sits with the security team — I will raise it and tell you the answer."

The last row is the one where holding the line matters most, and where writing the request and the refusal into the ticket protects everyone involved — including the requester, who is often being pressured themselves.

📖 Knowledge Management — KCS in Practice, and Keeping Articles From Rotting Service Management ▶
Concept
Capture in the Workflow, Not in a Documentation Sprint
Knowledge-Centred Service rests on one insight: knowledge is a by-product of solving tickets, and it must be captured while solving them or it will not be captured at all. Every organisation that schedules "documentation time" instead discovers that the time is the first thing cancelled when the queue grows — which is exactly when the knowledge is most valuable.
PrincipleIn practice

"Publish the imperfect one" is the principle that makes the other four possible. A team that only publishes polished articles publishes almost nothing, because polish is what gets deferred; a rough article that solves the ticket is worth more than a perfect one that does not exist, and the fourth row is how it gets better. The one to enforce as a rule rather than a value is search before solving, since it is also what tells you whether any of this is working.
Technique
Write in the User's Words, Not the Cause's
Articles are found by searching the symptom, and the symptom is described in the language of the person who has it. An article titled with the technical cause is invisible to precisely the person who needs it — they do not know the cause; that is why they are searching.

Concept

Knowledge bases decay silently: the console changes, the version moves, the article stays. Scheduled review by a documentation owner sounds right and rarely survives contact with real workloads. What does work is making correction part of use — the agent who follows an article and finds step 4 wrong is the one person guaranteed to know what it should say, and is already there.

An article that was opened and then followed by an escalation is a document that looked right and was not, which is worse than a missing one — it cost the agent time and confidence. Wire that link between article view and ticket outcome even crudely, and the review queue orders itself by evidence instead of by age.

📜 SLAs (Service Level Agreement), OLAs & Underpinning Contracts — The Chain of Promises Service Management ▶
Reference
Three Agreements, and Why the Chain Must Hold
The promise made to the business is only deliverable if every internal team and every vendor behind it has made a compatible promise. The classic failure is a four-hour SLA to the business resting on a next-business-day hardware contract — a chain that cannot hold, agreed by people who never compared the two documents.
AgreementBetweenFails when

Concept
What an SLA Should and Should Not Promise
Most SLA disputes come from promising a resolution time for problems whose difficulty is unknown at the moment of promising. Response is controllable; resolution frequently is not. 🗄️ Asset & Configuration Management — A CMDB (Configuration Management Database) That Stays True Service Management ▶
Concept
Asset Management and Configuration Management Are Different Jobs
Asset management tracks the things you own for financial and lifecycle reasons: what it cost, who has it, when its warranty ends, when it gets replaced. Configuration management tracks how things depend on each other so that a change or an incident can be reasoned about. They overlap in the records and diverge completely in purpose, and a database built for one is usually poor at the other.

Technique

CMDBs fail by being too ambitious: model everything, populate it by hand, update it by process. Manual data decays from the day it is entered, and a database people know is stale is consulted by nobody, which removes the last feedback that would have corrected it. The rule that breaks the cycle is to record only what something else keeps true.

It is also the data Change Management – The Process That Prevents Self-Inflicted Outages in the Governance domain assumes you have when assessing a change's blast radius.

📅 Capacity & Shift Planning — Staffing a Queue That Has a Shape Service Management ▶
Concept

Service-desk demand has a shape: a Monday-morning peak, a post-lunch second wave, spikes after every change window and every term or quarter start. Staffing to the daily average guarantees a queue in the peak and idle agents in the trough, and the daily average is the number most often used, because it is the one the reporting produces by default.
Look atReveals

Concept

This is queueing theory rather than management opinion, and it is deeply unintuitive: as utilisation approaches capacity, waiting time does not rise proportionally — it rises sharply, because there is no slack to absorb the random clustering of arrivals. A team planned at 95% utilisation is not efficient; it is a team whose waiting times will swing wildly for reasons nobody can point at.

💥 Cardinality — The Number That Decides Your Observability Bill Observability ▶
Concept
One Label Multiplies; Two Labels Multiply Together
In a metrics system, a distinct combination of label values is a distinct time series, and the cost of the system is essentially the number of active series. Adding a label with ten values to a metric with a hundred series produces a thousand. Adding a second label with fifty values produces fifty thousand. The multiplication is invisible in the code — it is one extra argument — and it is the single most common cause of an observability bill that doubles without anyone deciding anything. 
# 4 endpoints x 5 status codes = 20 series. Fine.
http_requests_total{endpoint="/orders", status="200"}

# Add user_id: 4 x 5 x 800,000 = the incident.
http_requests_total{endpoint="/orders", status="200", user_id="a91f…"}
The rule that prevents nearly all of it: a metric label must have a small, bounded set of values that you could write down. Anything unbounded — a user identifier, a request ID, a URL (Uniform Resource Locator) with parameters, an error message, an email address — is not a label. It belongs in a log or a trace, where the cost model is per-event rather than per-combination.
Reference

Nobody labels a metric with a user ID on purpose. These are the routes it takes instead, and every one of them has been someone's outage.
SourceWhy it is unbounded

Version or build identifierBounded but ever-growing; needs retention, not exclusion

Anything from user inputAttacker-controlled cardinality is a denial-of-wallet attack
Histogram buckets multiplied by other labelsEach bucket is a series; the multiplication is easy to miss
The last row is worth pausing on: a histogram with twelve buckets, three labels of five values each, is 12 × 125 = 1,500 series from one instrument. Histograms are the right tool for latency and they are expensive, so give them fewer labels than counters.
Troubleshooting
Controlling It Before the Invoice Does
Cardinality problems are discovered in one of two ways: a query that used to work times out, or finance asks a question. Both are late. These make it visible while it is still cheap to fix.

The last row is the reframing that matters. High-cardinality questions — what happened to this customer, on this request — are legitimate and important. Metrics are simply the wrong store for them, and reaching for events instead answers the question better and more cheaply than a label ever would.
🧩 The Three Signals & Wide Events — What Each One Is Bad At Observability ▶
Concept
Metrics, Logs and Traces Are Usually Taught by What They Are Good For
The standard introduction lists the three pillars and their strengths, which is true and does not help you choose. What decides a design is the weaknesses — because in practice you pick a signal, discover six months later that it cannot answer the question you now have, and re-instrument. Knowing the failure mode in advance is the whole value of the taxonomy.
SignalBad atCost model
MetricsAnything per-request; anything you did not think to labelPer series — grows with cardinality, not with traffic
LogsAggregation; correlation across services; consistencyPer volume — grows linearly with traffic, forever
TracesRare events, once sampling discards them; cost at full fidelityPer span, mitigated by sampling — which is what loses the rare event
Read the middle column together and a pattern appears: metrics lose the detail, logs lose the structure, traces lose the rare case. Most organisations run all three pipelines, separately instrumented, separately stored and separately queried — and still cannot answer questions that fall between them.
Concept
Wide Events — One Record Per Unit of Work
The alternative approach: emit one structured event per request, carrying everything known about it — route, latency, status, customer tier, region, feature flags, cache outcome, database time, build version, whatever else the code has in hand. Perhaps a hundred fields. Then compute aggregates from those events at query time rather than pre-deciding them at instrumentation time.
What this buys is the ability to ask a question nobody anticipated. "Slow requests are only for customers on the new plan, in one region, when a particular flag is on" is a three-way breakdown that no pre-aggregated metric contains, and which arbitrary-cardinality events answer in one query. That is the actual definition of observability — the ability to answer unknown-unknowns without shipping new code — as opposed to monitoring, which answers the questions you already had.
PropertyThree pipelinesWide events
New question needs new codeUsually yesUsually no
High-cardinality breakdownsProhibitiveThe point
Cost driverSeries count and log volumeEvent count and width
Long-range aggregatesCheap — metrics are already rolled upNeeds downsampling or a metrics layer alongside
Tooling maturity in most shopsEverywhereDepends on the backend supporting it
The first row is the one that decides it for most teams. If answering a new question about production reliably means shipping code and waiting a week, you are paying for that latency on every incident — and wide events remove it for exactly the questions nobody anticipated, which are the ones that matter at 2am. Keep a metrics layer alongside for the long-range aggregates and the alerting; the two approaches are complements, and choosing wide events does not mean deleting the dashboards.
Troubleshooting
A Realistic Position for Most Teams
This is presented in the industry as a choice of camp, which is unhelpful, because the pragmatic answer combines them and is available today on ordinary tooling.
UseFor

The practical first step is smaller than adopting a philosophy: stop emitting a log line per stage of a request and emit one wide event at the end instead. It reduces volume, removes the correlation problem within a service entirely, and is the same amount of code. The logging discipline underneath is in Structured Logging & Log Pipelines.
🔧 Instrumentation Strategy — What to Instrument First in an Unfamiliar System Observability ▶
Concept
Instrument the Boundaries First, Because That Is Where the Contracts Are
Inheriting a system with no useful telemetry, the instinct is to start where the complexity is — the gnarly module everyone warns you about. That is the wrong end. Start at every point where the service talks to something it does not control: inbound requests, outbound calls, database queries, queue publishes and consumes, cache operations. Boundaries are where latency is spent, where failures originate, and where a contract exists that can be violated.
A second reason to start there: boundary instrumentation is mostly free. Auto- instrumentation libraries cover HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) servers and clients, database drivers and message brokers without touching business logic, so the first pass is a dependency and a configuration change rather than a project.
Reference
The Order, and What Each Step Unlocks
Each stage is useful on its own, which matters — this is work that gets interrupted, and an order where every step pays off independently survives interruption.

🛠️ OpenTelemetry Architecture & the Collector as a Control Point Observability ▶
Concept
Four Pieces, and Only One of Them Is in Your Application
OpenTelemetry is discussed as one thing and is four, with a deliberate separation that is the whole reason to adopt it: the API (Application Programming Interface) your code calls, the SDK (Software Development Kit) that implements it, the collector that receives and processes telemetry, and the exporters that send it onward. The separation means your application code never names a vendor. Changing backend becomes a collector configuration change rather than a re-instrumentation project across every service.
PieceLives inChanges when

CollectorA sidecar, a daemon or a central serviceRouting, filtering, redaction, cost control

Instrumentation lock-in is the problem this solves and it is worth naming, because it is the reason organisations stay on observability vendors they have outgrown: the cost of leaving is re-instrumenting every service, which nobody will fund.
Reference
What the Collector Is Actually For
Teams commonly send telemetry straight from the application to the backend and treat the collector as an optional hop. It is the single most useful component in the stack, because it is the one place every signal passes through — and therefore the one place a policy can be applied without changing any application.

🎲 Sampling — Head, Tail, and Keeping the Traces That Matter Observability ▶
Concept
Uniform Sampling Discards Exactly What You Needed
Keeping one percent of traces is the default answer to trace cost, and it has a specific failure: the traces you want are the rare ones. An error affecting one request in ten thousand, sampled at one percent, is captured once per million requests — so during the incident, the trace you need does not exist. Meanwhile you have retained ten thousand identical successful traces of the same healthy endpoint, which nobody will ever look at.
Uniform sampling is the right tool for one job only — estimating aggregate rates, where any unbiased sample works. For investigation, which is what traces are actually for, the policy should be biased on purpose: keep everything unusual, keep a thin baseline of the ordinary.
Reference
Head, Tail and Where Each One Breaks
The distinction is about when the keep-or-drop decision is made, and it determines what information the decision can use.
Head samplingTail sampling

The head-sampling consistency row causes a specific, confusing symptom: services making independent sampling decisions produce traces with holes in them, where a middle service is simply absent. If your traces look incomplete, check that the sampling decision is propagated in the trace context rather than re-rolled at each hop.
Troubleshooting
A Policy That Works
Most teams need a small set of rules rather than a sampling strategy document. This one is a reasonable default and each rule earns its place.
RuleRateReason

One consequence to hold onto: once sampling is biased, trace counts are no longer traffic counts. Anyone computing an error rate by counting sampled traces will get a number that is wildly wrong in the alarming direction. Rates come from metrics, which are unsampled; traces answer "what happened in this case". Keeping that division clear prevents a whole category of confident, incorrect dashboards.
🔦 Debugging With Traces, Correlating Signals & Dashboards Worth Keeping Observability ▶
Concept
The Workflow That Finds Something Nobody Predicted
Dashboards answer questions somebody anticipated. The investigation that matters starts with a symptom nobody has a chart for, and it has a repeatable shape: start from the symptom, compare the bad population against the good one, find the attribute that separates them, then read one trace to understand the mechanism. Most engineers do the last step first — open a trace and stare at it — which shows what one request did without telling you whether it is representative.
StepQuestion
1What exactly is the symptom, in a number a user would feel?
2Which requests have it — define the bad population precisely
3How do they differ from the good ones? Break down by every attribute you have
4Which single attribute best separates the two populations?
5Now open a representative trace, knowing what it represents
6What in that trace explains the separation you found in step 4?
Step 3 is where wide events pay for themselves, and it is why the width matters more than the depth: an attribute you did not record cannot separate anything, and the field that explains the outage is regularly one somebody added "just in case".
Reference
Correlation — The IDs That Make It Possible
Moving between signals — from a metric spike to the traces behind it, from a trace to the logs of one span — is the difference between an investigation that takes minutes and one that takes an afternoon. 💰 Error Budgets, Policy & Why SLOs (Service Level Objective) Fail — Which Is Rarely Technical Observability ▶
Concept
The Budget Is Permission to Take Risk, Not a Prediction of Failure
An SLO of 99.9% means one thing in a hundred is allowed to fail, and the error budget is that allowance made explicit and spendable. The reframing matters more than the arithmetic: unspent budget is not a triumph, it is evidence of over-investment in reliability at the expense of shipping. A service that never touches its budget is either more reliable than it needs to be, or has an SLO set too loose to mean anything.
This is what makes the error budget the most useful idea in the SLO toolkit. It converts an argument between engineers who want to stabilise and product managers who want to ship into a shared number that answers it, and both sides can read it the same way.
Reference

🧱 Blast Radius Design — Bulkheads, Cells & Shuffle Sharding Reliability ▶
Concept
You Cannot Prevent Failure; You Can Decide How Much of It Reaches Users
Past a certain scale, reliability work stops being about preventing failures and starts being about bounding them. Something will exhaust a connection pool, a poison message will crash a consumer, a deploy will be bad, a single customer will send traffic nobody anticipated. The design question is not whether that happens — it is how many users notice when it does. A system where any one failure affects everyone has, in effect, chosen one hundred percent as its blast radius by default.
Each technique below trades efficiency for containment, and the trade is explicit: partitioned resources sit idle that a shared pool would have used. That inefficiency is what you are buying, and it is worth naming plainly, because it is the argument that will be made against every one of these designs.
Reference

Start at the top. The first two are configuration changes in an afternoon; the last two are architecture.
TechniqueContainsCost
Separate connection pools and thread pools per dependencyOne slow dependency consuming all capacitySome idle capacity; a config change

Separate queues per priority or tenantA poison message or a backlog blocking unrelated workMore queues to operate

Do the first two before discussing the last three. Separate pools and per-tenant limits are an afternoon's configuration and prevent the most common failure by a wide margin — one slow dependency or one loud customer consuming all capacity. Cells and shuffle sharding are genuine architecture with an operating cost that lands on the team every week afterwards, and a team that jumps there first usually has neither the routing maturity nor the appetite to run them.
Concept
Shuffle Sharding — The Combinatorics That Do the Work
Plain sharding assigns each customer to one shard: eight shards means a failed shard takes out an eighth of customers, and those customers are down completely. Shuffle sharding gives each customer a random pair of workers instead. The number of distinct pairs from eight workers is twenty-eight, so two customers share both workers only rarely — and a customer whose one worker is unhealthy still has another.
DesignOne worker failsOne customer is abusive
Shared pool of 8Degraded for everyoneEveryone affected
Sharded, 1 worker each1/8 of customers fully down1/8 fully affected
Shuffle sharded, 2 of 8Affected customers still have a healthy workerOnly those sharing both workers are affected — a small fraction
The property scales sharply: with more workers per customer and more workers overall, the chance that any two customers overlap completely becomes very small, so one bad actor's effect is confined to a handful of others rather than a whole shard. It needs the client to retry on its other assignment, and it needs the workers to be genuinely independent — shuffle sharding over workers that all share one database contains nothing at all.
📋 Failure Modes & Effects Analysis for Systems — Thinking It Through Before It Happens Reliability ▶
Concept
The Structured Version of a Conversation Teams Have Badly
Every design review includes some version of "what happens if the database is down?" — asked about whichever component the loudest person is worried about, answered from memory, and not written down. FMEA is the same conversation conducted systematically: enumerate every component, ask how each can fail, work out what the user experiences when it does, and record whether anything would tell you. Borrowed from manufacturing and aerospace, and it transfers cleanly because the failure of imagination it corrects is the same one.
Its value is not the document — it is that the analysis reliably finds failure modes with no detection and no handling, which are the ones that become long outages. Half the findings are usually fixed during the session, because they turn out to be a missing timeout or an alert nobody added.

⚖️ Load Shedding & Graceful Degradation — Choosing What to Drop Before You Are Forced To Reliability ▶
Concept
Accepting Work You Cannot Complete Is Worse Than Refusing It
An overloaded system that keeps accepting requests degrades in the worst possible way: every request queues, every request eventually times out, clients retry, the queue grows, and throughput collapses to near zero while the machine is fully busy. Nobody gets served, and the system is working as hard as it can. Refusing ten percent of requests immediately, so the other ninety are served correctly, is strictly better for users and is the decision the system has to make on its own — because by the time a human is involved it is already in the collapsed state.
The failure mode has a name worth knowing — congestion collapse — and its signature is distinctive: high CPU (Central Processing Unit), high queue depth, near-zero successful throughput, and every latency percentile pinned at the timeout. If a dashboard ever shows that shape, the system has no shedding and is spending all its capacity on work that will be thrown away.
Reference

Shedding is only useful if the system knows what matters, which means priority has to be attached to requests before the overload rather than inferred during it.

Two implementation details matter more than the policy. Reject fast and cheaply — a rejection that costs as much as serving the request sheds nothing. And return a status the client understands as "back off", with a retry hint, because a client that retries a rejection immediately has converted your load shedding into a load multiplier.

🎮 GameDays & Chaos Maturity — From an Annual Exercise to Continuous Verification Reliability ▶
Concept
A GameDay Tests the Humans; a Chaos Experiment Tests the System
The two are constantly conflated and they verify different things. Most organisations need the second one more urgently and run the first, because injecting a fault is a technical task and running an exercise requires getting people in a room. 📈 Near-Misses & Incident Metrics — What MTTR (Mean Time To Repair) Does and Does Not Tell You Reliability ▶
Concept
The Free Lessons Most Organisations Throw Away
A near-miss is an event that could have been an incident and was not — the alert that fired while someone happened to be looking, the deploy caught in canary, the failover that worked because a colleague had fixed something unrelated last week. They carry almost all the information of a real incident and none of the cost, and they are systematically discarded because there is no outage to justify writing anything down.
Industries with severe consequences learned this decades ago and built reporting systems for it, because the same conditions that produce a near-miss produce an accident when one coincidence goes the other way. The software version is cheap: a lightweight channel where "that was close" is a normal thing to say, and a fortnightly ten-minute pass over what people posted.

🧠 Resilience Engineering & Organisational Memory — Why "Human Error" Is a Bad Root Cause Reliability ▶
Concept
"Human Error" Is Where the Investigation Stopped, Not Where the Cause Was
Resilience engineering is a field with its own research literature, and its central finding is uncomfortable for the way most organisations run incident reviews: when an investigation concludes that someone made a mistake, it has stopped at the point where the interesting questions begin. The person's action made sense to them at the time, given what they could see and the pressures they were under. Understanding why it made sense is what produces a fix; concluding that they should have been more careful produces nothing, because the next person will have the same information and the same pressures.
Instead ofAsk
"They ran the command on the wrong host"What made the two hosts hard to tell apart at that moment?

"They should have escalated sooner"What signal would have told them it was time? Did it exist?
"They deployed without testing"What did the process make easy, and what did it make slow?

Each right-hand question has an actionable answer and each left-hand statement does not. The blameless-postmortem practice in Writing a Postmortem People Actually Learn From is the cultural half of this; the questions above are the analytical half, and they are what makes blamelessness produce findings rather than merely produce politeness.
Concept
The Work as Imagined and the Work as Done
A second finding from the same field, and the more useful one operationally: there is always a gap between the documented procedure and what people actually do, and the gap is usually why the system works. Operators continuously adapt — checking one more thing, skipping a step that is safe here, knowing which alert to trust. That adaptation absorbs variation the design never anticipated, and it is invisible until somebody enforces the documented process exactly and things get worse.
The practical consequence: when you find people deviating from a procedure, the first question is not how to stop them. It is what the deviation is achieving. Sometimes it is a genuine risk to close; often it is compensating for something the procedure gets wrong, and the correct fix is to change the procedure. Organisations that treat every deviation as non-compliance lose the adaptation and are surprised when reliability falls.
Troubleshooting

Much of an organisation's reliability lives in a handful of people's heads: which alert is noise, why that service has an odd configuration, what happened last time this failed. It is unwritten, it is load-bearing, and it leaves with them — which is why a team can lose two engineers and become measurably less reliable with no change to the system at all.

🔕 Alert Fatigue as a Reliability Problem, and Testing Runbooks Like Code Reliability ▶
Concept
A Noisy Pager Is an Outage You Have Not Had Yet

Troubleshooting
The Failure Mode Is You
In a small organisation the largest operational risk is not a technology; it is that one person holds every credential, every context and every undocumented decision. 🏢 IT for the Very Large — Federation, Delegation & Standardising Across Business Units Operating Context ▶
Concept
Past a Certain Size, Every Technical Problem Is an Agreement Problem
In a large organisation the hard part of standardising endpoint management is not the technology — it is that eleven business units each already have a solution, three of them have regulatory constraints, two are recent acquisitions with their own directories, and one has a chief executive who does not report into the same chain. The engineering is straightforward and takes weeks. The agreement takes quarters, and skipping it produces a standard that exists on paper while everyone continues as before.
The practical consequence for anyone moving from a smaller organisation: your effectiveness stops being a function of how well you build and starts being a function of how well you can get a decision made and made to stick. That is not politics as a distraction from the work — at this scale it is the work.
Reference
Central, Federated or Devolved — Picking Deliberately
Most large estates are accidentally federated: nobody chose the model, it emerged from acquisitions and reorganisations. Choosing explicitly, per capability rather than for the whole organisation, is what makes it workable.
ModelWorks forCost
Central — one team owns it everywhereIdentity, network core, security controls, licensingSlow for edge cases; central team becomes a bottleneck
Federated — central standard, local executionEndpoint management, service desk, application deliveryRequires real enforcement or it decays to devolved
Devolved — the unit decidesBusiness-specific applications, specialist labs, regulated unitsDuplication and inconsistent security posture
The useful rule: centralise what must be consistent to be safe — identity, the security boundary, anything an auditor asks about once for the whole company — and federate or devolve the rest. A central team that tries to own everything becomes the reason business units build shadow IT, and shadow IT is a worse outcome than a slightly inconsistent approved one.
Troubleshooting
Making Delegation Safe, and Standards That Actually Hold
Delegation at scale fails in two directions: too little, and the central team drowns while units route around it; too much, and nobody can say what the security posture is. Both are fixed by delegating scope with guard rails rather than delegating trust.

The last two rows are the same argument in different forms and they are the whole card. A central function that ships a genuinely better paved path and names a counterpart in each unit gets standardisation as a side effect. One that ships policy and reports non-compliance upward gets a compliance theatre in which everyone is technically adherent and nothing has changed — the same dynamic covered at team scale in Platform as a Product — Adoption, the Option Not to Use It & the Anti-Patterns.
🧰 MSP (Managed Service Provider) Operations — Multi-Tenant Tooling, Onboarding & the Support Model Operating Context ▶
Concept
Everything Multiplies by the Number of Tenants
Internal IT solves a problem once. A managed service provider solves it in forty environments, each with different licensing, different legacy, a different idea of what they are paying for, and no ability to see each other's data. Every decision that is merely inconvenient internally — a manual step, a per-client exception, an undocumented fix — becomes a recurring cost multiplied by the client count, and it is the accumulation of those that decides whether an MSP is profitable.
The consequence is that standardisation is not a preference at an MSP; it is the business model. Clients who insist on being different are not merely awkward — they are structurally unprofitable unless priced for it, and pricing for it honestly is a healthier answer than absorbing it.
RequirementWhy it is different from internal IT
Strict tenant isolation in every toolA cross-tenant leak is a business-ending event, not an incident
Documentation that a stranger can act onWhoever picks up the ticket has never seen this client
Standard build per client tierForty bespoke environments cannot be operated by a shared team
Automation that is tenant-aware by constructionA script that runs against the wrong tenant is the nightmare scenario
Time tracking against contractUnbilled work is invisible margin loss
Onboarding and offboarding runbooks for clientsClient churn is normal; both ends must be repeatable
Tenant-aware by construction is the row that has to be designed rather than remembered. A script that takes a tenant as a parameter will one day be run with the wrong one, at speed, by someone tired — so the safeguard has to be structural: separate credentials per tenant, a confirmation that echoes the client name, a dry run by default. Everything else on this list is discipline that degrades gracefully; that one fails all at once and in public.
Reference
Onboarding a Client — The Two Weeks That Decide the Relationship
Client onboarding done badly produces years of exceptions, unbilled work and disputes about what was agreed. It is the same discovery discipline an independent consultant needs, with the added requirement that the result must be operable by people who were not there.
StepDetail
Inventory everything before promising anythingDevices, accounts, servers, vendors, subscriptions, the thing in the cupboard

Agree what is in scope, in writing, with exclusionsThe "we assumed you covered that" conversation, prevented

The declined-control record matters more than it looks. MSPs are routinely blamed for incidents arising from controls the client refused to fund, and a dated, signed line saying so is the difference between a difficult conversation and a legal one.
Troubleshooting
The MSP-Specific Risks Worth Naming
A provider holds privileged access to many organisations at once, which makes it a high-value target in a way its own clients frequently do not appreciate — and which has been exploited repeatedly through exactly the tooling that makes the business work.
RiskControl
Remote management tooling as a distribution channelTreat the RMM platform as tier zero; MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication), restricted access, alerting on script execution

The first row is the one that has repeatedly caused mass incidents: the remote-management platform exists to push software to every endpoint at every client, so an attacker who reaches it has that capability too. It deserves the protection an organisation would give a domain controller, and it usually receives the protection given to an ordinary SaaS (Software as a Service) tool.
🌍 Remote & Distributed Workforces — Provisioning and Support Without an Office Operating Context ▶
Concept
Every Assumption That Depended on the Building Has to Be Replaced
A great deal of traditional IT practice quietly assumed the office: a device could be imaged on the network, a new starter collected their laptop from a desk, someone could physically look at a broken machine, the perimeter meant something, and a person on the corporate LAN (Local Area Network) was probably an employee. Remove the building and each of those becomes a design problem — and organisations that never revisited them are running controls whose assumptions no longer hold while believing they still do.
Assumed the officeReplacement

The network-as-trust-signal row is the consequential one and the most commonly left half-finished: VPN (Virtual Private Network) retained as though it were still a boundary, while the actual controls that matter are identity and device posture. The full argument is in Zero Trust — Never Trust, Always Verify.
Reference
The Support Model That Works Without a Desk
Remote support is not deskside support delivered over a video call. The constraints are different, and the failure mode — a user who cannot reach IT because the thing that is broken is how they reach IT — has to be designed for explicitly.

Attackers call service desks and ask for MFA resets, and they are convincing; a remote-only organisation has removed the informal recognition that used to backstop that. The procedure needs to be written, mandatory, and immune to being waived because a caller sounds senior and impatient — which is precisely the pressure it will be tested with.

🔀 Mergers, Acquisitions & Divestitures — The IT Workstream Nobody Staffs Properly Operating Context ▶
Concept
IT Finds Out Late and Is Expected to Deliver on Day One
Deals are negotiated in confidence, so IT is usually told shortly before or shortly after announcement — and is then asked how quickly everyone can share a directory, a mailbox domain and a set of applications. The honest answers are longer than anyone wants, and the pressure to promise a shorter one produces the classic outcome: a rushed trust relationship between two directories of unknown security posture, which is one of the most reliable ways to compromise both organisations at once.
The single most useful contribution IT can make early is a realistic sequence with the dependencies named. "Shared calendars in six weeks, single sign-on in six months, one directory in eighteen — and here is what each one requires" is far more valuable than an optimistic date, because the business can plan around a sequence and cannot plan around a date that slips repeatedly.
Reference
The Phases, and What Each One Actually Needs
Integration work has a natural order, and the errors come from attempting later phases before earlier ones are done — most often connecting directories before anyone has assessed what is on the other side.
PhaseWorkCommon error
Due diligenceAssess estate, contracts, licences, security posture, tech debtSkipped, or done without IT — the debt is then a post-close surprise

InteroperateFederation or guest access, shared collaboration, directory syncFull trust between directories before assessing the other side
RationaliseApplication overlap, licence consolidation, standardsDeciding on cost alone; the cheaper tool may be the unusable one
IntegrateOne identity, one endpoint estate, one service deskUnderestimating by a factor of two, reliably

Security due diligence deserves its own mention because it is where the risk concentrates. You are acquiring the other organisation's unpatched estate, its historical breaches — known and unknown — and its credential hygiene. Assume a compromise you have not found yet, and sequence the connection accordingly: assess, then interoperate narrowly, then integrate.
Troubleshooting
Divestiture — Separation Is Harder Than Integration
Selling or spinning out a business unit is treated as the easy inverse of an acquisition and is consistently harder. Integration adds; separation must untangle systems that were deliberately built to be shared, under a deadline set by a contract, with a transitional service agreement that turns every missed date into a penalty.

Two things make a divestiture survivable: get IT into the deal timeline early enough to influence the transitional service agreement — because the dates in it become your obligations — and treat the separation as its own funded project with a named owner. Run as a side task by the same team also doing their day job, it will overrun, and the overrun has a contractual price.
🌱 Green IT — Power, Cooling, Hardware Lifespan & the Honest Carbon Maths Operating Context ▶
Concept
Manufacturing Usually Outweighs Running — Which Inverts the Obvious Advice
The intuitive sustainability move in IT is to reduce power consumption, and for a datacenter that is right. For end-user devices it is mostly wrong: for a typical laptop, the emissions from manufacturing exceed the emissions from several years of use. That single fact reverses the usual advice — the most effective thing most organisations can do is keep laptops longer, and a three-year refresh cycle replaced by a four- or five-year one does more than any efficiency setting available.

♿ Accessible IT — Assistive Technology, Procurement & Testing Your Own Tools Operating Context ▶
Concept
Accessibility Is Usually Discussed as a Website Problem and Lands as an Internal One
Web accessibility gets attention because it is customer-facing and legally visible. The internal side gets almost none, and it is where an IT function's decisions have the most direct effect on colleagues: the expenses system that cannot be used with a keyboard alone, the ticketing tool that a screen reader reads as a list of unlabelled buttons, the conferencing platform without live captions, the security tool that requires distinguishing red from green. Every one of those was procured by IT, and each one makes someone's job harder or impossible.
The framing that changes decisions: accessibility needs are not exceptional and not always permanent. They include colleagues with a broken arm, a migraine, a noisy environment, an ageing eye prescription, a temporary loss of a hand to a bandage, and anyone using a phone in bright sunlight. Designing and buying for the widest range benefits far more people than the population it is nominally for.
Reference

Most of the accessibility of an employee's working day is decided by IT choices rather than by anything a person can configure afterwards.

👁️ Surveillance vs Monitoring — Where Legitimate Telemetry Becomes Something Else Operating Context ▶
Concept
The Same Data, Collected for a Different Purpose, Is a Different Thing
IT collects a great deal about what people do: sign-ins, devices, applications launched, files opened, sites visited, messages sent. Almost all of it is collected for defensible reasons — detecting compromise, troubleshooting, licence management, meeting a regulatory obligation. The line between monitoring and surveillance is not drawn by the data or the tooling. It is drawn by purpose, and it is crossed when telemetry gathered to protect the organisation starts being used to assess individuals.
MonitoringSurveillance

The right-hand column is rarely anyone's intention. It is where a monitoring programme arrives by drift: a capability exists, a manager asks a reasonable-sounding question, somebody answers it because they can, and a precedent is set that nobody decided.
Reference
