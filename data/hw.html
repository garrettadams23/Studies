⚡ Voltage, Current & Resistance — Ohm's Law With Worked IT Examples Electronics ▶
Concept
Three Quantities, One Relationship, and a Water Analogy That Mostly Works
Voltage is electrical pressure — the push. Current is flow — how much actually moves. Resistance opposes that flow. The water analogy holds well enough to reason with: voltage is the pressure in the pipe, current is litres per second, resistance is how narrow the pipe is. Ohm's law ties them together, and almost every practical electrical question in IT is one rearrangement of it.
QuantitySymbolUnitAnalogy
VoltageVVoltsWater pressure
CurrentIAmpsFlow rate
ResistanceROhms (Ω)Pipe narrowness
PowerPWattsWork being done — pressure × flow
 
V = I × R        I = V ÷ R        R = V ÷ I
P = V × I        P = I² × R       P = V² ÷ R
Where the analogy breaks: current does not "get used up" flowing through a device — the same current returns through the circuit. What is consumed is energy, which is the power line. Holding that distinction prevents most of the confusion beginners have about why a device "draws" current rather than being given it.
Reference
Worked Examples From Actual IT Situations
These are the calculations that come up in a datacenter, a comms room or a desk build, and each is a single application of the formulas above. 
# How many servers fit on a 16 A circuit at 230 V?
P_max  = 230 V × 16 A            = 3,680 W
usable = 3,680 × 0.8             = 2,944 W   # 80% continuous-load rule
servers = 2,944 ÷ 450 W each     ≈ 6

# PoE: is the switch budget enough?
24 cameras × 15.4 W (802.3af)    = 370 W     # at the powered device
allow cable loss, budget at PSE  ≈ 400 W+    # switch must supply more than PDs draw

# UPS runtime, roughly
load 800 W, UPS 1500 VA @ 0.6 PF = 900 W capacity  # VA is not watts
runtime falls non-linearly with load — read the vendor curve, do not divide
The VA-versus-watts trap in that last block catches people constantly. Uninterruptible supplies are rated in volt-amps, which equals watts only for a purely resistive load; computer power supplies are not one. Multiply VA by the stated power factor — commonly 0.6 to 0.9 — before comparing it to your load.
Troubleshooting
Voltage Drop — The Cause That Looks Like Something Else
Every conductor has resistance, so every conductor loses some voltage along its length. Over short runs this is irrelevant. Over long ones it produces symptoms that look like a faulty device: a camera at the end of a long cable run that reboots under load, a remote sensor that works on the bench and not in place, an access point that powers up and drops when its radios come on.
SymptomCheck
Device works near the switch, fails at distanceVoltage drop over the run — measure at the far end, under load
Reboots only when the device works hardMarginal supply; current rises, voltage sags further
Works on a bench supply, not in situThe installation's wiring, not the device
Intermittent, worse in warm weatherResistance rises with temperature; a marginal run becomes a failing one
Fine on one port, not anotherPer-port PoE (Power over Ethernet) budget or class negotiation, not the cable
The general lesson generalises past electronics: measure at the point of use, under the conditions that fail. A voltage reading taken at the supply with the device idle is the electrical equivalent of testing an application with no load, and it will look fine every time.
🌡️ Power & Thermals — Watts, Heat, and Why the PSU (Power Supply Unit) Calculation Matters Electronics ▶
Concept
Essentially All the Power a Computer Draws Becomes Heat
A computer does no mechanical work worth mentioning and emits almost no light. Nearly one hundred percent of the electrical energy it consumes leaves as heat. This is why the power figure and the cooling figure are the same number, and why "how many watts does this rack draw" and "how much heat must this room remove" are one question asked twice — a fact that surprises people the first time a comms cupboard becomes unusable in summer.
Rule of thumbUse
1 W consumed ≈ 1 W of heat to removeCooling sizing follows directly from the power draw
Cooling itself consumes powerTotal facility draw exceeds IT draw — this is what PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) measures
Every 10 °C rise roughly halves electrolytic capacitor lifeWhy hot equipment fails early rather than immediately
Silicon throttles rather than failsA hot machine is slow before it is broken — check thermals on performance complaints
Airflow direction is front-to-back, consistentlyOne reversed device recirculates hot air into its neighbours
The throttling row is the one that turns a thermal problem into a mysterious performance ticket. A laptop with a blocked vent or dried-out paste does not crash — it quietly runs at a fraction of its rated speed, and the user reports that "it got slow", which sends everybody looking at software.
Reference
Sizing a Power Supply Without Guessing
The common approaches are to buy the biggest affordable unit or to trust the number on the graphics card box. Neither answers the question, and the actual calculation takes two minutes.

GPU (board power, not "recommended PSU") ~250 W
Motherboard, RAM, fans, drives           ~80 W

target = total ÷ 0.6   ≈ 760 W   # run near 50–60% for best efficiency

ConsiderationWhy
Efficiency peaks near half loadAn oversized unit at 15% load is less efficient, not more
Modern GPUs (Graphics Processing Unit) draw brief spikes far above their ratingA unit sized to the average trips its protection on transients
Efficiency ratings describe efficiency, not qualityAn 80 Plus badge says nothing about ripple, protection or capacitor grade
Single-rail vs multi-railMulti-rail limits per-rail current; relevant only for high-draw builds
Capacitors ageA supply comfortable at five years old may not be at ten
Size for the transient peak, not the average, and buy a unit whose review measured ripple. Modern graphics cards draw brief spikes far above their rated draw, and a supply chosen to sit near its efficiency sweet spot on paper will trip its own protection under them — which presents as random reboots that get blamed on everything else for weeks. The efficiency badge says nothing about that behaviour; the review does.
Troubleshooting
The Power Supply Is the Most Under-Suspected Component
A failing supply does not usually stop working — it produces symptoms attributed to almost everything else, because the effects appear wherever the machine happens to be most sensitive. It is the component to suspect when the symptoms are diverse and make no collective sense.
SymptomWhy a PSU can cause it
Random reboots under load, no errorVoltage sags below tolerance when demand rises
Memory errors that move between modulesUnstable rails corrupt reads regardless of which module is fitted
Storage dropping out intermittentlyDrives are sensitive to rail quality
Machine will not start, no lights at allProtection has latched — unplug for a minute to reset
Works with one drive removedMarginal capacity, not a drive fault
Bulging or leaking capacitors, visibleEnd of life; replace the unit, do not nurse it

The diagnostic that settles it quickly is substitution with a known-good supply of adequate capacity — the "swap" step of systematic troubleshooting. Software diagnostics cannot see a rail sagging for microseconds under transient load, which is exactly the failure that produces the most confusing tickets.
🔩 Components & Schematics — Resistors, Capacitors, Diodes, Transistors Electronics ▶
Reference
Five Components Explain Most of What You Will Meet
Board-level electronics uses a small vocabulary repeatedly. Recognising these five and what each is for is enough to read most schematics well enough to reason about a fault, which is the realistic goal for someone in IT rather than in electronics design.
ComponentDoesTypical job on a board
ResistorLimits current, divides voltageSetting a signal level; protecting an LED (Light Emitting Diode); pull-up on a data line
CapacitorStores charge; passes changes, blocks steady voltageSmoothing supply rails; decoupling beside every chip; filtering
DiodeConducts one way onlyReverse-polarity protection; rectifying AC; clamping spikes
TransistorSwitches or amplifiesSwitching a load under logic control; every digital gate, in bulk
InductorResists changes in currentSwitching regulators; filtering noise
Two of these matter disproportionately in failures. Electrolytic capacitors dry out and bulge with age and heat — visible, common, and the classic repairable board fault. And the decoupling capacitors scattered beside every chip exist to supply the instantaneous current a chip demands when it switches; without them a board is unstable in ways that look like software faults.
Concept
Reading a Schematic — Follow Power First, Then Signals
A schematic is not a picture of the board; it is a map of connections, drawn for clarity rather than for physical layout. Two components adjacent on the diagram may be at opposite ends of the board, and a net drawn as three separate stubs labelled 3V3 is one electrical connection.
ConventionMeaning
Power at the top, ground at the bottomSignals generally flow left to right between them
Named nets — 3V3, VBUS, GNDEverywhere the same label appears is one connection
A dot at a crossingConnected. No dot means the lines merely cross
Ground symbols repeated everywhereAll the same net; drawn repeatedly to avoid a web of lines
Reference designators — R12, C4, U3, Q1Resistor, capacitor, integrated circuit, transistor
A bar over a signal name, or a leading /Active low — asserted when pulled to ground
The active-low convention causes a specific and common confusion when probing: a reset or chip-select line sitting at 3.3 V is not asserted, and a line at 0 V is not necessarily broken. Check the naming before concluding a signal is dead.
Troubleshooting
What Is Worth Knowing at Board Level, in an IT Job
Being honest about scope: component-level repair is rarely economic in a professional IT context, and attempting it on a device under warranty voids it. What this knowledge is for is diagnosis — deciding whether a board is repairable, whether a symptom is electrical or logical, and whether the vendor's explanation is plausible.
ObservationReasonable conclusion
Bulging or leaking electrolytic capacitorsAge or heat; the board may be repairable, the cause is environmental
A burnt or cracked component with a scorch markSomething failed catastrophically — check what it powers before replacing
Corrosion around a connector or batteryLiquid or leaked cell; damage extends further than it looks
Board looks perfect and the device is deadMost likely a chip or firmware fault — not visually diagnosable
Smell of burnt electronics, no visible damageReal, and worth taking seriously; power it down
Repeated identical failures across a fleetA design or batch fault; escalate to the vendor rather than repairing
That last row is the one where this knowledge pays for itself in an enterprise. Recognising that eleven identical machines failed the same way at the same age turns "unlucky hardware" into a warranty claim or a recall conversation, and it is a claim vendors take much more seriously when it comes with a component-level observation attached.
📟 Test Gear — Multimeter, Oscilloscope, Logic Analyser: What Each One Answers Electronics ▶
Concept
Each Instrument Answers a Different Question About Time
The three instruments are frequently ranked by cost or sophistication, which is the wrong axis. What separates them is the timescale of the question. A multimeter tells you a value now, averaged. An oscilloscope shows you a voltage changing, so you can see shape, noise and timing. A logic analyser ignores voltage shape entirely and decodes many digital lines into meaning — bytes, addresses, protocol transactions.
InstrumentAnswersCannot tell you
MultimeterIs the voltage right? Is this connected? Is this fuse good?Anything about shape, noise or brief events
OscilloscopeWhat does this signal look like over microseconds?What the data means, across many lines at once
Logic analyserWhat did these eight lines actually say, decoded as I²C or SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface)?Signal quality — it sees ones and zeros, not marginal edges
The practical division: if the question is "is power reaching this", reach for a multimeter. If it is "why is this data corrupted sometimes", an oscilloscope, because the answer is usually in the shape of the edges. If it is "what is this device sending", a logic analyser, which turns a wire into a transcript.
Reference
Multimeter — The Five Measurements Worth Being Fluent In
A multimeter is the one instrument worth owning even if you never intend to do electronics work, because it answers physical-layer questions that no software can.
ModeUseWatch out for
DC (Direct Current) voltageIs the rail present and correct? Measure under loadAn idle reading hides a sag; probe while the fault occurs
Continuity (beep)Is this cable, fuse or trace intact?Only on an unpowered circuit
ResistanceCable length checks, sensor values, is this shorted?In-circuit readings are affected by parallel paths
DC currentWhat is this device actually drawing?The meter goes in series — and this is how meters get destroyed
AC voltageIs mains present? Is this outlet live?Genuine hazard — know what you are doing or do not
Two safety notes worth stating plainly. Current measurement requires breaking the circuit and putting the meter in the path; connecting a meter set to current across a voltage source is a direct short through the meter, and it is the single most common way they are destroyed. And mains work is a category apart — if you are not trained and authorised for it, the correct action is to stop and call someone who is.
Troubleshooting
When the Answer Needs a Scope
Most IT professionals never need an oscilloscope, and the cases where one is the only answer share a signature: the fault is real, intermittent, and invisible to every logical diagnostic because the problem is happening below the layer software can observe.
SituationWhat the scope shows
Serial data corrupts occasionallySlow or rounded edges, reflections, wrong voltage levels
Device resets under loadThe supply rail sagging at exactly the moment of demand
Intermittent bus errorsRinging or crosstalk on the clock line
A sensor reads noisilyInterference riding on the signal, and often its frequency, which names the source
Timing between two events is suspectThe actual interval, which no logging can measure at that resolution
A cheap USB (Universal Serial Bus) logic analyser deserves a specific recommendation for anyone touching embedded or serial devices: for the price of a couple of network cables it decodes I²C, SPI and UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter) directly into readable transactions, and it converts "the device is not responding" from a guess into a transcript showing whether anything was sent, whether it was acknowledged, and at what address.
🧩 Motherboard Anatomy — Chipsets, PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) Lanes & the Block Diagram PC Hardware ▶
Concept
Lanes Are the Budget, and Almost Everything Competes for Them
A modern board is easier to reason about once you stop thinking of it as a collection of sockets and start thinking of it as a distribution network for a fixed number of PCIe lanes. The CPU (Central Processing Unit) provides a set number — some directly to the primary slot and one or two fast storage slots — and everything else hangs off the chipset, which itself connects to the CPU over a single link that all of it shares. That shared link is where surprising bottlenecks come from.
Connected toTypicallyConsequence
CPU, directlyPrimary x16 slot; one or two NVMe (Non-Volatile Memory Express) slotsFull bandwidth, no contention
ChipsetRemaining slots, SATA (Serial Advanced Technology Attachment), USB (Universal Serial Bus), network, audioAll share one uplink to the CPU
Shared or muxedAn NVMe slot that disables two SATA ports when populatedDocumented in the manual, discovered when a drive vanishes
The third row is the practical one and it causes a specific support call: a technician installs an NVMe drive and two SATA drives disappear. Nothing is broken — the lanes are shared, the board reallocated them, and the manual's block diagram said so. Reading that diagram before building is a five-minute step that prevents an afternoon of confusion.
Reference

Enough vocabulary to read a manual, order the right part, and describe a fault precisely to a vendor.

The diagnostic display is the row to pay for. A board that reports where it stopped during power-on turns "it does nothing" into a two- minute diagnosis, and every other feature on this list matters less on the day the machine will not start. The processor power connector is the other one worth memorising, because forgetting it produces exactly the same symptom as a dead board.
Troubleshooting
Form Factor, Firmware and the Mistakes That Cost a Rebuild
Most motherboard problems in practice are not faults — they are compatibility and process mistakes, made once, expensively.
MistakeConsequence
Assuming a socket match means CPU supportThe board needs a firmware update to recognise a newer CPU — and may need an older CPU to apply it
Not checking for flashback or firmware-update-without-CPU supportThe above becomes a chicken-and-egg problem needing a borrowed processor

The firmware-and-CPU-support trap is worth checking before ordering, not after. A board sold as supporting a processor may have shipped before that processor existed, and unless it offers firmware flashing with no CPU installed, the only route is temporarily fitting an older chip — which nobody has to hand at the moment they discover this.
🧊 CPU (Central Processing Unit) & Cooling — Sockets, TDP (Thermal Design Power), Thermal Paste & Throttling Diagnosis PC Hardware ▶
Concept
TDP Is a Cooling Design Target, Not a Power Consumption Figure
Thermal design power is widely read as "how many watts this chip uses". It is not. It is the sustained heat a cooler must be able to remove for the chip to hold its base behaviour, and modern processors routinely exceed it — sometimes by a large multiple — during boost, by design. A processor rated at 65 W can draw well over 200 W briefly, which is why a cooler sized to the rated figure produces a machine that is fast for thirty seconds and then is not.
TermMeans
TDP / base powerSustained heat the cooler must handle at base clocks
Boost / turbo powerShort-duration higher draw — often the number that matters
Thermal throttlingClocks reduced because the die reached its limit
Power throttlingClocks reduced because a power limit was reached, not a thermal one
Thermal velocity boost / opportunistic boostExtra clocks available only while the chip is cool
The distinction between thermal and power throttling matters diagnostically. A machine throttling at 95 °C has a cooling problem. A machine throttling at 70 °C has a power-limit configuration, which is a firmware setting rather than a fault — and is deliberate on many laptops and small-form-factor systems.
Reference
Thermal Paste — Small Job, Disproportionate Consequences
Paste fills microscopic gaps between the processor's heat spreader and the cooler's base; without it, air sits in those gaps and air is an excellent insulator. Almost everything people argue about here matters far less than simply having some, in roughly the right amount, with the cooler correctly seated.
PointReality
How muchA pea or a thin line; pressure spreads it. Excess is messy, not catastrophic
Application patternEndlessly debated, worth a degree or two at most
Paste choiceAny reputable compound is fine; exotic ones buy a few degrees
Reapplication intervalYears — but a laptop running hot at four or five years old is a strong candidate
Reusing old paste after removing a coolerDo not; clean both surfaces and reapply
Mounting pressure and evennessThe thing that actually matters — uneven seating beats any paste choice
Protective film left on the cooler baseHappens more often than anyone admits; check it
Mounting pressure is the row that matters and the one nobody argues about. Pattern, brand and quantity are worth a degree or two between them; a cooler seated unevenly or a protective film left on the base is worth thirty, and both are common. Tighten the mounting screws in a cross pattern, a little at a time, and check the base before you fit it.
Troubleshooting
"It Got Slow" — Diagnosing Thermal Problems
Thermal throttling is one of the most common causes of a gradual, unexplained performance complaint, and it is routinely investigated as a software problem for weeks because nothing in the operating system reports it prominently.
StepWhat you are looking for

Note the temperature at which it dropsNear 95–100 °C is thermal; much lower is a power limit

Dust deserves the emphasis it gets. A laptop's heatsink fins clog into a felt mat that air cannot pass through, and the machine does not report an error — it just becomes slow, and the user assumes it has aged. Compressed air and ten minutes routinely restore a machine that was being considered for replacement, which is also the cheapest sustainability win available.
🧠 Memory Deep — Channels, Ranks, Timings, ECC (Error-Correcting Code) & Diagnosing Bad RAM (Random Access Memory) PC Hardware ▶
Concept
Which Slots You Use Changes Performance Substantially
Memory controllers address several channels in parallel, and populating them correctly roughly doubles available bandwidth compared with putting everything on one. The slots are colour-coded in pairs to indicate this, and the pairs are usually not adjacent — which is exactly why people fill the two slots next to the processor and unknowingly halve their memory bandwidth. On systems where the graphics processor shares system memory, the effect is dramatic and looks like a faulty machine.
ConfigurationResult
One moduleSingle channel — half the bandwidth, and it is very noticeable with integrated graphics
Two modules, matched pair, correct slotsDual channel — the target configuration
Two modules, adjacent wrong slotsSingle channel; no error, just slower
Three modulesMixed; usually part-channel, and rarely what anyone intended
Mismatched sizes or speedsRuns at the slowest common settings; may run single channel
Consult the manual for the correct pair rather than reasoning from the layout — the convention varies. And note that a machine will boot and work perfectly in every wrong configuration above, which is why this is a performance mystery rather than a visible fault.
Reference
The Vocabulary, and What ECC Actually Buys
Memory specifications carry more numbers than any other component, and most of them matter far less than the channel configuration above.
TermMeansHow much it matters

ECC is the row worth understanding rather than memorising. Bit flips happen — from cosmic rays, from marginal voltage, from ageing chips — and on a desktop the result is an occasional crash or a silently corrupted file that nobody attributes to memory. On a server holding a database, silent corruption is far worse than a crash, and ECC turns most single-bit errors into a logged, corrected event. That log is the point: it tells you a module is failing before it takes anything with it.
Troubleshooting

Memory faults produce symptoms with no pattern: crashes in different applications, corruption in unrelated files, a machine that installs an operating system successfully and then fails randomly. Anything that behaves inconsistently and irrationally deserves a memory test early, because the test is free and rules out an entire category.

The XMP row is worth trying early because it is free and it explains a surprising share of "faulty memory": the modules are sold as capable of a speed the board and processor cannot reliably drive with that configuration, so the profile is stable in the vendor's lab and marginal in your machine.
🔌 Storage Interfaces — SATA (Serial Advanced Technology Attachment), NVMe (Non-Volatile Memory Express), PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) Lanes & Where the Bottleneck Really Is PC Hardware ▶
Concept
NVMe Beat SATA on the Protocol, Not Only on the Wire
SATA was designed for spinning disks: one command queue, thirty-two entries deep, with a protocol built around a device that has a head to move. NVMe was designed for flash, which has no moving parts and enormous internal parallelism — thousands of queues, thousands of commands each, and far less overhead per operation. The bandwidth increase gets the attention, and the queue model is what actually transforms behaviour under concurrent load.
SATA IIINVMe (PCIe)
Ceiling~600 MB/sSeveral GB/s, scaling with lanes and generation
Queues1, depth 32Many, deep
Protocol overheadDesigned around mechanical assumptionsMinimal, designed for flash
LatencyHigherSubstantially lower — often the thing users feel
PhysicalCable, or M.2 in SATA modeM.2, U.2 or an add-in card
The M.2 slot is a physical form factor, not a protocol. An M.2 drive may be NVMe or SATA, and a slot may accept one, the other, or both — so an M.2 SATA drive in an M.2 slot delivers SATA speeds and looks, to anyone who bought "an M.2 SSD (Solid State Drive)" expecting otherwise, like a defective product.
Reference
Lanes and Generations — Reading the Real Bandwidth
A drive's ceiling is set by lane count multiplied by per-lane speed, and a slot that is physically x16 may be electrically x4 or x1. Both ends negotiate down to the lower.
PCIe generationPer lane, roughlyx4 slot, roughly
Gen 3~1 GB/s~4 GB/s
Gen 4~2 GB/s~8 GB/s
Gen 5~4 GB/s~16 GB/s
Two practical consequences. A Gen 4 drive in a Gen 3 slot works perfectly and runs at Gen 3 speeds — not a fault, and worth checking before troubleshooting "slow" new hardware. And an M.2 slot fed from the chipset rather than the CPU (Central Processing Unit) shares its uplink with everything else on the chipset, so a large file copy alongside heavy network traffic can contend in ways the drive's specification never suggested.
Troubleshooting

🔬 Systematic Hardware Troubleshooting — Isolate, Swap & the Minimum Viable System Diagnosis ▶
Concept
Change One Thing, and Know What You Expect to Happen
Hardware faults reward discipline more than knowledge, because the search space is small and finite — a desktop has perhaps eight replaceable parts. What wastes the afternoon is changing three things at once, losing track of what was tried, and ending up unable to say whether the machine ever worked with the original memory in the original slot. The method is unglamorous and it is faster than intuition almost every time.
StepDetail
1. Reproduce itKnow the exact conditions; an intermittent fault you cannot trigger cannot be tested
2. Write down the starting stateWhich parts, which slots, which settings — you will need this in an hour
3. Reduce to the minimum viable systemBoard, CPU (Central Processing Unit), cooler, one memory module, power. Nothing else
4. Confirm the minimum works, or does notThis single step splits the problem in half
5. Add one component; retestOne. The discipline is the whole method
6. Swap with known-good, not with another suspectTwo unknown parts prove nothing about either
7. Record the result of every stepThe notes are what stop you repeating the third attempt twice
Step 6 is where most home diagnosis goes wrong. Swapping a suspect part into a second suspect machine produces a result nobody can interpret. Known-good means a part you have just seen working, in a machine you have just seen working.
Reference
Isolate by Half, and Start With What Is Free
Before removing anything, several checks cost nothing and resolve a surprising share of faults. Technicians skip them because they feel too simple, and then find them at the end.
CheckCatches
Is it plugged in, switched on at the wall, and is the socket live?More often than anyone admits, especially remotely
Reseat everything — memory, cards, cables, both endsPartial seating after transport; the single highest-yield step
Try a different cable, a different port, a different monitorDisplay faults that are not the computer
Clear CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor)A bad setting that prevents boot; costs a minute

"What changed?" is the most productive single question in all troubleshooting, and it is worth asking about the environment as well as the machine. A workstation that started failing after being moved is a reseating problem; one that started after a power cut is a supply problem until proven otherwise.

The data row overrides all the others. If the drive holds anything not backed up, powering the machine on repeatedly to chase a fault can turn a recoverable situation into an unrecoverable one — see the data-recovery triage card. Preserve first, diagnose second.
🚦 POST, Beep Codes & Diagnostic LEDs (Light Emitting Diode) — Reading a Machine That Will Not Boot Diagnosis ▶
Concept
A Machine With No Display Is Still Telling You Things
Power-on self test runs before anything you can see. The firmware checks the processor, initialises memory, brings up the chipset, finds a display adapter, then finally puts an image on screen — and if it fails before that last step there is nothing to read. The machine has not gone silent; it is reporting through the only channels it has left, which are beeps, LEDs and a two-digit code display. Knowing that these exist is what turns "it is dead" into a specific fault.

Reference

Troubleshooting
The Order That Resolves Most No-Boot Machines
Working this sequence takes about fifteen minutes and resolves the large majority of no-display faults without any parts.
OrderAction
1Confirm mains power and that the supply's own switch is on
2Look for diagnostic LEDs or a POST code; note it before changing anything
3Reseat memory, graphics card and both ends of every power cable
4Confirm the CPU power connector is fitted — the most-missed item in a new build
5Clear CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor); if it posts, a setting was the fault and you now know it
6Strip to minimum viable system and retest
7One memory module, in the slot the manual names for a single module
8Bypass the case: test the board on the bench, in case a standoff is shorting it
9Substitute a known-good power supply
Step 8 is the one people skip and it catches a genuine class of new-build fault: a mounting standoff under a part of the board with no hole shorts it against the case, and the machine is completely dead in the case and boots perfectly on the bench. If a newly-built machine will not post, testing it outside the case is worth the ten minutes.
🎲 Intermittent Faults — Heat, Vibration, Marginal Power & How to Reproduce Them Diagnosis ▶
Concept
Intermittent Means Conditional, and the Condition Is Findable
Faults described as random almost never are. They are conditional on something nobody has identified yet — temperature, load, elapsed time since power-on, physical position, humidity, what else is running on the same circuit. Reframing "it happens randomly" as "there is a condition I have not found" changes the work from waiting for it to happen into looking for what is different when it does.
PatternSuspect
Fails after twenty minutes, fine after a restThermal — something is heating past a threshold
Fails only under heavy loadPower delivery, or thermal, or both
Fails in the morning, fine laterCold — solder joints, or a marginal capacitor warming up
Fails when moved or bumpedMechanical — a connector, a socket, a cracked joint

The cold-morning row is genuinely counter-intuitive and worth knowing: some faults appear when cold and vanish when warm, because a marginal solder joint or a component's tolerances shift with temperature in the opposite direction to the usual assumption. "It only fails first thing" is a real diagnostic signal, not a user's imagination.
Reference

You cannot diagnose what you cannot reproduce, so the first work is making the fault happen on demand. Each technique deliberately drives one variable to its limit.

A script recording temperatures, fan speeds, voltages and system errors every ten seconds turns "it crashed again overnight" into a timestamped picture of what the machine was doing in the sixty seconds before it went.
Troubleshooting

And when a machine has been fully rebuilt and still fails, swap the whole machine to a different location before ordering another part, because that single test distinguishes the box from the room.
🪛 Soldering & Rework — Through-Hole, SMD Basics & Knowing When Not To Diagnosis ▶
Concept
Heat the Joint, Not the Solder
The single technique that separates a reliable joint from a bad one: bring the iron to the pad and the component lead together, let both come up to temperature, then feed solder into the joint so it melts on the work rather than on the iron. Solder melted on the iron and dabbed on cools into a dull, weak connection sitting on top of the pad — a cold joint, which may test as connected and fail later under vibration or thermal cycling.
Good jointCold joint
Shiny, smooth, concave filletDull, grainy, blobby, convex
Wets both pad and leadSits on one, balled up on the other
Mechanically solidMoves or cracks under gentle pressure
Made in two or three secondsMade slowly, or with repeated reheating
Flux is the other half and is consistently under-used by beginners. It cleans the oxide that stops solder wetting a surface, and most difficulty soldering — solder that will not stick, joints that need excessive heat — is a flux problem rather than a technique or temperature problem.
Reference
What Is Realistically Repairable, and With What
The gap between through-hole and surface-mount work is large, and being honest about which you are equipped for prevents turning a repairable board into a scrap one.

Larger surface-mount — resistors, capacitors, small chipsFine tip, tweezers, magnification, good fluxPractice required, achievable

Ground planes are the specific difficulty in through-hole rework on modern boards: a pin connected to a large copper plane conducts heat away faster than a small iron can supply it, so the joint never melts properly. The answer is a higher-power iron and a bigger tip rather than more time — prolonged heating is what lifts pads and ends repairs.
Troubleshooting

In an IT context, the decision not to solder is correct far more often than the decision to. These are the cases where attempting it makes things worse.

On a device holding the only copy of important dataRecover the data first, always

Three safety points are worth stating without hedging. Ventilate — flux fumes are an irritant and should not be breathed. Wear eye protection, because solder spits. And treat power supplies and CRT-era displays as dangerous while unplugged: their capacitors hold a charge that can injure you long after the mains is disconnected, and discharging them safely is a specific procedure rather than a matter of care.
🆘 Data Recovery Triage — When to Stop and Send It to a Lab Diagnosis ▶
Concept
The First Decision Is Whether to Power It On Again
Every additional power-on of a physically failing drive risks converting a recoverable situation into an unrecoverable one. A head that is contacting the platter damages the surface a little more each rotation; a drive with a failing motor may not spin again. The instinct — try it once more, maybe it will work this time — is precisely the wrong one, and it is the reason many lab cases are more expensive than they needed to be.
SymptomAction
Clicking, grinding, beeping, or a repeated spin-up-and-stopStop. Power off and do not power on again
Drive not detected at all, no soundStop; could be electronics, which a lab can address
Was dropped, or exposed to liquid or fireStop; do not attempt to dry it or run it
Detected, readable, some files errorImage it now, before anything else
Accidental deletion or a formatted volumeStop writing to it; recovery software on an image is reasonable
Encrypted volume without the keyNo lab can help; this is a key-management problem
RansomwareAn incident, not a recovery job — preserve and follow the IR (Incident Response) process
The single most important distinction is logical versus physical. A logical failure — deletion, corruption, a lost partition — leaves the hardware healthy and is often recoverable with software. A physical failure needs a cleanroom and specialist equipment, and every DIY attempt reduces what a lab can retrieve. The noises in the first row are the tell, and they mean stop.
Reference
Image First, Work on the Copy
When the drive is still readable, the correct first action is always to take a sector-by-sector image and then do all further work on that image. Recovery attempts on the original consume the limited number of reads a dying drive has left.
PracticeReason

Verify the image before touching the original againAn incomplete image discovered later is a wasted opportunity

One SSD-specific caution: flash drives can fail into a read-only state or fail completely with no warning at all, and their controllers may aggressively remap or discard data. There is far less "partial failure" runway than with a spinning disk, so the window to image is shorter and the case for imaging immediately is stronger.
Troubleshooting

Step 1 is the professional judgement that distinguishes room support from room repair. The meeting is the thing that matters; the room is not. 🤖 Microcontrollers vs Single-Board Computers — Choosing Correctly Embedded ▶
Concept
One Runs Your Code; the Other Runs an Operating System That Runs Your Code
A microcontroller executes your program directly on bare metal: it starts in milliseconds, does exactly one thing, and does it with predictable timing because nothing else is competing. A single-board computer boots Linux, and your program is one process among many — which buys networking, a filesystem, package management and every library in the ecosystem, at the cost of a boot time measured in tens of seconds and timing that is approximate. Choosing wrongly is the most common mistake in a first embedded project, in both directions.

Choose by the timing and power rows; everything else follows from them. If the job needs deterministic timing or battery life measured in months, no amount of convenience makes a Linux board correct — and if it needs networking, storage and video, no amount of determinism makes a microcontroller pleasant. The loss-of-power row is the one that catches hobby projects: an unattended board on a memory card wants a graceful shutdown or a filesystem that tolerates being cut off.

💽 Firmware Basics — Bootloaders, Flashing, JTAG/SWD, Bricking & Recovery Embedded ▶
Concept
The Bootloader Is a Small Program Whose Job Is Replacing the Big One
A microcontroller running your program cannot easily replace that program, because it is busy running it. The bootloader solves this: a small piece of code that runs first, decides whether to accept a new application over serial or USB (Universal Serial Bus), and otherwise hands control to the existing one. Almost every convenience in embedded development — plugging in a board and uploading code without special hardware — is the bootloader doing its job.

The distinction that matters: the first three methods depend on software already running on the device, so a bad flash can remove them. SWD and JTAG talk to the silicon, which is why they recover devices that have no working software at all. A device with accessible debug pins is very hard to permanently brick; one without them is one bad update away from being scrap.

⏱️ Real-Time Constraints — RTOS, Determinism & Why Linux Is Not Always Right Embedded ▶
Concept
Real-Time Means Predictable, Not Fast
This is the definition people get wrong, and getting it right changes how the whole problem looks. A real-time system is one where being late is a failure — not where things happen quickly. A system that responds in a guaranteed two milliseconds every single time is real-time; one that usually responds in fifty microseconds and occasionally takes thirty milliseconds is not, and is unusable for the jobs that need the guarantee. What matters is the worst case, and general-purpose operating systems optimise the average.
ClassMissing a deadline meansExample
Hard real-timeSystem failure, possibly dangerousMotor control, airbag, industrial safety interlock
Firm real-timeThe result is worthless, though nothing breaksA missed frame in a control loop
Soft real-timeQuality degradesVideo playback, audio, user interfaces
Not real-timeIt finishes when it finishesAlmost all ordinary computing
The practical consequence for anyone specifying a system: ask "what happens if this is late?" before choosing a platform. If the honest answer is "nothing much", Linux on an SBC (Single-Board Computer) is fine and far easier to work with. If the answer involves damage or danger, that decision has been made for you.
Reference
Where the Jitter Comes From
A general-purpose operating system introduces variability at several layers, all of them deliberate and all of them helpful for throughput. Each is a reason the worst case is far from the average.
SourceEffect
Scheduler preemptionYour process is descheduled at an arbitrary moment
Interrupt handlingAnother device's interrupt runs before your code
Virtual memory and page faultsA memory access takes a millisecond instead of nanoseconds

Power management and frequency scalingThe processor is slower than you measured, sometimes
Filesystem and I/O (Input/Output)Buffering, flushes, and a device that decides to be busy
Linux can be pushed a long way toward determinism — the real-time preemption work is mainline now, and with careful tuning (CPU isolation, locked memory, priorities, disabled frequency scaling) it achieves latencies good enough for a great deal of industrial work. It is genuine engineering rather than a checkbox, and the result is bounded latency in the tens of microseconds rather than the guaranteed microseconds a microcontroller gives free.
Troubleshooting
RTOS, Bare Metal, or Linux — and What an RTOS Actually Gives You
A real-time operating system sits between bare metal and Linux: it provides tasks, priorities, timers and synchronisation with a scheduler whose behaviour is predictable and documented, in a few tens of kilobytes.

Whichever you choose, the discipline that matters is measuring the worst case rather than the average. Log the maximum latency observed over hours or days of realistic operation, not the mean over a minute — because the mean will look excellent right up until the one occasion that matters, and in a hard real-time system that single occasion is the entire specification.

Concept
Digital Signals Are Analogue Signals We Agree to Round
There is no such thing as a digital wire. There is a voltage that rises and falls, and a receiver that calls anything above one threshold a 1 and anything below another a 0. The digital abstraction holds while the edges are clean and the levels are clear, and it stops holding the moment noise, cable length or a bad ground pushes the voltage into the gap between the thresholds. Almost every mysterious intermittent fault in wired equipment lives in that gap.

Log the temperature and clock speed under a sustained load rather than reading the fan. A processor holding a high clock at 95°C is behaving normally; one dropping to a third of its rated speed after ninety seconds has a cooling fault, and the difference is invisible from the outside.
Concept
The Repair-or-Replace Conversation, With Numbers
This decision gets made badly in both directions — expensive repairs to machines that owed nothing, and replacements for faults that cost twenty units of currency to fix. A short list of questions settles nearly all of them.

A machine that is slow because it has a mechanical drive and too little memory is not old — it is under-specified, and two cheap parts buy two more years. That is also the repair most likely to be skipped, because "slow" sounds like age rather than like a component.
📦 Warranty, RMA (Return Merchandise Authorization) & the Refurbished Question HW • Commercial ▶
The Inversion
A Warranty Is About Downtime, Not About Free Parts
People compare warranties on price and length and miss the variable that actually matters when a device fails: how long you are without it. A standard warranty where you ship the part away and wait is a very different product from advance replacement, where the new part arrives before you return the old — and for anything a business depends on, that turnaround, not the coverage, is what you are buying.
Warranty typeDowntimeWorth it when
Return-to-baseDays to weeks — you ship, they fix, they returnSpare-in-stock, non-critical kit
Advance replacementNext business day — part arrives firstA device with no hot spare
On-site / next-business-day engineerHours — they come to youServers, and anything you cannot swap yourself
Read the warranty as a recovery-time number and match it to how long you can actually be without the thing. Paying for a four-hour on-site response on a spare laptop is waste; running a production server on return-to-base is a gamble you will lose on the worst possible day.
The Decision
Refurbished Is a Good Decision When You Know Why the Price Dropped
Refurbished hardware is treated as either obviously smart or obviously risky, and neither reflex is right — the answer is entirely about provenance and warranty. Manufacturer-refurbished kit with a real warranty is often the best value in the catalogue; an anonymous “grade B” unit from a marketplace with a 30-day return is a gamble with no support behind it. The price is not the signal; the warranty and the source are.
Buy refurbished when it is manufacturer-certified and carries a genuine warranty, and treat the savings as real; buy it from an unknown source with no support and you have bought someone else's problem at a discount. The RMA path matters most for the parts you cannot afford to be without — which is exactly where the cheap no-warranty option looks tempting and costs the most.
🧰 Labels, Cables & the Toolkit for the Bench HW • Practice ▶
The Inversion
The Consumables Decide Whether the Next Person Can Work on It
The bench glamour is in the diagnosis; the thing that actually saves time is the boring discipline around it — a labelled cable, a photographed teardown, a screw tray that kept the M2s separate from the M3s. An hour of a later repair is decided by whether the last person labelled the cables and bagged the screws, and that person is often you, six months on, remembering nothing.

Velcro over zip ties is the small tell of someone who has done this before: a zip tie is cut and rebought every change, and eventually somebody's snips nick the cable underneath. The consumables are cheap; the time and the mistakes they prevent are not.
The Decision
Handling Discipline and Bench Discipline Are the Same Habit
This is the counterpart to what actually kills components on the bench: the same person who wrist-straps against static also labels, photographs and bags, because both are the habit of paying a small predictable cost now to avoid a large unpredictable one later. A bench without the consumables is not faster; it just moves the cost to reassembly.
Stock the boring things — labels, velcro, trays, a mat, a decent driver set — and use them every time, not just on the big jobs.
