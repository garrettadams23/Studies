🧒Threats Explained SimplySec+ • Start Here›
BIG IDEA
Malware = Digital Germs
Malware is any software built to harm or steal. Like germs, some spreads on its own, some needs you to 'touch' it (open a file), and good hygiene (updates, caution, antivirus) prevents most infections.
FIELD GUIDE
Virus vs. Worm vs. Trojan
A virus attaches to a file and spreads when you run it. A worm copies itself across the network with no help. A trojan pretends to be something good (a free game) but smuggles in something bad — named after the wooden horse.
ANALOGY
Vulnerability → Exploit → Threat
The unlocked window is the vulnerability. Climbing through it is the exploit. The burglar who does it is the threat actor. Fixing the lock (patching) removes the opportunity.
HUMAN HACKING
Social Engineering
The easiest way past a strong lock is to trick someone into opening it. Social engineering hacks people, not machines — impersonation, fake urgency, flattery, or pretending to be IT support. Skepticism is a control.
MODERN THREAT
Ransomware
Ransomware locks your files with encryption and demands payment for the key. The best defense isn't paying — it's good, tested backups kept offline, so you can restore and walk away.

MITRE ATT&CK
A Library of Real Attacker Techniques
MITRE ATT&CK is a free, publicly maintained matrix of Tactics (why), Techniques (how), and Sub-techniques (specific implementation) that real adversaries actually use. It's based on observed incidents, not theory.
Tactics: Initial Access → Execution → Persistence → Privilege Escalation → Defense Evasion → Credential Access → Discovery → Lateral Movement → Collection → Exfiltration → Impact
Use it to: map what an attacker could do, identify detection gaps, communicate about threats in a standard language.
INDICATORS
IOC vs IOA vs TTP
IOC (Indicator of Compromise) — forensic evidence that an attack occurred: malicious IP, hash of known malware, C2 domain. Reactive — tells you it already happened.
IOA (Indicator of Attack) — behavior suggesting an attack is in progress: port scan, privilege escalation, unusual process. Proactive — catch it as it happens.
TTP (Tactics, Techniques, Procedures) — the attacker's playbook. Hardest to change. Even if they change their malware hash, TTPs stay constant. Best for long-term defense.
COMMON MALWARE TYPES
Virus · Worm · Trojan · Ransomware · Rootkit · Spyware
Virus — attaches to legitimate files; needs user action to spread.
Worm — self-replicating; spreads over networks without human action (WannaCry, Slammer).
Trojan — looks legitimate; hides malicious payload (fake crack, infected installer).
Ransomware — encrypts your files; demands payment for the key.
Rootkit — hides malware from the OS itself; persistence at the kernel level.
Spyware/Keylogger — silently records keystrokes, screenshots, credentials.
Botnet agent — your machine becomes a "zombie" that attackers remotely control.
🎭 Social Engineering — People Are the Attack Surface THREAT • Practical ›
SIX INFLUENCE PRINCIPLES
Cialdini's Weapons Attackers Exploit
Reciprocity — do something for someone, they feel obligated to return the favor.
Commitment & Consistency — once someone agrees to a small ask, they're likely to agree to bigger asks.
Social Proof — "Everyone on your team already approved this."
Authority — "This is the CTO" / "Microsoft support calling."
Liking — you comply more with people you like or who seem like you.
Scarcity/Urgency — "This needs to happen in the next 30 minutes or the system goes down."
PRETEXTING
Creating a False Identity or Scenario
The attacker creates a believable story — "I'm from IT, we're doing an audit," or "I'm a new vendor, I need temporary access." They do research first (LinkedIn, OSINT) to make it convincing. The pretext gives the victim a reason to comply that doesn't trigger suspicion.
DEFENSE
Out-of-Band Verification Is the Key
When someone asks for access, credentials, or data:
1. Slow down — urgency is a manipulation tool. Real emergencies have procedures.
2. Verify independently — don't call the number they gave you; look it up yourself.
3. Follow the process — the process exists exactly for these moments.
4. It's okay to say no — "Let me verify this request through proper channels first." A real insider won't mind.
🩹 Vulnerability Management — Finding and Fixing Weaknesses THREAT • Foundation ›
VULNERABILITY LIFECYCLE
Identify → Assess → Prioritize → Remediate → Verify
Vulnerability management is a continuous cycle, not a one-time scan. Networks change daily — new systems appear, software updates fail, configurations drift. The cycle never ends.
CVE & CVSS
Common Vulnerabilities and Their Severity
CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) — a unique identifier for each publicly known vulnerability. Format: CVE-2021-44228 (Log4Shell). The year and a serial number. Lookup: cve.mitre.org or nvd.nist.gov.
CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) — a 0-10 score for severity:
9.0-10.0 = Critical: patch immediately
7.0-8.9 = High: patch this week
4.0-6.9 = Medium: patch this month
0.1-3.9 = Low: scheduled patching
0.0 = None: not a security issue
SCANNING TOOLS
Identify What's Exposed
Nessus (Tenable) — industry standard enterprise vuln scanner. Identifies unpatched software, misconfigurations, weak credentials.
OpenVAS — open-source alternative.
Qualys — cloud-based; no agent required for network scans.
Trivy — open-source container image scanner; finds vulnerable packages in Docker images.
OWASP ZAP — web application scanner; finds XSS, SQLi, CSRF, etc.
PATCH STRATEGY
Risk-Based Prioritization
You'll never patch everything immediately — there are always too many vulnerabilities. Prioritize based on:
1. CVSS score — base severity
2. Exploitability — is it being actively exploited? Check CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.
3. Asset criticality — a critical vuln on an internet-facing production server beats the same vuln on an air-gapped test system.
4. Compensating controls — if a WAF blocks the exploit vector, urgency is lower until you can patch.
ZERO DAY
The Patch Doesn't Exist Yet
A zero-day vulnerability is one that has no patch yet — vendors have had "zero days" to fix it. Zero-days in the wild (actively being exploited) are the most dangerous. Defenses: defense-in-depth (don't rely on patches alone), network segmentation to limit blast radius, EDR to detect exploitation behavior, and monitoring for indicators.
🏹 Threat Hunting — Proactively Finding What Slipped Through THREAT • Advanced ›
WHAT IS THREAT HUNTING
The Difference Between Hunting and Alerting
Reactive security waits for alerts — SIEM detects something, you investigate. Threat hunting flips this: you assume the adversary is already in your network (a reasonable assumption for mature organizations), then proactively search for evidence of compromise. It requires no alert to trigger; you drive the investigation.
ConceptAlert-Based (Reactive)Threat Hunting (Proactive)
TriggerSIEM rule firesHunter's hypothesis
Starting pointAlert tells you where to lookYou decide where to look
OutcomeConfirm or dismiss alertFind what rules missed; improve detection
Skill requiredTriage / analysisDeep knowledge of attacker TTPs
Maturity levelAll orgsMature orgs (assume breach mindset)
THE HUNT PROCESS
Hypothesis-Driven Hunting
Good threat hunting starts with a hypothesis based on threat intelligence, ATT&CK techniques, or recent threat reports — not just "look for bad stuff." A structured hunt has a clear scope and produces either a confirmed finding or improved detection logic.

LIVING OFF THE LAND
LOLBins — Attackers Using Your Own Tools Against You
Modern attackers rarely drop custom malware immediately. Instead they abuse legitimate OS tools that are already present and trusted — harder to detect than malware. These are called LOLBins (Living Off the Land Binaries). Knowing which tools are commonly abused helps focus your hunts.
