🏢 M365 Tenant Anatomy — The Tenant, Its Domains & the Admin Centers M365 • Foundations ▶
Concept
One Tenant, One Directory, Many Front Doors
A Microsoft 365 tenant is not a product — it is a directory with services attached. Underneath every admin center is the same Entra ID directory holding the same users, groups and devices. Exchange, SharePoint, Teams and Intune are tenants' workloads, each with its own console, its own vocabulary and its own idea of what a "group" is. Almost every confusing M365 problem comes from forgetting that: an object changed in one console and the change being read somewhere else entirely.
LayerWhat lives thereChanged from
DirectoryUsers, groups, devices, app registrations, rolesEntra admin center
SubscriptionLicenses, service plans, billingM365 admin center
WorkloadMailboxes, sites, teams, policiesThat workload's own console
ComplianceRetention, labels, eDiscovery, auditPurview portal
When something is not working, identify the layer before opening a console. A license problem, a directory problem and a workload problem look identical from the user's side — "I cannot get into Teams" — and each is fixed in a different place by a different team. The layer column is the triage question, and asking it first saves the hour normally spent clicking between admin centers.
Reference

Portal addresses move around, so the useful thing to memorize is not the URL (Uniform Resource Locator) but which console owns which object.

And they are changing now. Microsoft is moving these consoles onto the cloud.microsoft domain, one service at a time — the Microsoft 365 admin center now lives at admin.cloud.microsoft and the old address redirects there, the Exchange center also answers at admin.cloud.microsoft/exchange, and Teams answers at both teams.microsoft.com and teams.cloud.microsoft. Microsoft's own advice is to allow *.cloud.microsoft as a whole rather than host by host, and to leave its TLS (Transport Layer Security) undecrypted. The operational half does not wait for the rename: anything that filters by domain — a proxy, a secure web gateway, a Conditional Access named location, a firewall rule — needs cloud.microsoft allowed before the redirect reaches your tenant. Otherwise the symptom is a console that will not load, and nobody connects that to a domain change they never read about.
Memorize the ownership column and let the addresses change, because they will. Microsoft renames and relocates portals regularly; what stays stable is that Conditional Access lives with the directory and mail flow lives with Exchange. When a setting is not where you expect, ask which object owns it rather than searching for the page you remember.

🎫 Licensing Without Tears — E3, E5, Business & Group-Based Assignment M365 • Foundations ▶
Concept
A License Is a Bundle of Service Plans, and the Plans Are What Matter
Admins argue about E3 versus E5 as if they were products. They are bundles: each license contains a list of individually toggleable service plans, and a feature works or does not work because its service plan is enabled for that user. This is why "they have E5, it should work" is not a diagnosis — someone may have disabled the plan at assignment time, or the feature may live in an add-on that was never bought.
TierRoughly, what it addsBought when

Business PremiumIncludes Business Standard's services plus device management, protection against phishing, ransomware and data loss, and litigation hold, for up to 300 usersA business under that size that needs security and managed devices

Tier contents and names change with Microsoft's packaging — treat the split above as the shape of the decision, not as a current feature list, and check the licensing comparison before quoting it to anyone.
Technique
Assign to Groups, Never to People
Group-based licensing makes membership the single source of truth: put a user in the group and the license lands, remove them and it is reclaimed. Direct per-user assignment looks simpler on day one and becomes an audit problem by month six, because nothing records why a given person has a given SKU (Stock Keeping Unit).
PracticeWhy
One group per license bundleMembership answers "who has E5, and why"
Dynamic membership where a rule existsDepartment or job-title rules keep joiners licensed with no ticket
Watch for assignment errorsConflicting plans and missing prerequisites fail silently per user
Never disable plans ad hocA per-user tweak is invisible to the next admin debugging the feature
The last row is the one that costs a license audit. Turning off one service plan for one user is invisible to everybody afterwards and reproduces nowhere, so six months later nobody can say why that person's Teams behaves differently. Put the exception in a group with a name that explains it — the group is the documentation, and it survives the person who created it.

🔑 Admin Roles & Least Privilege — The Roles Worth Knowing, and PIM (Privileged Identity Management) for the Rest M365 • Identity ▶
Concept
Global Admin Is Not an Job Title, It Is a Blast Radius
Global Administrator can read, change and grant everything in the tenant, including making more Global Administrators. Every extra holder is another account whose compromise ends the tenant. The working rule most organizations converge on: a small number of permanent Global Administrators, at least two break-glass accounts that are excluded from Conditional Access and stored offline, and everyone else in the narrowest role that does their actual job.
RoleDoesGive it to
Global AdministratorEverything, including role assignmentAs few people as the org can tolerate
User AdministratorCreate/edit users, reset most passwordsService desk
Exchange AdministratorMailboxes, mail flow, transport rulesMessaging team
SharePoint AdministratorSites, storage, sharing policyCollaboration team
Security ReaderRead security posture, change nothingAuditors, analysts, anyone investigating
Global ReaderRead almost everything, change nothingThe default for "I just need to look"
Global Reader is the answer to most requests for Global Administrator. The great majority of people asking need to look at something, and handing them read-everything costs nothing and removes an account whose compromise ends the tenant. Reserve the top row for a handful of break-glass accounts with strong controls, and make everything else eligible-on-request rather than standing.
Technique
Eligible, Not Assigned — What PIM Actually Buys
Privileged Identity Management converts a standing role into one a person can activate for a bounded window, with justification, approval and an audit trail. The security value is real but often mis-stated: PIM does not stop a compromised session from doing damage during an activation. What it buys is that most accounts are unprivileged most of the time, and that every elevation leaves a record someone can review.

🔁 Self-Service Password Reset & Password Protection — Who Can Reset, and Which Passwords Get Refused M365 • Entra ID ▶
Self-service reset
A User Can Reset Only With Methods They Registered Before They Were Locked Out
Self-service password reset lets users reset or unlock their own password at aka.ms/sspr, with no call to the service desk. Before it lets anyone in, Entra ID runs a short list of checks, and a user who fails any of them is told to contact their administrator. The methods it accepts are Authenticator notifications, OATH tokens, text messages, voice calls and an emailed one-time code, and a policy can require one or two of them. Authenticator and the OATH tokens count as one method between them, and Authenticator cannot be the only method when one is required.
What Entra ID checksWhen it fails
The user is enabled for self-service resetA group-scoped policy left them out
The user registered as many methods as the policy requiresThey never registered, or the policy went from one method to two and they had one
A password managed on-premises can be written backWriteback is off, so a synced or federated user cannot reset in the cloud
Administrators meet the stricter two-gate policyAn admin registered only what the user policy asked for
Require registration at sign-in, or the first reset is when people find out. A user who never registered learns it at the moment they are locked out. The policy can prompt for registration at sign-in and ask for reconfirmation every so many days, and a change from one method to two belongs after everyone has two. Guests signed in with a personal Microsoft account cannot use it at all. Who may reset an administrator's password instead is in Admin Roles & Least Privilege — The Roles Worth Knowing, and PIM for the Rest.
Hybrid
A Synced User's Reset Is Written Back, and Active Directory's Policy Still Decides
When a user's password lives in Active Directory, a cloud reset is passed back to it by Entra Connect Sync or Cloud Sync, outbound over port 443 with no inbound firewall rule. Writeback is synchronous: the on-premises agent sets the password through Active Directory's own interface, so the domain's history, complexity, age and password filters are applied, and the user hears straight away if the password is refused. Writeback can be switched off in the Entra admin center without touching Connect, and can be allowed to unlock an account without resetting it.
Whose passwordWhat a self-service reset does
A cloud-only userSets it in Entra ID
A synced or federated user, writeback onSets it in Entra ID and Active Directory, under the domain's rules
A synced or federated user, writeback offNothing: the user is sent to an administrator
A member of a protected group, such as Domain AdminsCannot be written back: the sync account is not allowed to reset it
To Active Directory, a self-service reset is an admin reset. A third-party password filter configured only for users' own changes is skipped, so it has to be set to apply to admin resets too. And the domain's own policy wins over the cloud's: a password Entra ID would accept can still be refused on the way back. How the synced account came to exist is Entra Connect & Hybrid Identity — Choosing How Passwords Are Checked.
Password Protection
Banned Passwords Are Scored, Not Just Matched: Five Points or It Is Refused
Every change and reset is checked against Microsoft's global banned list, which is always on, cannot be turned off and is never published, and against a custom list of up to 1,000 terms of your own. The new password is lowercased and common substitutions are undone: 0 becomes o, 1 becomes l, $ becomes s and @ becomes a. Banned terms are then found with fuzzy matching, so a term one edit away still counts, and the user's first and last names and the tenant's name are found anywhere inside it if they are four letters or more. Each banned term found scores one point, each other character one point, and a password under five points is refused.
New passwordNormalizedScoreResult
C0ntos0Blank12contosoblank12contoso + blank + 1 + 2 = 4Refused
ContoS0Bl@nkf9!contosoblankf9!contoso + blank + f + 9 + ! = 5Accepted
Put base terms in the custom list, never variants. Microsoft's own example is a company called Contoso, in London, that makes Widgets: three entries, not a thousand spellings of them, because normalization and fuzzy matching already catch Contoso!1 and C0nt0so. The global list is built from Microsoft's sign-in telemetry rather than from breach dumps, aimed at password spraying, which tries a few of the weakest passwords against every account; the attack is in Password & Authentication Attack Types.
On-premises
On Domain Controllers, Start in Audit, and Expect Windows' Old Complexity Message
The same lists reach Active Directory through two pieces of software. A proxy service on any domain-joined server fetches the policy from Entra ID, so domain controllers never talk to the internet, and a DC agent on each domain controller, a password filter and a service, checks every change against the policy cached in SYSVOL (System Volume) and refreshed hourly. Nothing changes in the schema and no ports open on the DCs. A DC without the agent checks nothing, so enforcement is only universal once every DC has it, and a DC that has never downloaded a policy accepts every password and logs a warning.
PieceRuns onDoes
Proxy serviceAny domain-joined serverPasses policy downloads between the DCs and Entra ID, and keeps nothing

The policySYSVOL, replicated between DCsRefreshed when it is more than an hour old
A refusal looks like an ordinary complexity failure. In enforced mode a banned password gets Windows' usual message, that it does not meet the length, complexity, or history requirements of the domain, so a user whose password meets every rule they can see is probably meeting this one. Run in audit mode first and read its events before enforcing. The domain's own policy still applies alongside it, as Group Policy Architecture — Where the Settings Actually Live explains.
🧱 Administrative Units — Delegating Part of the Tenant, and Fencing Off the Accounts That Matter M365 • Entra ID ▶
Scoped roles
An Administrative Unit Narrows Where a Role Applies, Not What the Role Can Do
An administrative unit is a container of users, groups or devices in Entra ID. Assign a role with the unit as its scope, Helpdesk Administrator for a region's support staff for instance, and the role works only on the unit's members and none of the tenant's settings. A user can sit in several units, by place and by division, but units cannot be nested. Members are added by hand, or by a dynamic rule for users or devices, never for groups. Each administrator with a unit-scoped role needs Entra ID P1, the members need nothing beyond Free, and a dynamic rule needs P1 for every member it covers.
You wantWhat does it
One office's helpdesk to reset passwords there and nowhere elseA unit of that office's users, and Helpdesk Administrator scoped to it
Membership that follows HR data, such as departmentA dynamic rule on users or devices, with P1 for each member
A scoped admin who can rename a group and change who is in itThe group added to the unit
The same admin resetting those members' passwordsThe users themselves added to the unit, because a group brings itself in, not its members
Other users hidden from a scoped adminNothing: a unit limits what they can manage, not what they can read
A scoped role still stops at administrators. Within a unit, password resets, authentication methods and account deletion apply only to members who hold no admin role, so an administrator in Seattle is not reset by the Seattle helpdesk. In PowerShell the scope is the string /administrativeUnits/ plus the unit's ID, not the ID alone. The roles worth scoping in the first place are in Admin Roles & Least Privilege — The Roles Worth Knowing, and PIM for the Rest.
Restricted management
A Restricted Management Unit Stops Even Global Administrators Changing What Is Inside
An ordinary unit adds permissions; a restricted management unit takes them away. Once an object is in one, its Entra ID properties can be changed only by administrators assigned at that unit's scope. A tenant-wide Helpdesk, User or even Global Administrator can read the account but cannot reset its password, edit it, delete it or change a protected group's members. It holds users, devices and security groups, but not Microsoft 365 groups, mail-enabled security groups or distribution lists. Global and Privileged Role Administrators can still manage the unit itself, including assigning themselves a role on it, so nobody is locked out for good, and each step is logged.
A tenant-scoped administrator tries toAllowed?

Add a protected user to an ordinary groupYes
Assign themselves a role on the unit, as a Global AdministratorYes, and it lands in the audit log
Fence the accounts whose takeover ends the company, and expect workflows to break. Microsoft's examples are executives' accounts and their devices, whose BitLocker recovery keys a helpdesk could otherwise read, and the security groups that grant access to sensitive applications. A provisioning tool or script working at tenant scope will now fail on those objects, which Microsoft warns about. And the fence is around the directory object: Microsoft 365 services acting on related objects are not blocked. Flow
What Happens Between "Send" and the Inbox
Inbound mail is not handed to a mailbox and inspected afterwards. It runs a pipeline, and knowing the order tells you which control could have stopped a message — and which one cannot be blamed for letting it through.
#StageDecides

Connectors — The Four Shapes, and When Each Is Right

Troubleshooting

Reference
The Commands Worth Having in Muscle Memory
Delegation is one of the few areas where the console genuinely hides things the shell shows plainly — inherited permissions in particular. 
# Connect (v3 module; supports modern auth and MFA)
Connect-ExchangeOnline -UserPrincipalName admin@contoso.com

# Who can open this mailbox? Hide the inherited noise.
Get-MailboxPermission -Identity "support@contoso.com" |
  Where-Object { $_.IsInherited -eq $false -and $_.User -notlike "NT AUTHORITY\*" }

# Send As is a *recipient* permission, listed separately — a classic miss
Get-RecipientPermission -Identity "support@contoso.com"

# Send on Behalf lives on the mailbox object itself
Get-Mailbox -Identity "support@contoso.com" | Select-Object GrantSendOnBehalfTo
Three different cmdlets for three permissions on one mailbox is the single most common reason a delegation audit comes back incomplete.
🛡️ EOP & Defender for Office — Anti-Spam, Anti-Phish, Safe Links & Quarantine Exchange Online • Security ▶
Concept
Two Products, Stacked
Exchange Online Protection is the filtering every tenant gets: connection filtering, malware, spam and spoof intelligence. Defender for Office adds the parts that need detonation and time-of-click evaluation — Safe Attachments, Safe Links, and the investigation tooling. When a vendor asks "do you have Defender for Office", they are asking which of those two layers you are running, not whether you have antivirus.
CapabilityLayerCatches
Connection & spam filteringEOPBulk, known-bad senders, obvious spoofing
Anti-phish policyEOP, deeper with DefenderImpersonation of your own domains and named users
Safe AttachmentsDefenderMalicious payloads no signature knows yet
Safe LinksDefenderURLs (Uniform Resource Locator) weaponized after delivery
Time-of-click evaluation is the capability you are actually buying in the upper tier. A link that is benign when the mail arrives and weaponized an hour later defeats every filter that inspects at delivery, and that is the common shape of a modern phishing campaign — which is why the last row is the one to weigh when the license conversation happens. The anti-phishing row is worth tuning either way: impersonation protection for your own executives is cheap and specific.
Concept
Why Safe Links Exists — the Delayed Weaponization Problem
A link checked once at delivery is checked at the least useful moment. Attackers send a clean URL, pass every filter, then change what it serves an hour later when the mail is sitting read in an inbox. Safe Links rewrites URLs so the check happens when the user clicks, which is the only time the answer is current. The cost is that every link in the mail now points at a Microsoft host, which breaks naive "hover to check the domain" user training — worth knowing before rolling it out.
Troubleshooting
Quarantine — Who Can Release, and the Trap in the Answer
Quarantine policy decides whether users can release their own held mail. The tempting setting — let users release everything — undoes the control for exactly the messages it was most right about, because a convincing phish is convincing to its recipient too.
VerdictSensible user permission
Bulk / spamRelease themselves — high volume, low harm
Phish (high confidence)Request release only; an admin decides
MalwareAdmin only, always

Held mail is kept for a bounded window, the quarantine retention period — 15 days by default for spam and phishing in the default and custom anti-spam policies, 30 in the Standard and Strict presets, and settable from 1 to 30 — after which it is gone rather than delivered. A user who "will get to it next month" will not. The human side of why these messages work at all is covered by Phishing Beyond Email – Smishing, Vishing, and QR Code Scams in the Security domain.
🔎 Message Trace & Header Analysis — Proving Where a Message Died Exchange Online • Troubleshooting ▶
Technique
The Only Question Worth Asking First: Did It Arrive at the Service?
Every "they never got my email" ticket splits at one point. If the message reached Exchange Online, the trace shows it and the problem is yours to solve. If it never arrived, no amount of tenant configuration matters and the answer lies with the sender, their provider or DNS (Domain Name System). Establishing which half you are in takes about a minute and saves the other fifty.
Trace statusMeansNext step
DeliveredHanded to the mailboxLook at Inbox rules, focused inbox, the user's own filters

Trace data has a limited window — 90 days back at most, any 10 days of it answered at once and a longer range only as a downloadable report — so a three-month-old dispute may simply be unanswerable. Pull the trace early.
Reference
Reading a Header Without Reading All of It
Full internet headers are long and almost entirely irrelevant. Five fields answer nearly every question, and they are the ones an impersonation attempt cannot fake without failing.
HeaderTells you
Authentication-ResultsThe SPF, DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) and DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) verdicts as the service computed them
Received (bottom-up)The real path; read from the bottom, which is the origin
Return-PathThe envelope sender — what SPF is actually checked against
FromThe display sender — what the user sees, and what gets forged

🔐 Sensitivity Labels & DLP (Data Loss Prevention) — Classification That Survives Contact With Users Purview • Compliance ▶
Concept
Labels Classify. DLP Enforces. They Are Not the Same Control.
A sensitivity label marks content and can apply protection that travels with the file — encryption, a watermark, access restrictions that hold even after the file leaves the tenant. Data Loss Prevention watches actions and blocks or warns on them. Labels answer "what is this?"; DLP answers "should this be leaving?". Deploying either without the other produces the two classic failures: neat classification nobody enforces, or blocking rules that have no idea what they are protecting.
Sensitivity labelDLP policy

User seesA label in the app, and any watermarkA policy tip, warning, or block
Fails byBeing ignored or mis-appliedFalse positives, then exemptions, then irrelevance
The persistence row is the reason to bother with labels at all. Data Loss Prevention stops an action inside your tenant and is blind the moment the file is elsewhere; an encrypting label keeps working on a laptop that left with somebody. Technique

The most reliable predictor of a failed classification program is the number of labels. Every label is a decision demanded of someone who wants to get on with their work, and the marginal label adds hesitation without adding protection.

🔍 Sensitive Information Types & Endpoint DLP (Data Loss Prevention) — The Pattern That Finds It, and the Device That Stops It M365 • Purview ▶
Sensitive information types
A Sensitive Information Type Is a Pattern Plus Evidence, Scored at Three Confidence Levels
A sensitive information type is the pattern Purview looks for in content, and DLP policies, auto-labeling, retention labels and insider risk all use them. Each pattern has a primary element, which is a regular expression, optionally with a checksum, or a keyword list, a keyword dictionary or one of Microsoft's functions. It can add supporting elements found within so many characters of it, 250 in Microsoft's examples, and the more evidence a match carries, the higher its confidence. Microsoft's built-in types cannot be edited but can be copied, the named-entity types for names, addresses and medical terms cannot even be copied, and exact data match types are built from a table of your own values.
ConfidenceValueA rule set to it returnsMicrosoft pairs it with
Low65Low, medium and high matches: the fewest misses, the most false positivesHigher counts, 20 or more
Medium75Medium and high matchesBetween the two
High85High matches only: the fewest false positives, the most missesLow counts, five to ten
Confidence and count trade against each other. A rule that fires on one low-confidence nine-digit number fires on a great many numbers that are not identity numbers, and a rule that waits for twenty high-confidence matches misses the document that holds three. Pick the pair for the harm you are preventing. What DLP and labels do with a match is Sensitivity Labels & DLP — Classification That Survives Contact With Users.
Writing one
A Custom Pattern's Regex Has One Capturing Group and No Anchors
A custom type is made in the Purview portal under Information Protection > Classifiers > Sensitive info types, or copied from a built-in one. Its regular expressions run on the Boost.Regex 5.1.3 engine and follow two rules Microsoft enforces when the pattern is saved: no ^ or $, because nothing guarantees where scanned content starts or ends, and exactly one capturing group, with every variant inside it as alternation. A new type finds nothing in existing SharePoint and OneDrive content until that content is crawled again, and Microsoft's support will troubleshoot a pattern but not write one. 
# Refused: three capturing groups at the top level
(?:[\s,;])([A-Z]{2}[0-9]{3})(?:[\s,;])|(?:[\s,;])([A-Z]{2}[A-Z]{4}[0-9])(?:[\s,;])|(?:[\s,;])([A-Z]{2}[0-9]{5})(?:[\s,;])

# Accepted: one capturing group, the variants alternated inside it
(?:[\s,;])([A-Z]{2}[0-9]{3}|[A-Z]{2}[A-Z]{4}[0-9]|[A-Z]{2}[0-9]{5})(?:[\s,;])
Tune a custom type before it exists anywhere, not before a policy uses it. Endpoint DLP classifies every file against every type in the tenant, custom ones included, whether or not a policy refers to them, so a loose pattern costs classification on every onboarded device from the day it is saved. Why pattern matching over-matches, and what that costs a DLP program, is in Data Loss Prevention — Classification, Egress & the False-Positive Problem.
Endpoint DLP
On the Device, DLP Watches What Is Done With a File, Not Only Where It Is
Endpoint DLP takes the same policies to onboarded Windows 10 and 11 devices, the three latest macOS releases and Windows Server 2019 or later, though not domain controllers or Server Core, and servers only once it is turned on for them. Devices are onboarded with Intune, Group Policy, Configuration Manager, a local script or a VDI (Virtual Desktop Infrastructure) package. A file is classified each time it is created or changed, and a policy with the devices location applies only when both the user and the device are in its scope. What it adds is the action: copying, printing, uploading or pasting a sensitive file can be audited, blocked, or blocked with an override the user has to justify.

Start in audit; the activity log will already have data. Onboarded devices audit Office, PDF (Portable Document Format) and CSV (Comma-Separated Values) file activity by default even with no policy, so the activity explorer shows what a block would have stopped before anyone is blocked. Reference
Three Numbers, and What Each One Means for a Human
Media quality reduces to three measurements. Users describe them in words, and translating the complaint into the metric is most of the diagnosis.
MetricUser saysUsually caused by
Packet loss"They cut out"Congestion, or Wi-Fi with too many clients

Concept
Precedence — Why the Policy You Assigned Is Not the One in Effect
Every policy type has a Global (org-wide default) instance that applies to everyone who has not been given something else. Layered on top are group assignments and direct user assignments, and they do not merge — one policy wins outright.

Technique

📦 Retention, Litigation Hold & Archiving — Legal's Requirements in Mailbox Terms Exchange Online • Compliance ▶
Concept
Archiving and Retention Are Different Jobs Doing Different Work
The online archive is a storage answer: a second mailbox the user can see, into which old mail is moved on a schedule so the primary stays manageable. Retention is a compliance answer: rules about how long things must be kept or when they must go. They are routinely conflated in requirements documents, and the resulting design satisfies neither — an archive does not preserve anything a user can still delete.
MechanismJobUser sees
Online archiveMove old mail out of the primary mailboxA second folder tree in their client
Auto-expanding archiveGrow that archive past its initial ceilingNothing — it happens behind them
Retention policyKeep or delete on a schedule, org-wideNothing, until something will not delete
Litigation holdPreserve everything for this mailboxNothing at all — deliberately invisible
Archiving does not preserve anything, and that misunderstanding survives in most estates. Moving old mail out of a primary mailbox is a storage decision the user can see and, in some configurations, undo; only retention and holds preserve. The last row is the one to handle carefully — a litigation hold is deliberately invisible, which means it will still be there in three years unless somebody tracks it.
Concept
What a Hold Actually Does to a Mailbox
A hold does not stop a user deleting mail. It stops the deletion being final: purged items are retained in a hidden part of Recoverable Items where the user cannot reach them and eDiscovery can. This is what makes holds legally useful and operationally surprising.
ConsequenceWhat to do about it
Recoverable Items grows without boundMonitor its quota; a full one blocks the user from deleting anything
Held mailboxes cannot be truly deletedA leaver's mailbox becomes inactive rather than gone — that is the point
License implicationsHolds come with E3, E5, Business Premium and Exchange Online Plan 2; Business Basic, Business Standard, E1 and Plan 1 need the Exchange Online Archiving add-on; verify before promising counsel
Releasing a hold is irreversiblePurged content ages out for real once the hold lifts
The most common surprise in the table is the first: a mailbox on hold whose Recoverable Items has filled reports a hard delete failure to a confused user, and the fix is a quota change, not a mail problem.
Choosing
Translating What Legal Asked For
Requirements arrive in legal language and have to become one of four configurations. This mapping is worth having in the room during the conversation, because the difference between rows two and three is the difference between a defensible position and an expensive one.
They saidThey mean
"Keep everything for seven years"A retention policy with a seven-year keep, applied to locations
"Preserve this person's mail, they are under investigation"A hold on that mailbox, scoped to a case, invisible to them
"Delete anything older than three years"A retention policy that deletes — and check nothing else is holding it
"The mailbox is full and they will not tidy it"An archive with a move policy; this is not a compliance question at all
Which rule wins when several apply is decided by the precedence in Retention Policies vs Retention Labels — The Distinction That Trips Everyone, and the workflow for acting on a matter is in eDiscovery, Content Search & the Audit Log — Answering Legal Without Guessing.

🔌 The Admin Modules — Exchange Online, Teams & Graph From One Console M365 • Automation ▶
Reference
Three Modules, Three Connects, One Session
M365 administration from the shell is not one API (Application Programming Interface). Exchange and Teams keep their own modules for their own object models; Graph covers the directory and most newer surfaces. A working admin session usually has two or three connections open at once, each authenticated separately. 
# Install once (current user; no elevation needed)
Install-Module ExchangeOnlineManagement -Scope CurrentUser
Install-Module MicrosoftTeams          -Scope CurrentUser
Install-Module Microsoft.Graph         -Scope CurrentUser

# Connect — each is its own auth prompt and its own session
Connect-ExchangeOnline -UserPrincipalName admin@contoso.com
Connect-MicrosoftTeams
Connect-MgGraph -Scopes "User.Read.All","Group.Read.All"

# Ask for the least you need: consent is per-scope and it is remembered
Get-MgContext | Select-Object Account, Scopes
Technique
Automation Means App-Only Auth, Not a Stored Password
Interactive sign-in is right for a person at a keyboard and wrong for anything scheduled. The supported answer is an app registration with a certificate, granted exactly the application permissions the job needs. The temptation — a service account with a password in the script and MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication) exempted — creates a highly privileged identity that no Conditional Access policy protects, which is precisely the account attackers look for.
DoInstead of
App registration + certificateA password in a script or a vault the script can read unattended
Application permissions, scoped narrowlyGlobal Administrator "so it just works"
Scope Exchange app access to specific mailboxesTenant-wide mailbox access for a job that touches twelve
Rotate and expire the certificate deliberatelyA credential with a two-year life nobody diarized
The third row is the one that gets skipped and is the difference between a scoped job and a tenant-wide credential. Application permissions for mail are all-mailboxes by default, and scoping them to the handful the job touches takes one extra configuration step that almost nobody performs. Do it at creation; retrofitting it means finding out which mailboxes the script has been quietly reading.
Troubleshooting
Throttling Is Normal — Write for It
These are shared, multi-tenant services, and they will throttle a script that behaves like a crawler. A throttled response is not an error to retry immediately; it carries a wait time, and honoring it is the difference between a job that finishes and one that is progressively slowed.
SymptomHandling
HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) 429 from GraphSleep for the interval the response names, then retry — do not back off blindly
A loop of thousands of single readsBatch them; Graph accepts up to 20 requests per batch
Re-reading data that has not changedUse delta queries and ask only for the properties you use
Exchange cmdlets slowing over a long sessionReconnect; long-lived sessions accumulate their own limits
The Graph API itself — query shape, permissions model and paging — is covered by Microsoft Graph From PowerShell — The Modern Admin API in the Scripting domain. This card is the M365-specific part: which module owns what, and how to authenticate a job rather than a person.

Concept
The Constraint That Shapes the Whole Project
A verified domain can exist in exactly one tenant. That single fact drives everything else: contoso.com cannot receive mail in both tenants during a transition, so either the cutover is a hard switch for everyone at once, or users are temporarily addressed somewhere else. Every tenant-to-tenant plan is really a plan for working around this, and every timeline that ignores it is wrong.
ConstraintConsequence for the plan
One domain, one tenantThe mail cutover is a single coordinated event
Identity does not moveAccounts are created in the target, not transferred; SIDs (Security Identifier) and object IDs change
Teams is the hardest workloadChat history and private channels migrate poorly; set expectations early
Devices are re-enrolledAn Entra-joined device belongs to its tenant; it leaves and rejoins
Third-party tooling is normalNative tooling covers a fraction; budget for a migration product
The first row sets the shape of the whole program, and everything else is consequence. One domain in one tenant means the mail cutover is a single coordinated event with no staged middle — so the plan is built backwards from that hour. Budget for the last row honestly at the start: native tooling covers a fraction, and discovering that halfway through is how migrations slip a quarter.
Flow

🛡️ Defender for Office in Depth — Policy Order & Why a Message Got Through M365 • Exchange Online • Security ▶
The Fingerprint

The question every mail administrator is asked after a phish reaches an inbox, and few can answer quickly, is which policy applied, and why did it allow this? The answer is almost never “protection was off”. It is that protection is a stack of policies evaluated in a specific order, and a message takes the verdict of the first one that matches — so a higher-priority policy, or an allow-entry someone added months ago, quietly overrode the protection everyone assumed was in force.

⛔ Restricted Entities & Outbound Spam — Why a Mailbox Stops Sending, and Unblocking It Safely Exchange Online • Security ▶
Blocked from sending
A Mailbox Over Its Sending Limit Still Receives, and Everything It Sends Bounces With 5.1.8
Every Exchange Online mailbox may send to 10,000 recipients in any 24 hours, a hard limit of the service, and an outbound spam policy can set lower hourly and daily ones. Sending faster than 30 messages a minute is only slowed down. A sender who goes over a recipient limit, or who sends more spam than thresholds Microsoft does not publish, is restricted. They can still receive mail, but every message they send comes back in a bounce with 550 5.1.8 Access denied, bad outbound sender, and the user is listed on the Restricted entities page in the Defender portal, under Email & collaboration > Review, which is where an administrator unblocks them. Microsoft does not publish the spam thresholds so that spammers cannot work just under them.

A sending limit is a compromise detector that happens to be a quota. Microsoft calls a sender over the limits an indicator of a compromised account, and the limits are set high enough that an ordinary business user never reaches them. A user who suddenly has is far more often an attacker sending from a real mailbox than a salesperson with a big list, which is the case Business Email Compromise — The Attack With No Malware makes from the other side.
Unblocking
Unblock Last: Microsoft's Order Secures the Account Before It Can Send Again
Microsoft's steps for a restricted user assume the account is compromised, and they run in an order. Disable the account, or at least reset its password, and revoke its sessions. Remove sign-in methods and consented apps the user did not add, and admin roles they should not hold. Then remove the forwarding and inbox rules the attacker made, hidden rules included. Only then unblock the user, in the portal or with Remove-BlockedSenderAddress; the restriction lifts within an hour as a rule and within 24 at most. A password reset does not revoke app passwords, and for an account synced from Active Directory Microsoft says to reset it there, twice. 
# Who is restricted (Exchange Online PowerShell)
Get-BlockedSenderAddress

# Forwarding set on the mailbox, then every inbox rule, hidden ones included
Get-Mailbox -Identity user@contoso.com | Format-List Forwarding*Address,DeliverTo*
Get-InboxRule -Mailbox user@contoso.com -IncludeHidden |
  Format-List Name,Enabled,RedirectTo,Forward*,Identity

# Signed-in sessions and refresh tokens (Microsoft Graph PowerShell)
Revoke-MgUserSignInSession -UserId user@contoso.com

# Last, once the account is yours again
Remove-BlockedSenderAddress -SenderAddress user@contoso.com
Unblocking first gives the attacker back a mailbox that can send. The portal's unblock wizard says the same thing in its own steps, offering MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication) and a password reset before it lets the user go. A user who cannot send calls the service desk, and the pressure is to unblock them on the call, when the restriction may be the only thing that has stopped the attacker so far. Why a reset alone leaves a stolen session signed in is Token Lifetime & Revocation — The Logout That Does Not, and How to Fix It.
Connectors and the tenant
A Connector, or the Whole Tenant, Can Be Restricted Too, and the Bounce Code Says Which
The Restricted entities page lists connectors as well as users. An inbound connector from an on-premises server that an attacker created or changed to relay spam is blocked, and its mail bounces with 550 5.7.711. Above both sits the tenant. A daily cap on external recipients, the tenant external recipient rate limit, scales with the number of licenses, and mail from the tenant's onmicrosoft.com domain may reach only 100 external recipients a day. When a tenant sends too much spam, often through a compromised on-premises server or a connector made with a stolen admin account, Exchange Online blocks its outgoing mail altogether, and only Microsoft Support lifts that.

How a policy works
An Alert Policy Watches Audited Activity, and Fires Every Time, Past a Threshold, or When It Is Unusual
An alert policy names an activity from the audit log, conditions on it such as who did it or from which IP (Internet Protocol) address, and when to fire: every time it happens, when it passes a count within a time window, or when it is unusual against a baseline Microsoft builds over up to seven days. Each policy carries a category and a severity, Low, Medium, High or Informational, and can email a list of recipients up to a daily limit. Thresholds and unusual-activity alerts need E5, or E3 with an add-on such as Defender for Office 365 Plan 2; on E3 alone a policy can only fire every time. A new or changed policy takes up to 24 hours to start firing, and none of it works with audit logging off.

Writing your own
The Forwarding-Rule Alert Misses Rules Made in Outlook Itself, and a Custom Policy Fills the Gap

The Inversion
Hybrid Is a Migration State That Becomes Permanent
Exchange hybrid is sold and understood as a bridge — a temporary coexistence while mailboxes move to the cloud. The uncomfortable pattern is that the bridge becomes the destination. The last few mailboxes on-premises — the shared mailbox an app relies on, the mailbox tied to a legacy scanner, the one nobody dares touch — cost more to move than the previous thousand did, so the migration stalls at 98% and the on-premises Exchange server that was meant to be decommissioned runs forever, patched and licensed, for a handful of objects.

The Decision
Name the Exit Condition, Because Nobody Plans One
The fix is not technical brilliance; it is a decision at the start of the migration that hybrid ends, with a written exit condition: what has to be true — apps re-pointed, mailboxes moved, directory management moved to the cloud — for the on-premises Exchange to be switched off. Without that line, the last 2% is always someone else's problem next quarter.
Define, before the first mailbox moves, what “done” means and who owns getting there — including the plan for app mail relay and the final directory-management move. The migration that finishes is the one that treated hybrid as a phase with an end date; the one that runs a legacy server for a decade treated it as an architecture. 📮 Exchange Server On-Prem — DAG (Database Availability Group), Transport & the Server You Can't Quite Remove Exchange • Hybrid ▶
The Inversion
You Moved the Mailboxes to the Cloud. You Still Run an Exchange Server.
Migrating to Exchange Online is meant to end on-prem Exchange — and for the estate that still syncs identity from on-prem Active Directory, it doesn't, for a reason that surprises people. When AD is the source of authority for identity, the supported way to manage mail attributes — proxy addresses, mail-enabling, distribution groups — is the Exchange management tools, and those require at least one Exchange Server on-prem. Move every mailbox to the cloud and you still keep a “last Exchange server” for recipient management, because editing those attributes directly in AD is unsupported and breaks at the next schema change. The mailboxes left; the management dependency did not.
What still ties you to on-prem ExchangeWhy
Recipient managementMail attributes are edited through Exchange tools when AD is authoritative — not by hand in AD
Hybrid mail flowConnectors and transport between on-prem and Exchange Online
Public folders / legacy workloadsAnything not yet migrated still needs a host
The last Exchange server is not a mailbox host — it is a management shim for AD-sourced identity. The management-tools-only option reduces its footprint, but the only true removal is moving identity authority to the cloud, so recipient management no longer needs on-prem tools at all.
The Fingerprint
The DAG Keeps Mailboxes Alive; the Transport Pipeline Decides Where Mail Goes and Dies
Two on-prem mechanics every Exchange admin meets. High availability is the Database Availability Group: several servers each hold a copy of every mailbox database, with automatic failover — the unit of resilience is the database copy, not the server, so a box can be failed over, patched and failed back while a copy elsewhere stays active. Mail flow is the transport pipeline: a message passes front-end transport, the categorizer and mailbox transport, evaluated against transport rules and connectors at each hop — and “why did this message get dropped or rerouted” is answered by knowing which hop and which rule acted. The message tracking logs are the record.
MechanicWhat it doesThe question it answers
DAGDatabase copies across servers, automatic failoverWhich copy is active, and can I patch this node?
Transport pipelineFront-end → categorizer → mailbox, with rules and connectorsWhich hop and which rule changed this message's fate?
Message tracking logsA per-hop record of every messageWhere, precisely, did it go or die?
The DAG makes a server disposable; the transport pipeline makes a message's fate traceable. It is the same discipline the cloud Message Trace applies, just on your own tin.
The Decision
Keep Only What Hybrid Requires, Patch It Religiously, and Plan the Exit From On-Prem Identity
The trap is treating the leftover Exchange as either fully retired — and managing AD attributes in unsupported ways that break on the next update — or fully operational, running a whole HA (High Availability) estate for what is now a management shim. And there is a security edge: an unpatched on-prem Exchange Server is one of the most reliably exploited boxes on the internet, a perennial target for internet-facing pre-authentication exploits, so a neglected “last server” is not dormant — it is exposed.
So the honest plan is two-layered. Operationally: keep the last Exchange server minimal and patched on the day updates ship, because it is both load-bearing and a prime target. Strategically: the exit is not “remove Exchange” but “stop AD being authoritative for identity” — until then the shim stays. It is the on-prem half of the coexistence Exchange Hybrid — The Coexistence Nobody Meant to Keep describes, and its mail-flow tracing is the on-prem sibling of Message Trace & Header Analysis — Proving Where a Message Died.
🕐 Calendar Time Zones — Why the Invite Lands an Hour Out Exchange Online • Troubleshooting ▶
The Inversion
A Meeting Has No Time — It Has an Instant and Several Renderings
The store holds one instant. Every attendee's client then renders that instant in a time zone it decided on for itself, and the meeting the user is looking at is the rendering, not the instant. This is why "the invite is an hour out" is never a question about the meeting — the instant is almost always correct, and the ticket is a disagreement between two renderings of it.

That reframing does the diagnostic work, because it makes the useful question obvious and it is the one nobody asks: who sees it wrong? One attendee, everybody, or only the room. Each answer points at a different object, and without it every cause below stays on the table.
Who sees it wrongWhat to look at
One attendee, everyone else fineThat person's client or mailbox zone — the two can disagree, and the mailbox wins for anything server-side
Everybody, by the same offsetThe organizer's zone when they created it, or an equipment or room object in the invite
Only the panel outside the roomThe room mailbox carries its own zone, set at creation and usually never touched again
Only meetings from one external partyAn iCal feed with a floating time and no zone; the receiving client renders it in its own
Half a recurring seriesThe series crosses a daylight-saving boundary — see below, because this one is not a misconfiguration
Ask who sees it wrong before touching a setting. Three of these five rows are fixed on an object the reporting user does not own, and the reflex — walking the caller through their own Outlook settings — confirms nothing when the answer is a room mailbox or the organizer's laptop.
Failure mode
The Recurring Series That Is Right Until the Clocks Change
A recurrence is not a list of instants. It is a rule — 09:00 every Tuesday — plus the zone that rule is read in, and daylight saving is what makes those two facts come apart. A weekly meeting stored against a zone stays at 09:00 local across the change, which is normally what everyone wants. Stored against a fixed offset instead, it moves to 08:00 or 10:00 for the rest of the year.

The tell is that the series splits. Occurrences before the change are correct and occurrences after are not, or the reverse, and the boundary is a date rather than an attendee. It is also the one cause on this page that can be nobody's mistake: two countries change clocks on different weekends, so a call between them genuinely is an hour out for two or three weeks a year and the calendar is telling the truth.
SymptomCause
Correct before a date, wrong afterFixed offset rather than a named zone, or a stale time-zone database on one endpoint
Wrong for a fortnight twice a year, then rightThe two ends change clocks on different weekends. Not a fault
Wrong only for attendees in one countryA region that stopped observing daylight saving; the rule changed and an endpoint has the old one
Moved after the organizer traveledTheir client adopted the device zone and re-anchored new items to it
Do not repair a broken series occurrence by occurrence. Delete it and recreate it from a machine whose zone you have checked, because editing single instances of a series produces exceptions that survive the fix and fail again at the next change. The one exception is a meeting that genuinely spans two countries mid-transition — that one gets an explanation to the attendees, not a change.
The Decision
What to Standardize, and What Is Not Yours to Fix
Most of this is preventable at provisioning rather than diagnosable at the service desk, and the prevention costs nothing at the point it is cheap.
DoBecause
Set the zone on room and equipment mailboxes at creationThey default to the tenant's and nobody revisits them; a room in another office is wrong from day one
Include the zone in the joiner checklist for anyone hired outside the head officeThe mailbox zone is set on first sign-in and inherits whatever the machine said that morning
Treat an external booking system as untrusted for timeA floating time has no zone to be wrong about; the fix is at the sender and rarely available
Keep endpoints patchedTime-zone rules are data, governments change them, and the update arrives as a patch
This is not the storing-timestamps problem, and the two get conflated in every discussion of the subject. Dates & Times — Trickier Than You Think covers what a system should record, and the answer there — store the instant, render late — is already what the calendar does. What is left here is a configuration disagreement between objects that each hold an opinion about a zone, and no amount of correctness in the store prevents it. Fix the object, not the meeting.
🎫 Entra ID Governance — Entitlement Management & Access Reviews Entra • Governance ▶
The Inversion
Access by Ticket Is a Permanent Grant Nobody Remembers to Revoke
The default way people get access — raise a ticket, an admin adds them to a group, role or app — has a silent flaw: the grant is permanent. Nobody circles back when the project ends or the person changes teams, so access accretes and the effective tenure of a permission is “forever”. Entitlement management inverts it: access becomes an access package — a bundle of groups, roles, apps and sites — that a user requests self-service, an approver grants, and, critically, that is time-boxed and expires or comes up for review on its own. The unit of access stops being a standing membership and becomes a reviewed, expiring assignment.
AspectTicket-based accessEntitlement management
GrantManual, and permanent by defaultSelf-service request → approval → time-boxed assignment
What is grantedOne group or role at a timeAn access package — groups, roles, apps and sites together
Who may requestWhoever knows to askScoped by policy — internal users, specific groups, or external guests via a catalog
End of lifeNobody revokes itAutomatic expiry, with recurring review built in
Entitlement management makes the default state of a grant temporary and reviewed — the exact opposite of the ticket model's default of permanent and forgotten.
The Fingerprint
The Access Review's Default Action Is Where Governance Quietly Fails
An access review recertifies who still needs a group, role or app: a reviewer — the resource owner, the user's manager, or the users themselves by self-attestation — confirms or removes access on a schedule. The failure has a precise fingerprint: a review whose default action for non-responders is “approve”. Reviewers ignore the mail, the timer expires, everyone keeps their access — and the review still produces a green tick for the auditor while changing nothing. The whole value lives in the default: set non-response to remove, and a review nobody answers actually cleans up.
Review choiceWhat it really decides
Reviewer = the users themselvesCheap, but self-attestation is the weakest signal
Reviewer = resource owner or managerStronger — if they actually engage
Default on no response = approveThe review is theater; stale access survives
Default on no response = removeThe review has teeth; silence revokes
A review is only as good as its default action. Remove on no response is the single setting that turns a compliance checkbox into an actual control.
The Decision
Make Access Answerable First; Automate the Lifecycle Second
Entitlement management and access reviews are the engine of Entra ID Governance, with lifecycle workflows automating the joiner/mover/leaver steps on top. The reach-for- governance mistake is buying the Governance licenses and building elaborate access packages before you can even answer the basic audit question — who has access to what, and why. Start by making access answerable: catalog the resources, put the high-risk ones behind access packages with expiry, and run reviews with a remove-default on the crown jewels.
So the decision is sequencing, not tooling: standing access behind expiring access packages, reviews that revoke on silence, and lifecycle automation added only once the manual model is sound. It is the resource-access twin of just-in-time admin — Admin Roles & Least Privilege — The Roles Worth Knowing, and PIM for the Rest time-boxes privileged roles, entitlement management time-boxes standing access — and it pairs with the risk-driven gate Entra ID Protection — Conditional Access That Reacts to Risk, Not Just Rules applies at sign-in. Together they move the estate off permanent grants entirely.
