HELP DESK AS A FOUNDATION
Never Underestimate Tier 1

You'll touch every technology: networking, hardware, OS (Operating System), software, email, printers, VPNs (Virtual Private Network). You'll learn how organizations actually use technology. You'll develop communication skills under pressure. Most senior engineers started at a help desk. Do the job well, build your skills after hours, and move up within 12-18 months.
🤝 Professional Networking — Your Career Grows Through Relationships LIFESTYLE • Career ▶
THE HIDDEN JOB MARKET
Most Jobs Are Never Posted
Studies suggest 70-85% of jobs are filled through connections before or without a public posting. The public job board is where you compete with 200 strangers. The hidden market is where someone says "I know the perfect person for this." You want to be that person.
HOW TO BUILD YOUR NETWORK
Genuinely, Not Transactionally
Networking isn't about collecting LinkedIn contacts to spam when you need a job. It's about building relationships over time with people who share your interests.
• Tech communities: BSides security conferences, DEF CON groups, local user groups, Discord servers
• Open source: contribute even small things (docs, bug reports); maintainers notice
• Blogging/writing: write up what you learned even if it seems basic — beginners are looking for exactly that
• Mentorship: find a mentor AND mentor someone below you; both teach you things
LINKEDIN ACTUALLY WORKS
But Only If You Use It Right
• Complete profile with skills, certifications, and projects
• Write short posts about what you're learning — not performative, but genuine. "I just got my OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional), here's what actually helped me."
• Connect with people at companies you want to work for and have genuine conversations before asking for referrals
• Recruiters use LinkedIn constantly — if your profile reflects your skills, inbounds will come to you
THE GIVE-FIRST RULE
Add Value Before You Ask
The single most effective networking approach: add value to people without expecting anything in return. Answer questions in forums. Share useful resources. Help someone debug their script. Give a presentation at a meetup. Write a blog post. When you've given, people want to give back — and when you do need something, you'll have built the trust to ask.
✍️ Clear Technical Communication — The Skill That Multiplies Everything LIFESTYLE • Professional ▶
WHY IT MATTERS
Technical Skill ÷ Communication = Invisible
The most technically brilliant person in the room accomplishes nothing if they can't explain their findings, get buy-in for solutions, or write a ticket that others can act on. Communication is the multiplier for all your other skills. A mediocre engineer who communicates exceptionally will advance faster than a brilliant engineer who can't write a coherent email.
PYRAMID PRINCIPLE
Lead With Conclusion, Then Support
People in IT often bury the conclusion: "I checked the logs, then ran a packet capture, then tested the firewall rules, then discovered... the problem is a misconfigured DNS (Domain Name System) entry." That's backwards. Lead with: "The outage is caused by a DNS misconfiguration (confirmed). Fix takes 10 minutes. Here's what I found." Then explain. Decision-makers need the conclusion first.
ASKING GOOD QUESTIONS
Show Your Work Before You Ask
Before asking for help, document: what you're trying to do, what you've tried, what happened, and what you expected. "It doesn't work" is unusable. "I'm trying to get nginx to serve on port 443. When I run nginx -t it reports a valid config. When I curl localhost:443 I get 'connection refused'. ss -tlnp | grep 443 shows nothing listening. Here's my config block." — THIS gets a fast answer.
DOCUMENTATION
Write It Down or It Didn't Happen
Undocumented systems exist only inside the head of whoever built them. When that person is gone (new job, vacation, hospital) everything breaks. The rule: if you configure it, document it. If you solve it, write down the solution. If you build it, document how it runs, why it's built the way it is, and how to troubleshoot common problems.
Documentation doesn't have to be perfect — a messy Confluence page, a README, or even a Slack post that gets pinned is infinitely better than nothing.
BLAMELESS INCIDENT REVIEWS
Learn, Don't Prosecute
Post-mortems (incident reviews) are most valuable when they're blameless. "Who caused this?" prevents honest reporting and creates a culture of fear where people hide mistakes. "Why did our system make it easy for this mistake to happen?" leads to systemic fixes. The best organizations (Google, Netflix) publish their post-mortems publicly — the learning is more valuable than the embarrassment.
🧑‍🏫 Mentorship — The Fastest Way to Grow LIFESTYLE • Career ▶
FINDING A MENTOR
Ask Specifically, Not Generally
Don't send a cold message saying "Will you be my mentor?" — that's a big, undefined commitment that busy people usually decline. Instead:
• Ask one specific question
• Ask for 20 minutes of their time on a specific topic
• Come prepared with what you've already tried
• Make it easy for them to say yes or no
If it goes well, the relationship develops naturally. Start small.
BEING A GOOD MENTEE
Respect Their Time and Follow Through
The best mentees:
• Do the work between sessions (don't show up empty-handed)
• Come with specific questions, not "what should I do with my career?"
• Follow through on suggestions and report back
• Say thank you, specifically ("That advice about focusing on networking certs before security opened three job interviews")
Mentors give more to people who visibly use their advice.
MENTOR OTHERS
Teaching Forces You to Actually Understand
You don't need to be an expert to help someone with less experience. If you're 6 months ahead of someone, your struggles are fresh and your explanations more relatable than an expert's. Explaining something you've recently learned solidifies YOUR understanding dramatically. The cycle: learn it → teach it → deeply know it.
🎯 Interview Preparation — Getting the Job LIFESTYLE • Career ▶
THE STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) METHOD
Answering Behavioral Questions
Behavioral questions ask you to describe past situations: "Tell me about a time when..." They're designed to predict future behavior based on past behavior. The STAR method gives your answer structure so it's clear, complete, and compelling — instead of rambling.
LetterComponentWhat to Include
SSituationSet the context. Where, when, what was your role? Keep brief (1-2 sentences).
TTaskWhat was YOUR responsibility? What needed to be done or fixed?
AActionWhat did YOU specifically do? Use "I", not "we." This is the meat — be specific.
RResultWhat happened? Quantify if possible ("reduced tickets by 30%", "prevented $50k outage"). What did you learn?

You Should Always Negotiate

⚖️ Professional Ethics — The Responsibility That Comes With Access LIFESTYLE • Integrity ▶
WHY ETHICS MATTERS HERE
You Hold Keys to the Kingdom
IT professionals — especially in security and admin roles — have extraordinary access: to private data, to systems that affect people's lives, to the ability to surveil or disrupt. With that access comes responsibility. Your reputation for integrity is the single most valuable asset in your career; it takes years to build and seconds to destroy. Employers hire trust as much as skill.
CORE PRINCIPLES
Common Threads Across IT Codes of Ethics
Organizations like (ISC)², ISACA (Information Systems Audit and Control Association), and ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) publish codes of ethics. The recurring principles:
PrincipleIn Practice
Protect the public & common goodPublic safety and trust come before employer or self-interest
Act with integrity & honestyDon't misrepresent your skills, findings, or qualifications
Respect privacy & confidentialityDon't snoop in data you can access but have no business reason to view
Provide diligent, competent serviceStay within your competence; keep learning; don't fake it on critical systems
Avoid conflicts of interestDisclose anything that could bias your judgment
Authorized access only"I can" never means "I may" — access requires authorization AND a reason
THE "JUST BECAUSE YOU CAN" TRAP
Access Is Not Permission
The most common ethical failure in IT isn't dramatic sabotage — it's curiosity. An admin who reads a colleague's emails "out of curiosity," peeks at executive salaries in a database, or looks up an ex's records. You may have the technical access, but using it without a legitimate business reason is a serious ethical (and often legal) violation. The rule: access data only when you have both authorization and a genuine work reason. Assume every access is logged — because it usually is.
WHEN YOU'RE PRESSURED
Doing the Right Thing Under Pressure
Sometimes a manager or client pressures you to do something wrong: ignore a vulnerability, falsify a report, cut a security corner to ship faster, delete logs, grant inappropriate access. This is where the mindsets earlier in this guide come together. "Assume makes an ass of u and me" — get the request in writing; don't assume you understood the intent or that it's authorized. "You can't make someone make the right choice, but you can pick up the pieces" — state your professional objection clearly and in writing, offer the safer alternative, and escalate if needed; if they still choose poorly, you've created a record and done your duty. And "not my circus, not my monkeys" protects you from owning the emotional weight of a bad decision that wasn't yours to make.
When Pressured To...Professional Response
Ignore a serious vulnerabilityDocument the risk in writing; recommend the fix; escalate per policy
Grant access that violates least privilegeAsk for written authorization from the data owner
Delete or alter logs/evidenceRefuse — this may be illegal (obstruction, spoliation); escalate
Cut a security corner to hit a deadlineSurface the tradeoff to decision-makers in writing; let them own the risk decision
Know your organization's whistleblower and escalation channels before you need them. A clear, calm, documented objection protects both your integrity and your career — and is exactly what separates a trusted professional from a liability.

IC OR MANAGER?
Two Valid Ladders
Growth doesn't require becoming a manager. Most organizations have two parallel tracks: the Individual Contributor (IC) track (Senior → Staff → Principal engineer) and the Management track (Lead → Manager → Director). Staff+ ICs have just as much seniority and impact as managers — through deep technical leadership rather than people management. Choose based on what energizes you: solving hard technical problems, or growing people and teams. Neither is "higher."

Beginner Soft Skills – The Difference Between a Good and Great IT Pro
Core Concept
Why Soft Skills Matter More Than You Think
Technical skill gets you hired. Soft skills get you promoted, trusted, and recommended. Most IT failures aren't technical — they're communication failures: unclear tickets, blame-shifting, talking over people's heads, or simply not listening.

The best engineers aren't always the smartest — they're the ones people want to work with. That reputation compounds over a career far more than any certification.
Communication
Translating Tech for Non-Technical People
A huge part of IT work is explaining technical issues to people who don't share your vocabulary — managers, customers, other departments. Three techniques:

1. Lead with impact, not mechanism. Instead of "the database connection pool exhausted its max_connections limit," say "the system is slow because too many people are using it at once — we're adding capacity now."
2. Use analogies. "Think of a firewall like a bouncer at a club door — it checks IDs (rules) before letting anyone in."
3. Answer the question they're really asking: usually "is this bad?", "how long until it's fixed?", and "do I need to do anything?"

"Assume" makes an ass out of you and me — don't assume the person you're explaining to already knows the basics. Asking "would it help if I explained how DNS (Domain Name System) works first?" is a sign of respect, not condescension — when offered with the right tone.
Teamwork
Working Well on a Team Under Pressure
Incidents and deadlines bring out the worst in some people — finger-pointing, panic, talking over each other. Stand out by doing the opposite:

• State facts, not blame. "The deploy at 2 PM correlates with the spike" lands very differently from "Bob's deploy broke everything."
• Ask clarifying questions before jumping to solutions. "What changed right before this started?"
• Volunteer for the unglamorous work — writing the postmortem, updating the runbook, taking notes during the incident call. People remember who showed up.
• Give credit publicly, give feedback privately.

"Not my circus, not my monkey" applies to drama, not to people. Stay out of office politics — but always be willing to help a colleague who's struggling with a technical problem.
Self-Management
Owning Your Mistakes — and Your Growth
Everyone breaks production eventually. What defines your career is what happens next.

When you make a mistake:
1. Say so immediately — hiding it always makes it worse.
2. Focus on fixing it before discussing how it happened.
3. After it's resolved, write down what you learned.
4. Build a safeguard so it can't happen the same way again.

You can't make someone make the right choice, yet you can pick up the pieces afterwards — this applies to yourself too. You will misjudge things. The professionals who last decades aren't the ones who never err — they're the ones who recover gracefully, learn fast, and don't repeat the same mistake twice.

Concept

The default model for teaching a technical thing is to explain it clearly and thoroughly. Understanding while someone else drives is a different capability from doing it yourself, and only the second one is what anybody wanted.

🗂️ Curriculum Design & Assessment — Objectives, Sequencing & Cutting the Nice-to-Know LIFESTYLE • Enablement ▶
Concept
Design Backwards From What They Must Be Able to Do
The natural way to build a course is forward from what you know: start at the beginning of the subject and work through it. That produces a syllabus shaped like the topic rather than like the job, and it is why so much technical training spends its first third on history and architecture before anyone touches anything. Backward design inverts it — name the tasks people must perform, decide how you would know they can, and only then choose what to teach.
StepQuestion
1. OutcomesWhat must they be able to do, unaided, afterwards?

3. PracticeWhat exercises build up to producing that?
4. ContentWhat is the minimum they must know to attempt the exercises?

📐 Diagrams That Explain — The Small Number of Shapes Worth Using LIFESTYLE • Enablement ▶
Concept

The test before drawing: what question does this answer, and who is asking it? If you cannot state the question, the diagram will become the everything-picture by default.

📣 Proposals, Executives & Speaking — Being Persuasive Outside Your Team LIFESTYLE • Enablement ▶

🔎 Discovery & Managing Scope — The First Two Weeks, and Change Control Without Being Difficult LIFESTYLE • Independent ▶

Concept
Handover — Leaving Them Better, Not Dependent
Dependency is commercially attractive and professionally corrosive: the client who cannot operate what you built keeps paying, and every subsequent engagement is a rescue rather than a choice. 📊 The Assessment Report — Findings, Risk-Ranked Recommendations & an Owner per Item LIFESTYLE • Independent ▶
Concept

🧾 The Business Side — Cash Flow, Contracts & the Boring Protections That Matter LIFESTYLE • Independent ▶
Concept
Profitable Businesses Fail on Cash, Not on Profit
The number that ends independent practices is not the rate; it is the gap between doing the work and being paid for it. Thirty-day terms mean forty-five in practice and sixty when a large client's finance system is involved — so work delivered in January can be paid in March, while rent and tax are monthly. An independent can be fully booked, well priced, profitable on paper, and unable to pay themselves.

💷 Asking for a Raise — The Case, Not the Conversation CAREER • Money ▶
The Inversion
A Raise Is a Written Case Delivered Before the Budget, Not a Brave Conversation During the Review
People treat asking for a raise as a nerve problem — how to work up to the awkward conversation. That framing loses before it starts, because by the time of your annual review the budget is already allocated and your manager is dividing a fixed pool. The raise is won earlier and elsewhere: a written case, built on market data and delivered to the person who sets the budget before they set it. Negotiating a first offer is a single moment of leverage; an internal raise is a campaign with a calendar.
The case rests onNot on
Market rate for your role & region, with sourcesHow long it has been since your last raise
Scope you now own that you did not beforeHow hard you work, or how much you need it
Outcomes with a number attachedA list of tasks completed
Timing — before the budget cycle closesThe review meeting, where the money is already spent
The two arguments that feel most natural — loyalty and need — are the two that do not move a budget, because neither is about your value to the organisation. Market and contribution are the only levers, and both are strongest written down and raised early.
The Decision
Make It Easy to Say Yes, and Easy to Escalate Upward
Your manager usually cannot grant a raise alone; they have to argue it to their manager and finance. So the real job is to hand your manager the case they will re-present on your behalf — the market data, the scope, the numbers — so that saying yes is repeating your argument, not building one. A raise you make your manager assemble is a raise you have made harder to get.
Write the one-page case, deliver it a cycle ahead of the budget, and make your manager your advocate by giving them the ammunition to fight for you upward. The first-offer negotiation in Negotiating Your First IT Offer Without Feeling Like You're Being Greedy is a moment; this is a process — and the process, run early, beats the moment.
🧑‍🏫 Teaching a Tool You Just Learned — The Honest Way to Do It CAREER • Teaching ▶
Concept
One Step Ahead Is the Best Distance to Teach From
The instinct is to wait until you are an expert. Do not — the expert has forgotten what was confusing, and you have not. Someone who learned a tool three weeks ago still remembers which step failed silently, which flag they got wrong twice, and what the documentation assumed you already knew. That is worth more to a beginner than mastery, and it expires quickly: within a few months you will have stopped noticing the things that stopped you.

That last row is the highest-value artefact a recent learner can produce and the one experts almost never write: a table of the exact error text and what it actually indicated. It is the thing people search for at 11pm, and only someone who hit it recently has it written down.
Reference

The credibility problem is real and it has a simple solution: say where you are. Nobody minds learning from a beginner who is clear about being one; people mind a beginner performing expertise, and they can always tell.
SayNot

🧩 Subcontracting & Partnering — Growing Past Your Own Hours CAREER • Practice ▶
Concept
The Ceiling Is Arithmetic, and There Are Only Three Ways Through It
An independent practice built on billable hours has a hard maximum: your available hours multiplied by your rate, minus the time you spend selling and administering. Everyone who stays independent long enough meets that ceiling, and the three ways past it are raising the rate, selling something that is not hours, or involving other people. This card is about the third, which is the one that changes what the business *is* rather than what it charges.

ReferralIntroducing and stepping awayAlmost none — and almost no upside beyond goodwill

The third row is where most people get burned first, and the reason is worth stating plainly: when you are prime, the client's opinion of the work is their opinion of you. Delegating delivery does not delegate reputation, so the review effort you must spend is a real cost that has to be inside the margin before you agree the price.

📦 Productising a Service — From Bespoke Hours to a Repeatable Offer CAREER • Practice ▶
Concept
Same Work, Named Scope, Fixed Price
Productising does not mean building software. It means taking work you already do repeatedly, defining exactly what is included and excluded, pricing it as a unit, and selling it by name — "a two-week Microsoft 365 security review, £X, this deliverable" rather than "consulting, day rate, we'll scope it". The work is the same. What changes is that the sale is faster, the delivery is rehearsed, and the margin improves every time you run it because you have done it before.
Bespoke hoursA productised offer

DeliveryImprovised, competentlyA rehearsed sequence with reusable artefacts

Buyer's decisionApprove an open-ended engagementApprove a known amount for a known thing
The last row is why this sells better, and it is a fact about buyers rather than about you: an unfamiliar supplier asking for an open-ended commitment needs far more trust than one asking for a bounded, named amount. Productising lowers the trust required to say yes.
Reference

The candidate is always something you have already delivered three or four times. Inventing a product you have never run is how people price a fixed fee for work whose shape they do not know.

The exclusions row deserves the emphasis. "Remediation is not included; a fixed-price remediation can be quoted separately" is one sentence that prevents the most common way these engagements lose money — the client reasonably assuming that finding the problem includes fixing it.

Almost all of it is one of two things: the scope was not really fixed, or the offer was not really repeatable.

⚖️ Expert Witness & Forensic Work — Impartiality, Standards & Report Discipline CAREER • Practice ▶
Concept
Your Duty Is to the Court, Not to Whoever Is Paying
This is the rule that defines the whole practice and the one that surprises people arriving from consultancy. An expert instructed by one side is nonetheless obliged to give an independent opinion, including the parts that damage the case of the party paying the invoice. An expert who advocates is not merely unethical — they are ineffective, because experienced lawyers on both sides can recognise it and a court can disregard the evidence entirely. The framing is not "how do I help my client win" but "what does the evidence support, stated plainly, including its limits". *Jurisdictions differ substantially in how this is formalised; this is orientation, not legal advice.*
Consultancy instinctExpert obligation
Advocate for the client's positionState what the evidence shows, either way
Present findings confidentlyQuantify uncertainty explicitly, including what you could not determine
Recommend a course of actionAnswer the question asked; recommendations are usually not yours to give
Keep working papers informalEverything may be disclosed, including drafts and notes
Fix the problem you noticedChange nothing. You are examining, not remediating
Bill for effortNever contingent on the outcome — that alone can disqualify the evidence
Reference
Evidence Discipline
The technical work is ordinary forensics; what makes it admissible is the record around it. Assume every step will be examined by someone whose job is to find the gap.
PracticeDetail
Work on a verified copyImage, hash, verify, and analyse the copy. The original is not touched again
Record the chain of custodyWho had it, when, where it was stored, every transfer — contemporaneously
Use methods you can explainA result you cannot describe to a non-technical judge is not usable
Prefer reproducible toolingAnother expert should be able to run the same steps and get the same answer
Record what you did not examineScope limits are part of the finding, not an embarrassment
Timestamp everything, in a stated timezoneTimezone confusion has undone more digital evidence than any technical failure
Keep notes as if they will be readThey may be. Write them accordingly from the first hour
The timezone row is not a small point. Logs in UTC (Coordinated Universal Time), a device in local time, a cloud service in a third, and daylight saving in the middle produce timelines that appear to show events in an impossible order — and an expert who has not stated their normalisation invites exactly the challenge that destroys a timeline.
Troubleshooting

The report is the deliverable, and its discipline is different from every other report in this domain: it is written to be attacked.

Concept
Knowing It and Teaching It Are Different Products
Expertise does not package itself. A training offer is a different thing from the knowledge behind it: it needs a defined audience, a stated outcome, a fixed duration, materials that survive without you in the room, and a price that reflects preparation you do once and deliver many times. The economics are genuinely attractive — the second delivery costs a fraction of the first — and that is exactly why the first one has to be built properly rather than improvised from slides you already had.

Price per group, not per head, wherever you can. Per-head pricing invites the client to reduce attendance to control cost, which produces a half-full room and a worse session for everyone — and your cost barely changes between eight people and sixteen.

The lab is where budgets go, and the discipline that saves you is that it must be resettable in minutes and runnable without your laptop. A workshop whose environment only works on the trainer's machine is a workshop that cannot be delegated, cannot be run twice in a week, and fails on the day the machine does.
